Pain désert

Half of Île-de-France's communes have no bakery. While Paris holds some of the world's highest boulangerie densities, 607 of the region's 1,300 communes are baguette-free zones.

May 2026 4 min read Data: INSEE BPE (IDF)

Île-de-France is, on paper, one of the great bread regions of the world. Paris's 20 arrondissements average 10 bakeries per 10,000 residents. The city's 8th arrondissement has more than 14 — one boulangerie for roughly every 700 people. In central Paris, fresh bread is never more than a short walk away.

But Île-de-France is not only Paris. The same administrative region stretches far into the Brie plateau and the Vexin, encompassing 1,300 communes ranging from tightly packed inner suburbs to quiet Seine-et-Marne villages of 200 souls. And in nearly half of those communes, the nearest baguette requires getting in a car.

The outer ring goes without

The bread deserts are not spread evenly. Paris itself — all 20 arrondissements — has a bakery in every one. So does Seine-Saint-Denis (93), the densely urban département just north of the périphérique. The deserts begin where the density drops.

Seine-et-Marne (77) — France's largest département by area, covering most of the eastern half of the IDF region — tops the list. Nearly three in five of its 514 communes have no bakery at all. Val-d'Oise and Yvelines are not far behind.

Population is no guarantee

A natural assumption is that towns big enough to generate demand will attract a baker. The data is less reassuring. Saint-Germain-lès-Arpajon, in Essonne, has 9,338 residents — it has three pharmacies — and zero bakeries. Nandy (Seine-et-Marne) counts 5,814 people and no boulangerie. Le Port-Marly (Yvelines, 4,803 residents) is the same story.

A commune of 9,000 people with no bakery is not a quirk. In Île-de-France, it is a pattern.

The medicine desert is even larger

Bread is one metric. Medicine is another. Of IDF's 1,300 communes, 690 — 53 percent — have no pharmacy. In 587 of those communes, there is neither a bakery nor a pharmacy. That is nearly half the entire region.

The contrast with Paris is sharp. The 8th arrondissement has 58 pharmacies for 41,000 residents — one per 712 people, among the highest densities in Europe. Meanwhile, 690 IDF communes have none.

This is the shape of the region behind the postcard. Île-de-France contains some of the most service-dense urban geography in the world, and some of the most quietly under-served rural territory in northern France. These are not isolated Alpine villages — they sit within an hour of Notre-Dame. They are simply the other side of a very large and very unequal region.
