The name game

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another is called Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. In between, a nation ran out of ideas.

2026-05-20 4 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union — 35,014 of them in the 2023 registry. Most are tiny: the median commune has a few hundred residents, and thousands have fewer than fifty. But every single one, no matter how small, carries an official name registered with the INSEE. That's a lot of names to invent.

Inevitably, people ran out. Of those 35,014 communes, 32,764 have a unique name — but 1,450 names are shared between two or more communes. That's ~5,400 communes that share their name with at least one neighbour, somewhere in France. If you look up Sainte-Colombe on a French map, you'll need to specify which one.

Sainte-Colombe, twelve times over

The most overworked commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. Twelve separate communes carry it — scattered across the Ain, Ardèche, Charente, Côte-d'Or, Gironde, and elsewhere. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu, ten times each. The top of the list reads like a medieval saints' calendar.

The repetition isn't accidental. Many of these names were assigned independently, centuries apart, by local clergy naming their parish after the same patron saint. Medieval France had a limited canon of favoured saints, and villages from Normandy to the Pyrenees drew from the same short list. Sainte-Colombe, a third-century martyr from the Rhône valley, was popular in Burgundy and Provence both. The result: twelve villages with the same name and no coordination between them.

A nation named for its saints

The saint-name phenomenon runs deeper than any individual name. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,898 — more than one in nine — begin with Saint or Sainte. Add the Latin-derived San, Sain, and partial derivations and the figure climbs further. The settlement network of France is, in a very literal sense, a map of the medieval church's geography.

After saints, the naming logic shifts to landscape. La, Le, Les — the French articles — apply to geographic features: valleys, hills, rivers, forests. Villers (105 communes) comes from the Latin villare, meaning a small agricultural holding. Villeneuve (70) means "new town." Pont (63) is a bridge. Fontaine (58) is a spring. The names are a geological and agricultural inventory of medieval land use.

Y and the forty-five-character behemoth

Then there are the extremes. At the short end, Y — a commune in the Somme — holds the record for France's shortest place name: a single letter. It's pronounced like the French letter i-grec, and it appears in INSEE's commune registry exactly as written, no accent, no article, no explanation. Population: around 100 people. Length: 1 character.

At the long end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne. Forty-five characters. It was formed by a merger of three separate villages in 1972, each of which contributed its name to the new combined entity. The result is a hyphenated chain that takes up most of a line on any printed form — and yet is perfectly grammatical in the French tradition of compound commune names.

The shape of French names

Most commune names fall between six and twenty characters — long enough for a recognizable place name, short enough to fit a sign. The distribution peaks around 7–8 characters and then thins out steadily toward the long tail of merger-compounds. The average commune name is 11.8 characters. Y is an outlier in one direction; Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont in the other.

The long tail tells a political story. France went through waves of commune mergers — in the 1970s under the Marcellin law, and again in the 2010s–2020s — but unlike other European countries, France rarely abolished the old village names. Instead, it hyphenated them. Every compound name above 30 characters is the footprint of a merger, each village insisting on keeping its identity in the new combined name. The French commune system is famously resistant to simplification, and the name distribution reflects it.

A note on the data. Commune names and counts come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 communes registered in the French INSEE system as of 2023. Names are the official communeName field as recorded in the APL commune-level file, which draws from the INSEE commune reference table (COG). The 1-letter commune Y (INSEE code 80829, Somme) and the 45-character Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (INSEE code 51513, Marne) are confirmed against the INSEE commune table.
