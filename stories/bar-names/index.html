What France Calls Its Bars

29,238 names for 49,385 bars — from the ubiquitous Café des Sports to the 17,831 bars with a name that belongs to them alone.

2026-05-11 5 min read

When INSEE's SIRENE business directory records a bar, it asks for two things: an address and, optionally, a dénomination. The name is not required. Roughly 41% of France's 49,385 registered bars don't bother — they operate under their owner's legal name or a corporate entity code, invisible to any tourist who tries to find them on a list.

The other 59% — 29,238 bars — filed a name. Those 29,238 names are a cultural document. They span a century of French social life: the institutions bars were built next to, the battles and racehorses that captured the national imagination, the Parisian argot that seeped into the provinces, and the vast majority that exist nowhere but the chalkboard above one specific zinc counter.

The Café des Sports (× 133)

France's single most common bar name is the Café des Sports. It appears 133 times, spread across 56 of France's 96 metropolitan departments. Add Bar des Sports (83), Le Café des Sports (21), Le Sporting (18), and Le Bar des Sports (14) and the sports-themed cluster reaches 448 bars — the largest single theme in the national registry.

The French café des sports is not a sports bar in the American sense. There is rarely a large screen, rarely a dedicated menu of chicken wings, rarely a cover charge for a big game. It is simply the neighbourhood bar where the subject of last night's match is acceptable as an opening line with a stranger. The name is so common it has become almost content-free: a signal of normal, un-themed café life.

The bar next to everything

Read down the top 20 and a pattern appears: Café de la Mairie (89 bars), Café du Centre (89), Café de la Place (87), Café de la Poste (66), Café du Commerce (66), Café de la Gare (65). These are not coincidences. They are a map of how French bars located themselves in the social fabric of the commune.

Across all variants, 1,344 registered bars take their name from a civic institution or public landmark — the town hall, the square, the station, the market, the post office, the church. The bar was the institution adjacent to the institution: a secondary waiting room, a secondary meeting point, a secondary form of public space. The naming reflects that dependency exactly.

The Café du Commerce name has its own history: in 19th-century France, le commerce meant the local merchant trade — the café du commerce was where small shopkeepers and artisans congregated to discuss prices, complain about regulations, and settle small debts over a glass of wine. The phrase café du commerce eventually became French slang for uninformed political opinion confidently expressed — the armchair expertise of the bar counter. That the name survives on 66 registered premises is somehow satisfying.

The mystery classiques

Below the sports bars and the civic names sits a cluster of names that feel quintessentially French without obvious explanation. They are not themed (not civic, not sports, not nature). They are not recent. They occur across the whole country, in ports and ski towns and industrial suburbs, without any obvious common origin. They spread by imitation: a bar with one of these names felt like a real bar, so a new bar owner copied it.

Le Longchamp is the leader at 96 — the most common single bar name after Café des Sports. The Hippodrome de Longchamp, opened in 1857 in the Bois de Boulogne, made the name synonymous with a certain Parisian glamour. Bars near racetracks adopted it first; then bars everywhere adopted it for the aura. Le Brazza and Le Fontenoy are more specifically French colonial and military history, names that would have meant something concrete to a bar-goer in 1900 and mean almost nothing today, preserved in paint and neon on a hundred different façades.

Le Balto is the strangest. It is Parisian argot — aller au balto means to go to the bar, roughly equivalent to going down the pub in English. The etymology is disputed: it may derive from the city of Baltimore (via an American-themed café that became generically the word for bar), or from a brand of rolling tobacco sold at tabacs. Either way, 89 bars across 38 departments have adopted it as their name — including 5 in Paris 75 itself, where it presumably means something more specific than it does in Vendée.

The 61% that exist nowhere else

The names above — repeated across dozens or hundreds of bars — are the visible fraction. The majority of French bar names are unique.

20,431 distinct names filed
87% of distinct names appear once
17,831 bars with a name found nowhere else in France

Out of 20,431 distinct names, 17,831 — 87.3% — appear exactly once in the entire national registry. That means 61% of all named bars in France carry a name that exists in no other registered bar in the country. These are the truly local: Bar Chez Mimile, Le P'tit Bouchon, Au Rendez-vous des Chasseurs, La Tchatche, Le Zinc de la Butte. Each one is the bar of one neighbourhood, one village, one quartier.

The pattern holds whether the name is poetic or mundane. There is exactly one Le Bout du Monde (The End of the World) in the SIRENE registry — and it is in the Creuse, which is appropriate. There is exactly one L'Improbable, one Le Troquet du Bout de la Rue, one Bar à Papa, one La Vie en Rosé. And there is exactly one %.

The Café des Sports is the national archetype — the name so generic it signals normalcy. But the median French bar is named something that will never be seen again outside its commune. France has the cliché and the singular, in roughly equal measure, 49,385 times over.

A note on the data. Bar names are drawn from the dénominationUniteLégale and enseigne fields in INSEE's SIRENE national business registry, March 2026 snapshot. The dataset covers active establishments with NAF code 56.30Z (débits de boissons — bars, pubs, brasseries serving alcohol for on-site consumption). Of 49,385 active geocoded bars, 29,238 carry at least one name field. Names are compared in uppercase with leading/trailing whitespace removed; no other normalisation is applied (accents preserved, articles retained). Category counts (sports, civic, etc.) are keyword matches and may include some false positives. The same bars.json source file is used in Story 4 (Where France drinks).
