What's in a name?

35,014 communes, one letter, twelve Sainte-Colombes — and one village that needs 45 characters just to introduce itself.

2026-05-17 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, there is a commune called Y. That's it. One letter, a dot on the map, 89 residents — and the shortest official commune name in France. It's not a typo in the data. It really is just Y.

France has 35,014 communes, and their names span exactly one letter to forty-five. Most sit quietly in the 7-to-10 character range: Bordeaux, Libourne, Pauillac. But at both ends of the spectrum, the names become a historical document — of saints, of mergers, of feudal geography, of the particular stubbornness of small French villages about what to call themselves.

Y, and thirteen two-letter communes

Y (département 80, Somme) is unique: the only commune in France with a single-character name. Its origin is probably Norse — the same root as eau, meaning water. The commune sits near a confluence of marshes that once defined the region. It has been called Y since at least the 13th century.

Below Y, there are 13 two-letter communes. They read like a code: By, Eu, Gy, Oz, Py, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz — and a few with accents: Bû, Oô. Most are in rural départements, many with Celtic or Norse roots predating French standardisation.

Saint-everything

One in nine French communes — 3,898 of them, 11.1% of the total — carries the name of a saint. This is not a quirk; it's the structure of Catholic rural France, where parishes centred on a patron saint gave towns their names. Saint-Pierre, Saint-Martin, Saint-Jean, Sainte-Marie: the most common Catholic given names became the most repeated place names.

The most common of all is Sainte-Colombe: twelve communes, twelve different départements, one shared name for twelve distinct villages that have never met each other. They stretch from the Hautes-Alpes (population 59) to Seine-et-Marne (population 1,793). The only thing they have in common is a 3rd-century martyr and a certain stubbornness about renaming.

The 45-character village

At the other extreme: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. A village of 491 people in the Marne (département 51). The hyphenated monster reflects a 1972 commune merger — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont absorbed Saint-Genest-et-Isson and simply concatenated the names. The result is the longest official commune name in France, longer than most Twitter bios, and a challenge for any envelope.

The runner-up — Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 chars, Haute-Saône) — is also a merger. In fact, nearly every commune with a name over 35 characters is a fusion of two formerly independent villages. The hyphens are a history of administrative marriages, some happy, some not.

The shape of French place names

Most commune names are modest: the median is around 10 characters, and the distribution peaks sharply at 7–8. The long tail stretches out through the hyphenated merger towns, thinning to a handful of extremes past 35 characters.

The 10.6% of communes that share a name with at least one other commune are mostly in the Saint-* cluster, but also in the article communes — Le Pin, La Roche, Les Bois — where the definite article absorbs what might otherwise have been a distinctive name. France has 2,140 communes starting with Le, La, or Les. They are, collectively, the most anonymous communes in the country.

A name is a decision made centuries ago and then simply not reversed. Y decided not to change, and so it is still Y. Sainte-Colombe was named twelve times independently. Saint-Remy absorbed a neighbour and kept both names. Each commune carries its etymology in its address — and 35,014 of those etymologies are still registered, still mapped, still official.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories (INSEE vintage 2021 for population, DREES 2023 for APL scores). Name analysis was done directly on this dataset. Commune mergers and name etymologies are from public administrative records; the 1972 merger date for Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is well-documented in French administrative history.
