What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other EU country. Their names run from a single letter to 45 characters, and 11% of them start with "Saint."

2026-05-29 4 min read

France is famously over-communed. The country has 35,014 communes — around 40% of all municipalities in the EU — most of them small villages unchanged since Napoleon reorganized the territory in 1789. Every one of them has a name. Those names average 11.8 characters. But that average hides extraordinary range: from a single letter to a hyphenated archipelago of villages that merged on paper but couldn't agree what to call the result.

The shortest: Y

The commune of Y sits in the Somme département, 140 km north of Paris. Population: 89. It is, as far as records go, the shortest place name in France — and one of the shortest in the world. No one is entirely sure how it got its name, though the most plausible theory is that it derives from a local Picard dialect word. It has a church, a mairie, a bus stop, and the kind of quiet dignity that only a place this small can sustain.

The shortest Y — Somme (80), population 89

Y isn't alone at the short end. There are 13 two-letter communes in France: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Most are small villages in the hundreds or fewer — Eu, with 6,591 residents and a medieval castle, is the outlier.

The shape of French commune names

Most commune names cluster between 6 and 12 characters. The distribution has a gentle peak at 7–8 characters — think Ambléon, Ambronay, Beaulieu — before thinning out past 20 as village mergers pile hyphen onto hyphen.

The saints: 11% of France

If you pick a French commune at random, there's a 1-in-9 chance its name starts with Saint or Sainte. Exactly 3,898 of France's 35,014 communes — 11.1% — carry a saint's name. The tradition runs deep into medieval Catholicism: parishes were founded under a patron saint, and the commune inherited the name when the Revolutionary republic replaced the parish as the basic unit of civic life.

The density isn't uniform. In Ardèche (département 07), nearly three communes in ten start with Saint — 97 out of 335. Creuse (28.2%), Loire (27.8%), and Haute-Vienne (27.2%) are close behind. These are historically rural, deeply Catholic departments in the Massif Central corridor. Meanwhile, Corsica, Alsace, and the Nord come in well below the national average.

Most common exact names

Sainte-Colombe: 12 times

The single most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. Twelve different communes carry it, scattered from Gironde to Seine-Maritime. Sainte Colombe of Sens was a third-century Christian martyr; her cult spread widely during the early medieval period, leaving a dozen villages named in her honor. The second most common, Saint-Sauveur, appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear ten times.

The other end: 45 characters

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, département Marne, population 491. It came into being in 1972, when three separate villages merged under the Fifth Republic's loi Marcellin, which offered financial incentives for communes to consolidate. The new commune needed to keep all three village names visible to satisfy local politics, so it strung them together with hyphens.

This is a pattern in the longest names: the top ten longest are nearly all merger artifacts. The loi Marcellin created around 800 fusions, and the clumsiest naming decisions live on in databases as 35-, 40-, 43-character strings.

Between Y and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, France has named everything. The 35,014 names are a compressed history of the country's religion, its geography, its administrative anxieties, and its occasional willingness to let a village just be called Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL dataset (2023 vintage, 35,014 communes including overseas territories), joined to administrative boundary data from data.gouv.fr. Name-length and frequency analysis performed on all commune names as listed in the dataset. The same 35,014-commune dataset powers the medical deserts story.
