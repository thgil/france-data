35,014 ways to name a village

France has a commune called Y. And one called Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Between those extremes: a nation of saints, rivers, and accidental doubles.

2026-05-26 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme département — department 80, in northern France — whose full official name is a single letter: Y. Population 89. Founded before the Normans arrived. The name pre-dates French itself, probably derived from a Latin or Germanic root for water or settlement. And it is, by the government's own records, the shortest commune name in France.

It is not alone. France has 14 communes with names of just one or two characters. Each one is a small puzzle — compressed history, an ancient tongue worn down to almost nothing by the centuries.

From one character to forty-five

Most commune names are reassuringly sensible — between seven and fifteen characters, averaging 11.8. But the distribution has a very long right tail. At the far end sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in Marne: 45 characters, the result of two villages merging in the 1970s, neither willing to disappear entirely. The hyphen connecting them is, in a small way, a treaty.

The saint empire

The single most striking pattern in French commune names is the saints. 3,882 communes — 11.1% of France — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. This is not a medieval quirk that has since faded; many of these names date to the early Christian settlement of Gaul and survived every revolution, annexation, and administrative reform. The Dordogne alone has 130 saint-named communes, more than a quarter of its total.

The twenty most common exact names in France are almost all saints. Sainte-Colombe tops the list — twelve communes share that name, scattered from the Hautes-Alpes to the Yonne. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Post a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France" without a département code and the postal service has twelve guesses.

3,700 communes without a unique name

In total, 1,450 commune names are shared by more than one commune. That's 3,700 places — over one in ten of all French communes — that share their name with at least one neighbour somewhere in the country. The collision isn't just a post-office problem. In the age of Google Maps, a search for "Sainte-Marie, France" returns a different town depending on whether the algorithm favours population or GPS proximity.

The duplicates are distributed across France, a side-effect of independent naming by thousands of local communities over centuries. There was no central registry until relatively recently. By the time anyone tried to standardise the map, the names had already set like concrete.

Names as compressed history

Read the names long enough and patterns emerge. The -sur- suffix (1,991 communes) records the rivers: the Loire, the Seine, the Marne, the Rhône. The -en- and -de- connectors encode terrain — Montigny-en-Arrouaise, Saint-Gilles-de-Crétot. The saints preserve the missionaries. The hyphens preserve the mergers. And at the very bottom of the alphabet, in the Somme, a single letter holds a name so old it has worn itself down to almost nothing.

A note on the data. Commune names and properties (INSEE code, département, population) come from the DREES APL 2023 commune file as distributed on data.gouv.fr — 35,014 communes covering metropolitan France and overseas territories. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 base. Name lengths, duplicate counts, and saint-prefix tallies were computed from that file; no external name database was used.
