The Name Game

One letter. Forty-five letters. And 3,882 saints in between — what France's 35,000 commune names reveal.

2026-05-11 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Germany — a country with 20 million more people — has fewer than 11,000. Spain has 8,000. Italy has 7,900. France's municipal patchwork is almost comically dense: a legacy of the Revolution, which turned every parish into a self-governing unit and never fully reversed course. Each one has a name. The names, taken together, tell you something about the country.

The saints take over

The most obvious pattern: saints. of French communes begin with Saint- or Sainte- — 3,882 communes in all. That's the entire population of a mid-sized city, converted into place names. The medieval Church named its villages after the local patron saint, and those names survived every revolution, every republic, every redrawing of the map.

There are 3,152 distinct Saint- names — but several saints are so popular they appear in a dozen différents départements simultaneously. Sainte-Colombe holds the record: twelve communes, from Ardennes to Yonne. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin, ten.

The hyphen habit

38.3% of French communes have a hyphenated name — 13,410 of them. France assembles its villages with hyphens the way other countries use spaces. The pattern intensifies with the 1972 loi de fusion, which allowed struggling communes to merge without giving either partner's name up entirely: the result was a new compound name, joined with a hyphen, often absurdly long.

The record holder is in the Marne: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. Four words. Two saints. A name that defeats autocomplete and challenges map labels. Population: 491. It was formed in 1972 when the commune of Saint-Genest-et-Isson was absorbed by its neighbour. Neither side wanted to lose their name. France, characteristically, let them have both.

A town called Y

At the other extreme: Y. One letter. The commune of Y sits in the Somme, département 80, population 89. It is the shortest place name in France and one of the shortest in the world. The name almost certainly derives from a Picard word for water — ie or ea, cognate with the English ea in many English river names — reduced over centuries to a single syllable and then a single letter. The shape of French names

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long — roughly the length of Saint-Denis. Most names cluster between 4 and 15 characters, but the distribution has a long right tail: a few hundred communes with names over 25 characters, and 50 above 31. The histogram shows this clearly.

The long tail is a distinctly French phenomenon. Elsewhere, when villages merge, they pick a new name or drop one. In France, they hyphenate. The geography of the hyphen is itself a map: heaviest in Normandie, Bourgogne-Franche-Comté, and the Grand-Est — regions that experienced the most municipal fusions in the 1970s. The names got longer. The villages got smaller.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base. The dataset used here is the same communes-apl.geojson file that powers the medical deserts story.
