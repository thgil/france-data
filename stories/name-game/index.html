The name game

France has 35,014 communes — and their names are a thousand-year-old puzzle. One is a single letter. Twelve share the same name. One needs 45 characters just to say hello.

2026-05-13 4 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union — 35,014 of them, ranging from a handful of houses in the high Alps to cities of a million. Each carries an official name, most of them frozen in administrative amber since the Revolution. Those names, taken together, are a linguistic archive of medieval France: its saints, its geography, its dialects.

France's shortest commune: Y

Y Somme · 89 residents · dept 80

Just the letter Y. One character, one commune, in the Somme — the département of the Somme valley, red-brick towns, and World War I memorials. Y has 89 residents and an APL score of 2.78, comfortably above the medical desert threshold of 2.5. Geographically unremarkable; linguistically peerless. Its name comes from a medieval Picard word for "water" — the commune sits beside a pond. The vowel stayed. Everything else was trimmed away by centuries of use.

Y is not alone in its brevity. Thirteen communes manage their existence in two letters:

Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne with its rare double-o accent) — each a complete toponym compacted to the minimum. The average commune name runs to 11.8 characters. These are outliers by a factor of six.

The saint empire

The single most common first word in a French commune name is Saint. Not La, not Le, not any topographic prefix — Saint, appearing 3,882 times. That's 11.1% of all communes, more than one in nine. Medieval Christianity didn't just shape the country's calendar; it named its map.

Dordogne leads the ranking with 128 Saint- communes — one for roughly every 32 square kilometres of the département. Gironde (121), Isère (105), and Saône-et-Loire (105) follow. These are ancient heartlands of rural France, where the saint-name network was laid down by the Church during the Carolingian era and never seriously revised. The map of Saint- communes looks a lot like the map of pre-industrial settlement density.

One name, twelve places

The most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. Twelve separate communes share it — in Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. Saint Colombe was a Gaulish martyr, possibly legend, possibly historical, revered in the early Church. Her dozen namesakes are spread from Normandy to the Pyrenees.

Saint-Sauveur (11 communes) and Saint-Aubin (10) follow closely. Beaulieu — "beautiful place," a secular name — ties Saint-Aubin at 10. Among the secular names, Le Pin (9) and Beaumont (8) and Verrières (8) punch above their weight. The pattern isn't random: recurring names mark places where the same physical type — a spring, a high ridge, a pine forest, a beautiful meadow — appeared repeatedly across France and got the same label each time.

The commune that needed 45 characters

At the other end of the spectrum:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

45 characters. Marne, département 51, population 491. The name records a 2016 municipal merger: the commune of Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont absorbed Saint-Genest and Isson, and French administrative law required all three names to be preserved in the official title. The result is a postal address that doubles as a précis of local political history. It does not fit on a standard road sign without a font size that renders the text illegible. This is a known French administrative problem, without a known French administrative solution.

The top-ten longest names average 40 characters. Almost all of them are merger products — two or three medieval villages hyphenated into a single legal entity at some point between the 1970s and 2020s. France's communes are constitutionally almost impossible to abolish — the commune predates the Republic and the Republic enshrined it. What you can do is merge them, which creates names like Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Or let centuries of ordinary speech erode them to Y. Either way, the name is the last thing to go.

A note on the data. Name counts and statistics are derived from INSEE's 2023 APL dataset for 35,014 French communes, which includes the official commune name (communeName) as recorded in the INSEE reference file. Counts reflect the commune structure at the 2023 vintage — some recently merged communes may appear under their merged name. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base used in the APL calculations. The APL score for Y (2.78) is from DREES 2023.
