France has twelve Sainte-Colombes

3,700 French communes share their name with at least one other. The winner? Sainte-Colombe — a 3rd-century martyr with a dozen villages to her name.

2026-05-15 4 min read

French addresses always include a département code. "75" is Paris. "29" is Finistère. "46" is Lot. This is not bureaucratic excess — it is a hard necessity. France has 35,014 communes, and 3,700 of them share their name with at least one other.

Send a letter to Saint-Marcel, France without specifying a département and it could end up in any of nine different villages. The post office would have to guess.

The twenty most-duplicated names

Of the 25 names that appear most often, the overwhelming majority invoke a saint. Medieval communities named themselves after the patron saint whose relics or chapel they sheltered. The same saints were venerated all across Gaul — and so the same names were chosen, independently, by communities hundreds of kilometres apart.

The champion: twelve Sainte-Colombes

Sainte Colombe (also spelled Colomba) was a young Gallic martyr venerated from at least the 3rd century, her cult centred in Sens. Her name spread far. Today it belongs to twelve communes, scattered from the Manche coast to the Seine-et-Marne plain, from the Hautes-Alpes to the Landes. Hover the dots to see each one.

Why so many identical names?

France's communes were largely founded — or formally named — between the 9th and 15th centuries, when the Catholic Church was the primary source of civic identity. A village built near a chapel of Saint Aubin would call itself Saint-Aubin. A village with a relic of Sainte Marie would call itself Sainte-Marie. There was no central registry, no naming authority, no mechanism to prevent two villages in different provinces from arriving at the same name.

The Revolution of 1789 formalised the commune as the basic unit of local government — and encoded whatever names already existed. The 3rd Republic later added the département number as a suffix to postal addresses, quietly solving the ambiguity problem that had been building for centuries. The INSEE commune code, used today on every administrative document, embeds the département in the first two digits.

The duplicate names remain, not because France couldn't standardise them, but because it chose not to. A commune's name is part of its identity. Asking the twelve Sainte-Colombes to rename themselves would be, to each of them, a small amputation.

Of the 1,450 names that appear more than once, the most common pattern repeats: saintly prefix, holy figure, no further distinction. Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin (10), Saint-Marcel (9), Saint-Michel (9), Saint-Pierre (9). The secular duplicates — Beaulieu (10), Le Pin (9), Beaumont (8) — follow a similar logic: common landscape features, common aspirations, no central arbiter.

France has 2,007 communes with sur in their name — X-sur-Y, where Y is usually a river. Those names describe location, not devotion. But the principle is the same: each village found its own way to a name, and the result is a country where the address is never quite complete without the number.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (communes-apl.geojson, 35,014 communes). APL scores measure estimated GP consultations accessible per standardised resident per year; scores below 2.5 indicate an official medical desert. Commune centroids are computed from polygon geometries. The Sainte-Colombe in département 05 (Hautes-Alpes, pop. 59) has APL = 0.00 — a severe medical desert.
