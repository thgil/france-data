The commune called Y

France has 35,014 communes. 3,700 of them share a name with at least one other. The shortest official name is a single letter.

2026-05-31 4 min read

In the département of Somme, 20 kilometres south-east of Amiens, there is a commune of 89 people. Its postal code is 80829. Its INSEE code is 80829. And its official name — the one on the road signs, the one on the birth certificates, the one in every administrative database in the French Republic — is Y.

One letter. Not an abbreviation, not a label error. Y is the shortest commune name in France, and probably in Europe. It has existed under that name since at least the 14th century, when French spelling hadn't yet settled down enough to insist on vowels.

The two-letter club

Y is not alone. France has 13 more communes with names of exactly two letters. Together, the 14 shortest-named communes in France span ten departments, from the Ardennes to the Pyrénées-Orientales.

These names are mostly Norse or pre-Latin in origin — ancient words compressed by centuries of French phonology until almost nothing remained. Eu (pop. 6,591, Seine-Maritime) is one of the oldest names in Normandy, recorded as early as the 10th century. Oz (pop. 213, Isère) sits in the Alps. Sy (pop. 54, Ardennes) is the smallest in the club by population. Uz (pop. 36, Hautes-Pyrénées) is the smallest of all.

When France runs out of names

Brevity is only one kind of commune-name curiosity. The more widespread one is duplication. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,700 — roughly one in ten — share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country.

Most of these duplicates come in pairs: 1,048 names are used by exactly two communes. A further 216 names are shared by three. The numbers thin out quickly — but they don't reach zero until you get to the name used twelve times.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most replicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. Not Saint-Martin (nine), not Saint-Michel (nine), not Saint-Pierre (nine) — Sainte-Colombe, twelve times over, across twelve different departments.

The twelve Sainte-Colombes are not clustered. They don't form a cult, a franchise, or a regional tradition. They are scattered from the Hautes-Alpes (dept. 05, pop. 59) to the Manche (dept. 50, pop. 184) to Seine-et-Marne in the Île-de-France suburbs (dept. 77, pop. 1,793). Three are in the Atlantic south-west. One is in the Doubs. One is in Haute-Normandie. The only thing they have in common is Sainte Columba — a 6th-century Frankish martyr whose cult spread from monastery to monastery across medieval France, leaving a dozen villages named in her honour and now completely unaware of each other.

The full ranking

The chart below shows the 20 most common commune names in France. The Saint-/Sainte- prefix dominates — of the top 20, fourteen begin with a saint's name. The remaining six (Beaulieu, Le Pin, Beaumont, Verrières, Brion) are geographical descriptions that were simply too apt to be used only once.

France's administrative map is, in this sense, a palimpsest. The commune names preserve 1,500 years of Christianity, a handful of Norse placeholders, a few inarticulate syllables from a language spoken before Latin arrived, and twelve separate acts of medieval piety toward a now-forgotten saint. The republic standardised everything else. The names it left alone.
