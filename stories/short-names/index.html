Y

One French commune has a single-letter name. Thirteen more have two. The rest average 11.8 characters — and the longest runs to 45.

2026-05-19 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Each one has an official name, registered in the INSEE commune code index and stamped on every administrative document, road sign, and postmark in France. Most names run eight to twelve characters. A handful run to thirty or forty. And then there is Y.

Y is a commune in the Somme, département 80, with a population of 89. Its name is not an abbreviation. It is not a typographic error. It has been called Y — a single capital letter — since at least the twelfth century, most likely derived from a Picard or Latin word for water or marshland. The French postal service has a special exception for its address. French postal workers reportedly find it easier to sort by departmental code than to handle the one-character commune with no context before or after it.

The 14 shortest names in France

Beyond Y, there are thirteen communes with two-letter names: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, and Uz. Together, these fourteen communes — one 1-letter, thirteen 2-letter — account for the extreme left tail of a distribution that otherwise clusters firmly around seven to ten characters.

The map reveals no strong geographic clustering. The two-letter communes are scattered from the Pyrenees to Normandy, from the Ardennes to the Isère. But there are two loose regional signals: the northwest cluster (Eu and Ry, both in Seine-Maritime; Ri in the Orne; Bû in Eure-et-Loir) carries the fingerprint of Old Norse — the Viking settlers of Normandy left behind place-name endings like -y and -ry meaning "estate of" or "river." And in the deep Pyrenees, Py, Ur, and Uz (alongside Oô) are from pre-Latin Gascon or Ibero-Basque roots that predate any Romance language.

How long is a French commune name?

The length distribution below shows all 35,014 communes by the number of characters in their official name. The shape is immediately clear: the distribution peaks at seven to eight characters, with a long right tail that stretches to 45.

The median name is 10 characters. The mean is 11.8 — pulled right by the long tail of hyphenated composite names. Three sources explain why French commune names skew long compared to most European countries.

First, saint names. Exactly 3,898 communes — 11.1% of France — begin with Saint or Sainte. A further 539 carry saint names deeper in a compound like Castries-et-Saint-Martin. "Saint" alone adds five characters before you even reach the patron's name, and most saint compounds are hyphenated triples: Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite.

Second, directional and topographic connectors: -sur-, -en-, -sous-, -les-, -de-. Every hyphen is a character, and French loves to locate a commune relative to its river, neighbour, or valley. Neuville-sur-Escaut. Sainte-Marie-aux-Chênes.

Third, commune mergers. France does not rename merged communes — it hyphenates them. When three villages merge, you get the third name in our story.

The 45-character champion

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is the longest commune name in France at 45 characters. It is in the Marne (département 51), with a population of 491. The name preserves three separate villages — Saint-Rémy, Bouzemont, and Saint-Genest-et-Isson — that merged in the nineteenth century and kept all three names, hyphenated into one administrative entity. No village wanted to give up its identity. The French state obliged.

France's 10 longest commune names

The gap between Y (1 character, 89 people, one marshland word older than France itself) and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters, 491 people, three village histories fused by a hyphen) is the full span of French naming culture. One end is a sound from a forgotten pre-Latin language that outlasted empires because the Somme valley kept using it. The other is an administrative compromise so French in character it almost becomes a philosophical statement: all three villages were right; the merger was necessary; and therefore the name must be all three names, joined but separate, forever.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which aggregates commune-level characteristics from INSEE sources. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name lengths are counted from the full official string as stored in the APL dataset (UTF-8 character count, including hyphens, spaces, and accented characters). The commune Y is confirmed in the INSEE commune code index as code 80829, département Somme.
