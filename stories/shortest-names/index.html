Y, and Friends

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y — a single letter, population 89. Here's the full spectrum, from one letter to a 45-character administrative marvel.

2026-05-12 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y.

Just Y. One letter. Population: 89. It sits in the Somme, about 15 kilometres east of Amiens, and it has been confounding postal services, bureaucrats, and directory enquiries since the 13th century. The name comes from a Picard word for water — there's a small lake nearby — but that etymology is cold comfort when you're on hold trying to explain your address. "Y — no, just Y — yes, like the letter."

Y is the only commune in France with a single-letter name. Its most famous export is the joke that it holds the world record for the shortest place-name postmark.

The two-letter club

Y is alone at 1, but it has 13 companions at 2 letters. Together they make a small, scattered club — tiny villages in mountain valleys and Norman farmland, each with a name that looks like a typo and an address that breaks online forms. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, population 36) is the smallest. Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591) is the largest and most famous — a port town with a real château and a legitimate Wikipedia article.

The typical commune name

Most French commune names land between 7 and 10 characters — words like Colomars, Évrecy, Maubeuge. The median is exactly 10 characters; the mean is 11.8. The distribution is right-skewed: short names are rare curiosities, long names are products of administrative history.

The longest names: a merger story

At the right end of the distribution sits a 45-character commune: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne, population 491. It is not a place with a colourful history — it is the product of paperwork. France has been running a commune merger programme since the 1970s, offering incentives for tiny villages to consolidate into a single administrative unit. The rule is simple and, in retrospect, diabolical: the new commune gets a name that concatenates all the old names, joined by hyphens.

Three villages became one. Three already-long names became one 45-character name. France's second-longest — Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur, 43 characters, Haute-Saône — is the same story. So are most of the top ten.

A republic of saints

3,898 communes — exactly 11.1% of France — begin with Saint or Sainte. France has more saint-named communes than any other country in Europe, a legacy of early medieval Christianisation and the administrative structure of the parish system, which predates the revolutionary commune by centuries.

The most common patron is Saint Martin (202 communes whose names begin with Saint-Martin), followed by Jean (155) and Pierre (144). But "most common" is deceptive: each of those 202 Saint-Martins has a different suffix that makes it legally distinct — Saint-Martin-d'Uriage, Saint-Martin-de-Belleville, Saint-Martin-en-Bresse. In practice, the most-repeated exact name is Sainte-Colombe (12 identical communes in 12 different départements), followed by Saint-Sauveur (11) and Saint-Aubin (10).

Names are compressed history. Y is a medieval water-word that survived because no one ever bothered to change it. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is a 21st-century budget spreadsheet dressed up as a place. Between them sit 35,012 others, each one a small puzzle of etymology, geography, and administrative survival.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level file, which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. The administrative boundary data is the same source used in the medical deserts story. Name lengths are measured in Unicode characters (so Bû is 2 characters, not 3 bytes).
