Y is a Commune

One letter. 89 residents. And 13 neighbours whose names run to exactly two characters. A tour of France's shortest — and longest — commune names.

2026-05-08 4 min read
Y

Somme · Département 80 · Population 89

In France's official commune register, one entry is a single letter. The commune is called Y. It sits in the Somme, a département in the flat agricultural north, about 30 km southeast of Amiens. Its population is 89. Its INSEE code is 80829. Its name, in every official database, is: Y.

Y is not a typo. It's not a scan artefact. It's an ancient settlement whose name was already shortened to this vowel by the Middle Ages — derived, most likely, from a Gaulish root meaning "water" or "yew tree", ground down by centuries of spoken contraction until the written form followed. France has preserved it exactly as spoken.

The two-letter club

Below Y come 13 communes with two-letter names. They scatter across France — from the Ardennes to the Pyrénées-Orientales, from Normandy to Val-d'Oise — and collectively host just over 13,000 residents. The largest by far is Eu (Seine-Maritime, 6,591 people), a market town that once hosted the royal Château d'Eu. The smallest is Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, 36 residents), a hamlet in the foothills. Two communes in Pyrénées-Orientales — Py and Ur — share a département but not their fortunes: Py has an APL medical-access score of 0.16 (a severe desert), while Ur scores 5.80 (well-served).

The other end

If Y represents one extreme, the other belongs to a commune in the Marne:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

That's 45 characters — the longest commune name in France. It has a population of 491. The name is the archaeological record of a 19th-century fusion: two villages, Bouzemont and Isson, joined administratively while both kept Saint-Remy (and Saint-Genest) as honorific prefixes. Résultat: a name longer than most Twitter handles.

There are 79 communes with names over 30 characters, clustered mostly in the east (Haute-Saône has three in the top ten alone) and in Normandy. Almost all of them are fusion products — when France merged villages into communes nouvelles, the default was to concatenate names with hyphens. Brevity was never the priority.

The shape of French commune names

Number of communes at each name length (characters), 35,014 communes total.

The distribution has a clear peak at 7–8 characters — the mode is 7. Names like Montoir, Bellême, Créances, or Château sit comfortably here. But the curve has a long right tail: instead of falling steeply after the peak, it levels out into a broad plateau between 15 and 22 characters before finally thinning. That plateau is the fusion effect — centuries of administrative mergers producing names like Saint-Hilaire-de-Riez or Villers-sous-Saint-Leu.

The median commune name is 10 characters. The mean — pulled right by the long tail — is 11.8. And 3,898 communes (11.1%) start with Saint or Sainte: more than one in nine of France's 35,014 communes is named after a saint. The shortest saints are still significantly longer than Y.

Y has been a commune since the Revolution sorted France's 44,000 villages into administrative units. It has survived every subsequent reorganisation — the Napoleonic prefectoral system, the 1884 municipal law, the 2015 communes nouvelles wave — with its name intact. Other communes merged, renamed, or fused into longer hyphenated forms. Y remained Y. There is something stubborn and irreducible about it, which seems appropriate for a place that has been here since the Gauls.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the communes-apl.geojson file built for the medical deserts story, which joins DREES APL 2023 scores to INSEE commune boundaries. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name lengths are measured in Unicode characters (not bytes). Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL file. APL scores mentioned for Py and Ur are from the same source.
