The Republic of Saints

4,410 French communes — one in eight — are named after a saint. Creuse is nearly one in three. Paris is zero.

2026-05-07 5 min read

France officially separated church and state in 1905. The law is called laïcité. But look at the map of commune names and you'll find 4,410 places — 12.6% of all 35,014 French communes — that still carry a saint's name. In the Creuse, the rate is nearly one in three. In Alsace, it barely registers. In Paris, it's zero.

This is hagiography written in cartography: a snapshot of medieval Christianity, the Reformation's uneven reach, and the administrative inertia of a country that has renamed everything except itself.

One king: Saint-Martin

Of the 4,410 saint communes, 249 are called Saint-Martin — easily the most common. That's one Saint-Martin for roughly every 5,600 km² of France, or, to put it another way, more than Luxembourg has municipalities in total. Saint-Jean (168) and Saint-Pierre (163) come second and third. Together the top three saints account for 580 communes — more than the entire department of Gers has towns.

Across all saint communes, there are 1,095 distinct saint names in use. Many are obscure enough to require a hagiography dictionary: Saint-Gengoux, Saint-Hippolyte, Saint-Cyr, Saint-Saud, Saint-Aquilin. These are medieval local saints, evangelists of small valleys, patrons of markets long since moved to larger towns. Their communes survive. The saints are forgotten except on road signs.

A geography of persistence

The distribution is not random. The Massif Central — Creuse (30.6%), Ardèche (29.3%), Haute-Vienne (28.7%), Dordogne (28.4%) — is the heartland. This is old Catholic France: rural, mountainous, slow to change. Communes here were named in the early Middle Ages and, unlike in the revolutionary north, rarely renamed.

Alsace is the sharpest counter-example. Bas-Rhin has a saint-commune rate of 1.4%. Haut-Rhin, 2.5%. The region only became French in 1648 (Westphalia) and again in 1919 (Versailles), and its naming traditions are Germanic and Alsatian rather than Latin Catholic. Corsica is similar: its communes follow a separate Genovese-influenced tradition, giving just 0.8% (Corse-du-Sud) and 0.4% (Haute-Corse).

Paris — 21 communes in its département — has zero. The capital's commune names are arrondissements by number, and its nearby satellites carry secular names: Montrouge, Vincennes, Pantin, Ivry. The Revolution renamed what the Middle Ages had consecrated.

Masculine and feminine

Of the 4,410 saint communes, 4,051 use the masculine Saint- and just 359 use the feminine Sainte-. There are plenty of Sainte-Marie (50), Sainte-Colombe (33), Sainte-Croix (27) — but for every Sainte there are more than eleven Saints. This is not a quirk of the data; it reflects the medieval church's canon, which was overwhelmingly male. The Blessed Virgin gets special treatment — she appears in composite names like Notre-Dame or Mont-Saint-Michel — but in the plain naming convention, the saints are mostly men.

France has had 230 years to rename these communes. After 1789, the Revolutionary government tried: Saint-Martin-de-Ré became Port-de-Ré, Saint-Malo became Port-Malo, Saint-Nazaire became Port-Nazaire. Most reverted within a decade. In 1793, the Convention gave up. The saints, it turned out, were harder to dislodge than the kings.

A note on the data. Commune names and centroids come from the DREES APL 2023 commune file, which covers 35,014 communes. Saint communes were identified by matching names against the pattern Saint(e)- using a regular expression. Centroids are computed as the mean of each commune polygon's boundary vertices. The 4,410 figure may slightly overcount if a commune's name contains Saint as a geographic descriptor rather than a patron saint (e.g. Saint-Clair-sur-Epte), but no correction was applied; the pattern is robust at this scale.
