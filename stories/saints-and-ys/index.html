The names France gave its villages

35,014 communes, one of them called "Y". 3,882 called Saint-something. And one name so long it took two villages and seven hyphens to settle on.

2026-05-16 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. They range from Paris — 2.1 million people, a city dense enough to be its own département — all the way to Rochefourchat, in the Drôme, which last counted 1 resident. They carry names drawn from Latin saints, Norman lords, Occitan rivers, and centuries of local politics. And then there is Y.

Y Commune in the Somme (département 80). Population: 89. The shortest place name in France — and, debatably, in the world.

Y sits about 25 kilometres east of Amiens, between the Canal de la Somme and a flat stretch of Picardy farmland. It has a church, a mairie, and a name that confounds GPS systems and postal workers in equal measure. Its INSEE code is 80829. The name, spelled and pronounced exactly as written, appears to derive from a Gaulish root meaning "water" — cognate with the Latin aqua.

The two-letter club

Y is not alone. France has 13 communes with names of just two letters, each a small puzzle in its own right. Eu, in Seine-Maritime, is a real town of 6,591 residents and was a royal residence of the Duc d'Orléans. Gy, in Haute-Saône, has a thousand inhabitants and a Gothic church. Oô, in Haute-Garonne, sits at the foot of the Pyrénées and pronounces its name roughly like oh-oh. Together they are a strange club.

Move up to three letters and the list includes Gap — préfecture of the Hautes-Alpes, population 40,500, a city of real consequence with a name the length of a password hint. Gex, in the Ain, sits 10 kilometres from Geneva. France does not seem to find short names undignified.

The Saint empire

At the other end of the cultural register: the saints. 3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. France was christianised commune by commune, and the naming record makes that visible. The apostle who covered the most ground was Martin: 202 communes carry his name in some form, from the bare Saint-Martin (five examples) to Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, one commune, Calvados). Jean comes second at 155; Pierre third at 144.

Most common saint-name families

Counts include all communes whose name starts with Saint-X or Sainte-X (e.g., Saint-Martin and Saint-Martin-de-Belleville both count toward Saint-Martin).

The saints are not evenly distributed. Normandy (Seine-Maritime has 10 Saint-Martins alone), Burgundy, and the old pays of the west run densest. The south contributed its own traditions — more Saints in Occitan forms, more saints with Iberian origins near the Pyrénées. And everywhere the same practical problem: if your commune is one of 155 Saint-Jeans, how does the postman know?

35,014 communes, 32,764 names

The answer is the INSEE code — a unique five-digit identifier assigned to every commune in France. France has only 32,764 distinct commune names for 35,014 communes: 1,450 names are shared by two or more communes. Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times. There are 11 Saint-Sauveurs, 10 Saint-Aubins, and 10 communes simply called Beaulieu. The most-duplicated non-saint name is Le Pin, with nine examples, each in a different département.

The hyphen arms race

38.3% of France's communes have at least one hyphen in their name. Most are straightforward compound forms: Aix-en-Provence, Villers-lès-Nancy. But a small number reflect something stranger — the fusion of two communes that refused to choose. When two villages merge under French administrative law, they often keep both names, joined by a hyphen. And if each village already had a hyphenated name, the result can run to extraordinary lengths.

The current record-holder is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne (département 51): 45 characters, 7 hyphens, population 491. Below it, the longest names cluster in Haute-Saône, Normandy, and Burgundy — regions with a history of small commune consolidations in the 1970s under the Loi Marcellin.

    The INSEE code is 51513. Nobody knows it. The name is typed, abbreviated, cursed, and somehow remembered. It is, in its way, a perfect object: a bureaucratic record of two villages that merged their administrations but never quite agreed on which one mattered more.

    Y has a mairie. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson has a mairie. Every Sainte-Colombe has a mairie. Each has a five-digit code, a seat on the departmental council, a budget for road salt and primary schools. The names are an accident of history; the administration is very much on purpose. France governs 35,014 units of local democracy, and it has given each one, however briefly, a name worth saying aloud.
