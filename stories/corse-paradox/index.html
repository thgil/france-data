The Corsica paradox

Haute-Corse has more bars per capita than any French department. It also has 49% of its communes with no accessible GP. The same island holds both records.

2026-05-10 5 min read

If you rank France's 96 metropolitan departments by bars per 1,000 residents, the winner is not Paris, not Bretagne, not the Nord — it's Haute-Corse. The island department has 2.11 bars for every thousand people it officially records. Paris sits seventh on the same list, at 1.46. The Paris suburbs — Essonne, Yvelines, Hauts-de-Seine — sit at the very bottom, below 0.35.

Bars are a proxy for a certain kind of social life: the café as village institution, the zinc as the place where everything happens. By that measure, Corsica seems abundantly alive. But look at the same island through the lens of healthcare access, and a different picture emerges. Haute-Corse has the highest share of communes with an APL score of exactly zero — 116 of its 236 communes, or 49%. In half the island's communes, the official estimate of GP accessibility is not low, not scarce: it is nothing.

The full ranking by bars per 1,000 residents reveals a clear pattern: rural and mountain departments lead; the Paris suburbs trail. Corsica takes the top two spots. Lozère (1.76) and Cantal (1.66) round out the top five — sparsely populated highland departments where the café has historically served as the town's only meeting place. At the other end, the IDF departments cluster: dense, suburban, commuter-oriented, with the lowest bar density in France.

The tourist coast and the interior

The paradox resolves when you look at where, on the island, both the bars and the APL=0 communes are. Corsica is not uniformly bar-rich. Its bars are heavily concentrated along the tourist coast — the resort towns of Calvi, Porto-Vecchio, Bonifacio, and Saint-Florent — and in the two cities of Bastia and Ajaccio. These coastal centres have both high bar densities and high APL scores, because the same concentration of seasonal visitors that fills the terraces in July also sustains a year-round population of doctors serving a large catchment area.

Saint-Florent (Haute-Corse), pop. 1,689. Sixteen bars — 9.5 per 1,000 residents. APL score: 7.42, nearly three times the desert threshold. A small coastal town with summer tourism and year-round GP access. Both records belong to the same town type.

Inland, the picture changes sharply. The mountainous interior of Corsica — the Castagniccia, the Niolo, the Sartenais — contains hundreds of hamlets with fewer than 500 residents, no resident GP, and often just one bar (or none). The bar, where it exists, is the social hub; but it cannot replace the absent clinic.

What the scatter shows

Plot each Corsican commune as a dot — bars per 1,000 on the horizontal axis, APL score on the vertical, dot size proportional to population — and the tourist coast / remote interior split becomes visible. The orange dots (APL above 4.0) cluster in the middle range of bar density: Bastia, Ajaccio, the coastal resorts. The red dots at the bottom (APL = 0.00) are scattered everywhere on the bar axis: some have bars, some don't. Doctor access and bar access are not the same thing, and they follow different geographies.

Why both records co-exist

France's APL score measures something specific: the estimated number of GP consultations available per resident per year, computed using a catchment-area model that accounts for travel time. For a commune to have APL = 0, there must be no GP within a reasonable travel band — or more precisely, no GP whose catchment this commune falls into. In the Corsican interior, where the nearest town with a doctor can be 30–40 minutes of mountain road away, this condition is easily met.

The bars, meanwhile, measure something else: the historic density of débits de boissons, alcohol-licensed premises, recorded in the SIRENE business register. Corsica's high count reflects both genuine café culture (the island has a strong tradition of the local bar-tabac as civic institution) and the island's unusual economic geography: a large seasonal tourist population generates commercial density that the permanent population alone would not sustain.

Linguizzetta (Haute-Corse), pop. 1,103. One bar. APL score: 0.00. The bar exists; the doctor does not. The two maps — bars and GP access — do not overlay neatly.

The result is an island that scores paradoxically on national rankings: first in bars per capita, in the top tier for APL=0 communes. France's bar map and France's healthcare map are both real. They just describe different things — and in Corsica, the gap between them is most visible.

Data

Bar counts: INSEE SIRENE national snapshot, March 2026, filtered to NAF 56.30Z (débits de boissons), geocoded via SIRENE Géolocalisation. 49,385 active establishments. Department-level bar-per-capita computed by joining SIRENE bar commune codes to DREES commune populations.

Healthcare access: DREES APL aux médecins généralistes 2023, commune-level scores, joined to administrative boundaries. APL = 0.00 means the catchment-area model found no accessible GP for that commune's residents.

Method

Bars per 1,000 residents = total SIRENE-registered bars in a department ÷ total DREES population in that department × 1,000. Metropolitan departments only (excluding overseas 97x codes). Small departments (pop below 50,000) excluded from ranking. The APL=0 count covers communes where the 2023 score is literally 0.00, not just below the 2.5 desert threshold.
