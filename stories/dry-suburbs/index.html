The dry suburbs

France has 7.5 bars per 10,000 residents. Haute-Corse has 21. Essonne has 3. The bar desert isn't where you think it is.

2026-05-08 4 min read

The previous story in this series mapped 49,385 French bars by location. The obvious question was where you find the most — and the count clearly favoured Paris (3,120), Nord (1,961), and the big Mediterranean départements. But raw counts reward size. The more revealing question is: where do you find a bar per resident?

The answer inverts almost everything you'd expect.

Corsica at the top — and it's not close

Haute-Corse leads the table at 21.1 bars per 10,000 residents — one bar for every 474 people. Corse-du-Sud follows in third at 16.9. Together, the island accounts for two of the top three slots nationally, ahead of Lozère (17.6, second), Cantal (16.6, fourth), and Hautes-Alpes (15.6, fifth).

None of these départements are densely urban. What they share is a combination of dispersed rural settlement, strong café culture as a form of civic infrastructure, and — in Corsica's case — a year-round tourism economy that both generates and sustains bar licences. The village café in rural France isn't just a bar: it can double as the post-office relay, the only hot meal within fifteen kilometres, and the place where the commune's social life happens. Small populations keep the numbers low in absolute terms, but those few establishments are doing real work per resident.

Paris: comfortably above average, but not first

Paris (75) ranks 7th nationally, at 14.6 per 10,000 — double the national average and roughly tied with Alpes-de-Haute-Provence (04). This tracks: the inner city has an extraordinary density of licensed premises along its boulevards and in its arrondissement centres. But it's not the leader. Corsica and four small mountain départements beat it, partly because those areas have fewer "bar substitutes" (restaurants, cinemas, department stores) competing for the same social function.

The suburban paradox

Look at the bottom of the table. Essonne (91) is last: 3.07 bars per 10,000, one bar for every 3,259 residents. Above it: Yvelines (3.3), Hauts-de-Seine (3.3), Val-d'Oise (3.7), Seine-et-Marne (3.8), Val-de-Marne (4.0). Six of the ten least-served départements in France are Île-de-France suburbs. Seine-Saint-Denis (5.3) is the exception that proves the rule — its denser older urban fabric means more neighbourhood bars.

This is the suburban paradox. The Paris suburbs are among the most populous, most connected, and most economically productive territories in France. They are not bar deserts because they are poor or remote. They are bar deserts because of how they were built.

The lotissements pavillonnaires and grands ensembles constructed from the 1950s to the 1980s were designed around the car and the commute, not around the street-level café. Commercial zones were segregated from residential ones. A neighbourhood that was designed as a dormitory for Paris workers had no organic need for a licensed premises on the ground floor. Decades later, the licensing numbers reflect that planning logic exactly.

The same geography, again

The diagonale du vide — the low-density band cutting from Meuse to the Pyrenees — appears in the bars-per-capita ranking too, but here it scores better than average, not worse. Rural France is not a bar desert. The Gers (14th), Creuse (13th), Nièvre (12th) all sit comfortably above the national rate. The places that are genuinely thin on bars per resident are, almost without exception, either the urbanised IDF ring or the highly suburbanised areas around major cities like Toulouse (Haute-Garonne, ranked 88th at 4.2 per 10k) and Strasbourg (Bas-Rhin, 89th at 3.9).

The pattern that keeps surfacing in this data — medical deserts, bakery deserts, bar deserts — is fundamentally a story about planning models and their long aftermath. The map of bars per capita in 2026 is, in large part, a map of how France built housing in 1970.

A note on the data. Bar counts come from INSEE SIRENE (NAF 56.30Z — débits de boissons, active establishments, March 2026). Population figures are INSEE 2021 commune-level counts aggregated to département, sourced from the DREES APL commune dataset used in Story 2. 96 départements are included (metropolitan France plus all five overseas départements; the aggregate "97" bucket in SIRENE is excluded). The national average is population-weighted across these 96 units.
