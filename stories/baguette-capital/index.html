The baguette capital

6,299 bakeries, 1,300 communes, one question: which IDF commune has the most bread per person?

2026-05-16 4 min read

Paris 18e arrondissement has 155 bakeries — more than any other commune in Île-de-France, and, by most accounts, more than anywhere else in France. That number appears in travel guides. It feels like an answer. But baguette capitals aren't built on raw counts. They're built on the ratio of bread to people.

First, the obvious winners

By raw count, the ranking is almost entirely a Paris story. Every arrondissement in the top thirteen is within the périphérique. Saint-Denis (Seine-Saint-Denis) sneaks into 14th place with 67 bakeries — the only non-Paris entry in the top fifteen. Paris as a whole holds 1,800 of the region's 6,299 boulangeries: 29% of the bread for 19% of the population.

The ranking flips

Normalize by resident population and the order inverts completely. Paris 1er arrondissement leads with 20.8 bakeries per 10,000 residents — one boulangerie for every 481 people. The 18e, all 155 shops of it, manages just 7.7 per 10,000: solid, but 12th among Paris arrondissements on this metric. The city that seemed like the answer turns out to have internal variation of nearly 3-to-1.

The 1er is a business district and tourist hub. Its official resident population — 17,308 — dramatically understates the full daily bread demand. Office workers arriving by the million on weekday mornings, visitors clustered around the Louvre, students crossing from the nearby Quartier Latin: every croissant counts, and many are counted against a denominator that doesn't include the buyer. On real-world demand, the 1er's lead would be wider still.

The market towns of Brie

Outside Paris, a cluster of small market towns in Seine-et-Marne hold their own. Rebais — a bourg of 2,151 in the agricultural plain of the Brie, 70 km east of Paris — has five bakeries: 23.2 per 10,000, nearly twice the IDF average. Bourron-Marlotte, tucked at the edge of the Fontainebleau forest, has four for 2,700 residents. These are places where the boulangerie is still genuinely the social heart of the commune — the one shop that reliably opens at six in the morning and fills the square with the smell of bread.

These small-town numbers are fragile: a single closure can erase a commune from the top 20 overnight. That fragility is itself a story. The village boulangerie is protected in law (France subsidises bakeries in rural communes through the boulangerie de pays scheme) precisely because its disappearance would hollow out the commune in a way that losing any other shop would not.

Paris is still an outlier

At the département scale, the picture simplifies. Paris runs at 8.0 bakeries per 10,000 residents — 50% above the IDF average of 5.3, and nearly twice the outer ring. The inner suburbs cluster between 4.9 and 5.2; the sprawling outer grande couronne sits at 4.3–4.5. The gradient is clean: the more urban the density, the more bread per head.

Nearly half of IDF has no bakery

The baguette is not a universal pleasure in Île-de-France. Of 1,300 communes in the region, 607 — 47% — have no recorded bakery at all. These are not obscure hamlets: they include established suburban municipalities where residents drive to a supermarket or a commercial zone for their bread, if they buy it at all. The artisanal boulangerie is a city-centre and market-town phenomenon. It thins through the inner suburbs and disappears across much of the rural fringe and commuter belt.

So: which is the baguette capital?

It depends on what you're counting. By raw number of boulangeries: the 18e arrondissement, 155 shops. By bakeries per resident: the 1er arrondissement, 20.8 per 10,000 — one for every 481 people. By the feel of a town built around its bread, where the boulangerie is the first building to light up in the morning and the last to lock up at noon: a small commune in the Brie, where five bakeries serve two thousand people and the ratio alone can't capture what it means.

The three answers are all correct. They just measure different things, and France being France, the difference matters.

A note on the data. Bakery counts come from the INSEE Base Permanente des Équipements (BPE), Île-de-France region, distributed by Région Île-de-France. The vintage is circa 2014, with population from the 2010 census. Coverage is 1,300 communes and Paris arrondissements in IDF. National coverage (for a true France-wide baguette capital story) would require the full SIRENE parquet extract, which is out of scope here.
