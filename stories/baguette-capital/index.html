The baguette capital

6,299 bakeries, 1,300 communes. Which corner of Île-de-France is most bread-obsessed by the numbers?

2026-06-07 4 min read

Île-de-France has 6,299 active boulangeries spread across 1,300 communes. Every one of them is a registered business, classified under NAF code 10.71C, with a population to serve and a commune to call home. The obvious question is: which commune wins on a per-resident basis? The honest answer has two parts — one for small villages, one for actual cities — and a surprise either way.

The village answer: Châtenoy, 67 per 10,000

If you rank all 693 communes that have at least one bakery, the winner is Châtenoy (Seine-et-Marne, pop. 149): one bakery, 67 per 10,000 residents. Second place goes to Villiers-en-Bière (pop. 215), also Seine-et-Marne: 46.5 per 10,000. The top of this list reads like a tour of small Brie villages. These are real artisan boulangeries, genuinely beloved — but the number is partly a small-denominator artefact. One bakery in a village of 150 people will always beat a city arrondissement with 50 bakeries and 50,000 residents.

The pattern is geographically coherent, though. Many of these bread-rich villages sit in Seine-et-Marne (département 77), IDF's large, rural eastern département. A single artisan bakery per village is the historical norm there — and it survives. Compare to Yvelines or Essonne, where the same village-sized communes often show zero.

Highest density — cities (min. 5,000 residents)

Most bakeries by count

The city answer: Paris 1er, 20.8 per 10,000

Filter to communes with at least 5,000 residents and Paris's inner arrondissements take the top seven slots. The 1er leads at 20.8 bakeries per 10,000 — roughly one for every 481 residents. The 4e (16.1), 8e (14.8), and 9e (13.0) follow. These are tourist-heavy, high-footfall arrondissements. The bakery density there isn't serving residents alone; it's also serving the daytime population of offices, hotels, and visitors.

Suburbs that might surprise: Enghien-les-Bains (8th, 11.9/10k) and Le Raincy (9th, 11.6/10k) both exceed many Paris arrondissements. Fontainebleau (10th, 11.2/10k) benefits from being a small historic town — compact geography, dense local commerce.

Paris leads by département too

Bakeries per 10,000 residents — by département

Paris (8.0/10k) is the only IDF département to exceed 6 bakeries per 10,000 residents. Seine-Saint-Denis (93) is second at 5.2/10k — and it's the département where 100% of communes have at least one bakery registered (Paris also hits 100%; the outer five all fall short). Yvelines (4.5/10k), Essonne (4.3/10k), and Val-d'Oise (4.4/10k) cluster just below the IDF average of 5.3/10k. This is the same outer ring that ranks among France's worst medical deserts — the structural access gap runs through bread as surely as it runs through GPs.

607 communes, but only 3.4% of the population

607 of IDF's 1,300 communes have zero recorded bakeries. That sounds alarming. But 97% of those communes are small: together they hold only 400,924 residents — 3.4% of IDF's 11.8 million people. Most live within a short drive or train ride of a bakery in a neighbouring commune. The real bakery deserts — communes where bread access is genuinely difficult — are narrow, thinly populated, and concentrated in the outer edges of Seine-et-Marne and Val-d'Oise.

The fact that 3.4% of the population equals zero-bakery communes makes IDF unusually well-covered by international standards. France's combination of regulatory protection for the boulangerie artisanale label and strong neighbourhood-commerce culture means the trade has survived where supermarket chains might have replaced it elsewhere.

A note on the data. Bakery and pharmacy counts per commune come from the INSEE Base Permanente des Équipements (BPE), Île-de-France edition, covering 1,300 communes and Paris arrondissements. Population figures are from the 2010 census. The BPE vintage is circa 2014, so absolute counts may have shifted — but the geographic pattern is durable. Bakeries per 10,000 figures are pre-computed in the source data; density rankings for cities use only communes with at least 5,000 residents to avoid small-denominator distortions.
