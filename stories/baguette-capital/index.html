The baguette capital

6,299 bakeries across Île-de-France. One arrondissement has a boulangerie for every 481 residents — and 607 communes have none at all.

2026-05-20 5 min read

Île-de-France has 6,299 registered bakeries spread across 1,300 communes and 12 million people. Counting them gets you to the dot-density map. But the more interesting question is density: where is the bread thickest, and where does it run out?

The standard measure is per 10,000 residents — and on that scale, Paris's historic centre dominates. The spread across its twenty arrondissements is wider than you might expect.

The per-capita ranking

Paris 1er tops the list at 20.8 bakeries per 10,000 residents — one boulangerie for every 481 people. It has just 17,000 residents, but 36 bakeries: nearly one per street. The 4th (16.1) and 8th (14.8) follow. At the other end, the 13th sits at 5.7 per 10,000 — barely a quarter of the 1st's rate, one bakery for every 1,745 residents.

The pattern has a geographic logic. The inner arrondissements — the 1st through the 9th, clustered around the Seine's bends — were built for street-level commerce. The 1st's narrow medieval lanes and the 6th's covered arcades were designed before the automobile. High foot traffic, small units, affordable rent for neighbourhood shops: the conditions that sustain a boulangerie have barely changed in 200 years. The outer arrondissements, built later and wider, run at roughly half the density.

Among the suburbs, a few outliers punch above their weight. Enghien-les-Bains (95, spa town: 11.9), Le Raincy (93: 11.6), and Fontainebleau (77, tourist town near the forest: 11.2) all clear Paris's lowest arrondissements. But they're exceptions. Most of the inner suburbs — Boulogne, Levallois, Vincennes — settle in the 5–8 range. The outer ring is thinner still.

The bread deserts

More surprising than who's bread-richest is who has nothing at all. 607 of IDF's 1,300 communes — 46.7% — have zero recorded bakeries. Taken together, those communes are home to 400,924 people.

Seine-et-Marne is the worst case. 301 of its 514 communes (59%) have no bakery at all. The département is IDF's largest geographically — stretching south and east into genuine agricultural countryside, into villages of 300 people where the last baker retired and no one took over. Val-d'Oise and Essonne follow, each with roughly half their communes without any bakery.

The contrast with the inner ring is stark. Hauts-de-Seine has 36 communes and just one (Sèvres) with no bakery. Val-de-Marne: 47 communes, one bread desert. The density of the inner suburbs is sufficient to sustain the trade. In Seine-et-Marne, it often isn't.

The same map, again

The gradient is clean: the closer to central Paris, the better served. Paris itself: 0% of arrondissements without a bakery. Hauts-de-Seine and Val-de-Marne: 2–3%. Essonne and Val-d'Oise: 42–46%. Seine-et-Marne: 59%.

If you've read the medical deserts story, this pattern is familiar. The same periphery pays the price — not from policy or poverty, but from the arithmetic of foot traffic. A village of 400 people cannot sustain a boulangerie any more than it can retain a general practitioner. The economics are identical: a small business needs enough passing customers to stay open. Below that threshold, the business closes, the baker retires, and the commune joins the list of 607.

The bread map and the doctor map are, it turns out, the same map. So is the bar map. The diagonale du vide is also a diagonale du pain.

A note on the data. Bakery counts by commune come from the INSEE Base Permanente des Équipements, Île-de-France edition (republished by Région IDF). Population figures are from the 2010 census in the same dataset. Both are circa 2014 vintage — more than a decade old. The number of bakeries has likely shifted (especially in Seine-et-Marne, where rural decline has continued), but the structural pattern — dense inner city, sparse outer ring — is durable. A full-France, current-vintage version of this analysis would require the national SIRENE parquet file, which is 2.6 GB and out of scope here.
