The Doctors' League Table

Thirteen French regions, ranked by access to a GP. The league leader is Provence. The bottom side is the Loire Valley. Île-de-France is in the relegation zone.

2026-05-07 5 min read

France's medical desert map shows the problem at commune level: 12,771 of 34,886 communes fall below the official APL 2.5 threshold. But communes are unequal in size — a desert commune might hold 80 residents or 80,000. To see who actually bears the burden, you need to aggregate by population. Do that, and a sharply different picture emerges than the one on the commune-count map.

The APL (accessibilité potentielle localisée) score estimates how many standardised GP consultations a resident can access per year, accounting for distance, travel time, and local demand. A score below 2.5 is officially classified as a zone sous-dense — a medical desert. The national average is around 3.7. But the national average conceals a 46% spread between the best- and worst-served regions.

The surprise at the bottom

Centre-Val de Loire — last place, APL 2.92. One in three residents (34%) lives in a medical desert. Nearly 60% of its 1,754 communes are under-dense. The Loire Valley — famous for its châteaux, its wine, its claim to "pure French" pronunciation — has the worst population-weighted medical access of any metropolitan region.

Centre-Val de Loire is not France's most rural region. It contains large cities: Orléans (pop. 116,000), Tours (138,000), Chartres (40,000), Bourges (65,000). Those cities are well served, and their GPs pull the regional average up. But the region's interior — the Beauce plain (Eure-et-Loir, Loiret), the empty farmland of the Sologne (Loir-et-Cher), the declining towns of Berry (Indre, Cher) — is an almost unbroken medical desert. It is flat, fertile, sparsely populated country where villages can be 30km apart and the bus runs twice a day.

Eure-et-Loir (28) and Indre (36) are among the worst-served départements in metropolitan France. Together they contribute more than 350,000 desert-dwellers — a significant fraction of the region's 875,000 total — to France's 11-million-strong desert population.

The surprise at the top

Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur — first place, APL 4.25. Just 4.3% of its 5.1 million residents are in a medical desert. PACA has a higher population-weighted APL score than Paris.

PACA's top ranking is counterintuitive: southern France is not typically associated with healthcare efficiency. The region contains large areas of rugged arrière-pays (Alpes-de-Haute-Provence, Hautes-Alpes) where access should be poor. In fact both Alpine départements rank among the best-served in the country — the concentration of ski stations has produced an unusually dense network of GPs serving both residents and seasonal visitors.

But the bigger driver is the Côte d'Azur. Alpes-Maritimes (06) and Var (83) have extremely high GP densities driven by decades of retirement migration. Wealthy retirees with high healthcare demand created a market; French GPs followed. The Bouches-du-Rhône (13) — dominated by Marseille, France's second city — is similarly well served. The combination of retirement wealth on the coast and ski infrastructure in the Alps produces a region that simply has more doctors per resident than anywhere else.

The industrial north's secret

Hauts-de-France — second place, APL 4.13. Nord, Pas-de-Calais, Picardie. Often described as France's Rust Belt. Second in medical access.

Hauts-de-France is France's most densely populated region outside Île-de-France. Its industrial history — coal, steel, textiles — built dense towns with functioning institutions: hospitals, social housing, GPs. The concentration of population in Lille, Douai, Valenciennes, Amiens, and the mining belt means the average resident is never far from a doctor. Only 9.4% of the region's 6 million residents are in a medical desert — the same rate as Bretagne, and lower than Occitanie.

The Île-de-France paradox

Île-de-France places twelfth out of thirteen — second to last, ahead of Centre-Val de Loire. Its population-weighted APL of 3.26 is pulled up by Paris itself (APL 4.96) and dragged down by the surrounding départements. Seine-et-Marne (77), Yvelines (78), Val-d'Oise (95), and Essonne (91) all sit near or below the desert threshold.

In absolute terms, Île-de-France's 3.47 million desert-dwellers dwarf every other region. The runner-up, Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes, has 1.2 million — barely a third. Paris grows its GP count slowly while the suburbs grow fast; the gap between the city proper and the banlieue is the structural story of IDF health access.

What drives the regional pattern

Three forces shape the league table. First, retirement geography: where retirees move, GPs follow — PACA and Bretagne are both retirement magnets and both perform well. Second, population density: dense industrial towns (Hauts-de-France) sustain large GP catchment areas; dispersed farming country (Centre-Val de Loire) cannot. Third, the Paris effect: Île-de-France's enormous suburban population, sprawling across poorly-served communes nouvelles far from any city centre, is the single largest contributor to France's 11-million desert population.

The APL score itself is a flow model: it estimates consultations accessible within a realistic travel radius, adjusted for how many people are competing for the same GPs. A commune that gains a new GP sees its APL rise not only there but across a wide radius — the system is more interconnected than a simple per-resident ratio would suggest. What the regional table ultimately shows is that France's medical geography is not primarily an urban/rural split: it is a retirement-coast surplus, an industrial-town baseline, and a suburban-Paris deficit.

A note on the data. APL scores are from DREES (Direction de la recherche, des études, de l'évaluation et des statistiques), 2023 vintage, covering 34,886 metropolitan French communes and overseas territories. This story uses metropolitan France only (excluding départements 97 and 98). Population figures are INSEE 2021 census estimates embedded in the same dataset. "Population-weighted APL" is computed by weighting each commune's APL by its population and summing within region; this gives the APL score that an average resident of the region experiences, as opposed to an arithmetic mean across communes (which would overweight small communes). The "desert threshold" of APL 2.5 is the DREES official zone sous-dense definition.

Regional boundaries follow the 2016 reform (13 metropolitan regions). Département-to-region mapping is based on the INSEE COG. Corse (2A + 2B) is included as a metropolitan region despite its island geography.
