A Town Called Y

France has 35,014 communes. One is named "Y". Thirteen more have two-letter names. And 12 different communes are all called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-29 4 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — the smallest administrative unit in the Republic, ranging from Paris (2.1 million people) down to villages barely large enough to fill a church. Each has a name. Most names are sensible and medieval: Saint-something, Beau-something, Vieux-something. And then there is Y.

Y
Commune · Somme · Population 89

Y sits in the Somme, about 100 km north of Paris. It has 89 residents, a post office, and a name that is, by every metric, the shortest place name in France. The origin is almost certainly Gaulish or Latin via Old French — either ia (water) or a contracted form of a longer Roman settlement name worn down to a single syllable over the centuries. At some point, somebody stopped abbreviating.

The two-letter club

Y is alone at one letter, but it has 13 near-neighbours in the two-letter bracket. They form an eclectic group: a Seine-Maritime pair (Eu and Ry), a Pyrénéan trio (Ur, Py, Uz), a Haute-Garonne hamlet (Oô — with its magnificent double-o), and the well-known market town of Eu, population 6,591, which once hosted William the Conqueror and now hosts a Sunday market and a château open to visitors.

Oz, in Isère (population 213), is a ski resort in the Alpes. Us, in Val-d'Oise (1,338), is a quiet suburb between Pontoise and Cergy. None of them require two syllables.

The saint army

3,882
communes begin with Saint- or Sainte- — 11.1% of all French communes

France is a republic, but its geography is still thoroughly Catholic. Nearly one in nine communes starts with Saint or Sainte. The most contested holy name: Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 communes spread across seven départements, each one presumably convinced it is the real Sainte-Colombe. Runner-up is Saint-Sauveur, at 11. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times.

The saints are not just a naming curiosity — they are also a dating tool. Villages that were founded or renamed between the 11th and 13th centuries almost universally adopted a patron saint's name. Villages that predate Christianity — particularly in Brittany, Alsace, and the Basque Country — didn't, and those regions have the most distinctively non-Saint names on the map.

The other extreme

At the far end of the distribution sits the longest commune name in France:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne · Population 491

This is not one village — it's three, merged by administrative fiat in 1972 and told to pick a single name. The compromise: keep all three names, joined with hyphens and an et. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson have been hyphenated together ever since, their shared name longer than most tweet-length sentences. Similar logic produced Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône (43 characters), the runner-up.

A more recent count of the 35,014 communes finds that 32,764 of them have unique names — meaning about 2,250 share a name with at least one other commune. Navigation in France has always required a département.

Seven characters, on average

The distribution of name lengths has a clear peak at 7–8 characters and a long right tail. Short names are rare because they're old — a 7th-century settlement worn smooth to two letters is the exception, not the rule. Long names are rare because they're usually administrative accidents: mergers that preserved everyone's identity by concatenating it.

The bimodal bump you can see around 15–20 characters is largely the Saint- compounds: Saint-Martin-de-Quelquechose runs to 25–30 characters without even trying. Strip the saints out and the distribution would be almost perfectly right-skewed around 6–7 characters.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the APL 2023 dataset published by DREES, which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. The commune name field (communeName) is as recorded in the INSEE administrative register. Names with accented characters (Bû, Oô) are preserved exactly. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the same dataset.
