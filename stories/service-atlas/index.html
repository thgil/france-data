France, by the numbers
Culture May 2026 5 min read

Everything at once, or nothing

1,300 communes in Île-de-France. Three essentials: a bakery, a pharmacy, a bar. 537 communes have all three — and they hold 94% of the region's people. 509 communes have none.

There's a test you can apply to any French commune. It has three parts. Does it have a bakery — somewhere to buy bread before 8 a.m.? A pharmacy — somewhere to fill a prescription without driving across a département? A bar — somewhere to sit down with someone?

In Paris, all twenty arrondissements pass. The 1st arrondissement has 36 bakeries, 19 pharmacies, and 123 bars — that last figure works out to 71 per 10,000 residents, more than ten times the national average. Even the quieter arrondissements, the residential 15th and 13th, have dozens of each. The capital is, by this measure, extravagantly served.

The picture changes quickly once you leave the périphérique.

Just 2.3% of the population — 275,000 people — live in a commune with no bakery, no pharmacy, and no bar.

The distribution is lopsided in a way that reflects something fundamental about French geography. Most communes are small; most people live in large ones. The 509 "empty" communes are mostly tiny villages in Seine-et-Marne or the outer Yvelines, places with a few hundred residents and no commercial street. They rely on neighbouring towns for every errand. The 537 fully-served communes are the cities, inner suburbs, and market towns — and together they hold nearly everyone.

Zoom to Seine-et-Marne. This is the largest département in Île-de-France — 514 communes, more than the other seven combined — and the one that makes the map look complicated. Of those 514 communes, 254 score zero. Another 78 have just one service. Only 132 have all three.

The geography is not random. The green communes are the old market towns — Meaux, Provins, Fontainebleau, Coulommiers — plus the suburban corridor along the RER lines. The red ones are the villages between: places built around farming and forestry rather than commerce, where the weekly market two villages over is the nearest thing to a bakery.

The inner ring tells a different story entirely. Hauts-de-Seine (92), Seine-Saint-Denis (93), and Val-de-Marne (94) together cover 123 communes. Of those 123, only two score below three — and both of those have two services rather than none. These are the petite couronne, the dense inner suburbs that grew with postwar Paris and never stopped. Every commune has a high street. Every high street has bread, medicine, and somewhere to drink.

The pattern holds a quiet lesson about how French village life actually works. The bar, the bakery, and the pharmacy are not three separate decisions. They live or die together, on the same commercial streets, responding to the same pressures: enough foot traffic, enough residents who don't own a car, enough density to sustain a business on margins that were never generous. Where you find one, you usually find the others. Where you find none, you find a commune that relies entirely on the car and the town next door.

The 275,000 people living in those 509 communes know this already. They drive for bread. They drive for prescriptions. There's nowhere local to sit down with someone. The map is green where people are dense enough to sustain a commercial street; it's pale where they're not. It's a measure of density dressed as a measure of services.

What makes the IDF version of this story interesting is the scale of the contrast. Within one région — a small one, by French standards, barely 12,000 km² — you have Paris's 1st arrondissement (71 bars per 10,000 residents) and rural Seine-et-Marne villages where there's nothing at all. The Périphérique is twelve kilometres from the most bar-dense patch of France. From there to the nearest commune with zero services is maybe forty kilometres more.

Sources & notes

Service counts per commune: bakery and pharmacy data from INSEE BPE (IDF commerces) and Région IDF — Carte des pharmacies, both circa 2014 vintage. Bar counts from SIRENE national snapshot (NAF 56.30Z, March 2026), filtered to IDF departments (75, 77, 78, 91, 92, 93, 94, 95). Commune polygons from the same IDF BPE source.

A commune "has" a service if it has at least one recorded establishment. Zero-count communes may include places genuinely without the service, or places where SIRENE/BPE coverage is incomplete (particularly for bakeries, which use the older BPE vintage). The ~12-year gap between the bakery/pharmacy data (2014) and the bar data (2026) means a small number of communes may have gained or lost services in the interim. The broad pattern — a dense urban core surrounded by a rural periphery with no commercial street — is stable across all three datasets.
