The Saints' Republic

One commune is called Y. Another's name runs to 45 characters. Between them lie 35,014 names — a 1,000-year record of faith, geography, and the French gift for bureaucratic creativity.

2026-05-14 4 min read

France has a commune called Y. Not Ys, the legendary sunken city of Breton myth — just Y. One letter. It sits in the Somme (département 80), has a population of 89, and has been a separate administrative unit since long before the Revolution. The postal code is 80190. The mayor presumably signs letters with a single character for "commune name".

Y is an extreme, but it isn't alone. France's 35,014 communes carry names ranging from one letter to 45. The distribution below shows the full range — and why both ends of it are worth examining.

The two-letter clubs

Below Y are 13 communes with two-letter names. They are scattered across France, without obvious geographic clustering — these are ancient place names that happen to survive in compressed form, often from Gallo-Roman or Germanic roots contracted by centuries of phonetic wear:

Most are tiny. Sy (Ardennes) has 54 people. Py (Pyrénées-Orientales) has 79. But Eu (Seine-Maritime) is a market town of 6,591 — its name is ancient Norman, possibly from the Latin Augium. Bû (Eure-et-Loir) has 2,022 residents and a boulangerie.

One in nine communes is a saint

France is officially a secular republic — but you wouldn't know it from the commune register. Of 35,014 communes, 3,898 begin with "Saint" or "Sainte": 11.1% of the country's administrative map is named after a Christian holy figure.

The concentration is highest in the Massif Central and the west. In Ardèche (07), 29% of communes carry a saint's name. In Creuse (23) and Loire (42), it approaches 28%. These are old territories where the parish was the first unit of local identity, and the saint was the parish.

The most common name in all of France is Sainte-Colombe — 12 different communes share it, in 12 different départements. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11. Saint-Aubin, Saint-Marcel, Saint-Michel, Saint-Pierre, and Sainte-Marie each appear nine times. If you are standing in a French village called Sainte-Colombe, there are 11 others just like it.

The hyphen as history

38.3% of French communes — 13,410 of them — contain a hyphen. Many are ancient compound toponyms: Pont-sur-Seine (bridge on the Seine), Villers-lès-Nancy (Villers near Nancy), Fontaine-sous-Jouy (spring below Jouy). The hyphen was standardised in the 19th century as part of the administrative rationalisation that froze place names in their modern form.

But a new wave of long hyphenated names arrived after 2015, when the loi NOTRe pushed municipalities to merge into communes nouvelles. The resulting names often concatenate all the constituent villages, preserving everyone's identity in one unwieldy official title. The extreme result:

What a name says

French commune names encode geography (Rocher, Val, Pont, Mont), religion (3,898 saints), feudal origin (Villers, Villeneuve, Beaumont), and linguistic layers — Latin, Frankish, Breton, Basque, Alsatian, Occitan — deposited in the order that cultures arrived and left. The name Y doesn't tell you any of this, which is perhaps why it feels so strange. It is a place that somehow escaped being described.

The longest name tells the opposite story: a name that tried to describe everything at once, and nearly succeeded.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories.
