The city floor

Medical deserts are a rural problem — except when they aren't. Argenteuil has 107,000 people, a direct RER line to Paris, and an APL of 1.74.

2026-05-08 5 min read

France's medical desert map is a geography lesson about rural decline. The diagonale du vide — the empty diagonal from the Meuse to the Pyrenees — shows up on every APL choropleth. Small towns, scattered hamlets, the deep countryside. The story writes itself.

Except size isn't destiny. The relationship between how many people a commune has and how well-served it is medically turns out to be messier, and more surprising, than the map implies.

Bigger towns fare better — mostly

Among France's 34,349 communes with an APL score, the desert rate drops steadily as population grows. Half of the smallest communes — those with under 100 residents — sit below the 2.5 threshold. For towns of 500 to 2,000 people, it's still nearly one in three. By the time you reach cities of 50,000 or more, the rate falls to 7.1%.

The improvement is real but uneven. The sharpest drop happens in the transition from small villages (under 2,000) to market towns (2,000–10,000). After that, the rate doesn't fall much as cities get bigger — towns of 10,000 and towns of 50,000 both hover near 14%. Something structural keeps mid-sized French cities persistently underserved relative to their population.

The cities that didn't get the memo

Eleven communes with more than 50,000 inhabitants are officially medical deserts. That's 7.1% of France's large cities — a small share, but the cities in question are not small. Their combined population is nearly 700,000 people.

Argenteuil has 107,221 residents and an APL of 1.74. That puts it in the same medical-access bracket as Ardengost — a village of 12 people in the Pyrenees. The RER C runs through Argenteuil. It is twelve kilometres from the Arc de Triomphe. It is, by official measure, a medical desert.

The ring strikes again

Look at where the large-city deserts cluster and a familiar pattern emerges. Of the eleven communes with over 50,000 people below the APL threshold, nine are in Île-de-France. Not Paris itself — Paris's arrondissements score between 3.9 and 6.5 — but the inner and outer suburbs that ring it: Val-d'Oise, Seine-et-Marne, Yvelines, Essonne, Seine-Saint-Denis, Hauts-de-Seine, Val-de-Marne.

The medical desert story has always had two faces: the rural diagonale du vide, and the invisible ring around Paris. At large-city scale, the ring is essentially the only story. Argenteuil, Sarcelles, Villejuif, Bobigny, Chelles, Corbeil-Essonnes, Sartrouville, Sevran, Clamart, Antony — all suburban Paris, all medical deserts despite their size.

Where the big cities get it right

On the other end of the scale, France's great regional capitals dominate the top of the access rankings. Saint-Paul on Réunion scores 6.88 — likely boosted by its status as the island's largest city with concentrated health infrastructure. Among metropolitan France, Strasbourg (6.46), Bordeaux (6.35), and Lille (6.11) lead. Amiens, Angers, Caen, Metz — France's mid-tier provincial capitals cluster near the 5.5–6.0 range. These are cities that have retained their function as service hubs for a wider hinterland: universities, hospitals, specialist practices, all concentrated in one place.

Paris itself is split. The city's APL isn't a single number — it breaks down by arrondissement, ranging from 3.89 (16e, wealthy but GP-light) to 6.54 (1er). The inner Paris average sits comfortably above 4.0. The city is not a desert. Its suburbs often are.

The floor, not the ceiling

City size sets a floor for medical access — not a ceiling. The gradient is real: France's tiniest communes suffer the most. But being large doesn't guarantee being well-served. The Paris ring demonstrates that you can have 100,000 residents, a motorway bypass, and an APL that puts you in the rural desert bracket.

Across all communes, 16.4% of the French population with an APL score — about 11 million people — lives below the 2.5 threshold. 1,211 communes score below 1.0, the severe desert level. Those are rural. The suburban ones don't make the headlines, but they add up.

A note on the data. APL scores (Accessibilité Potentielle Localisée) come from DREES's 2023 commune-level release — 34,886 communes, of which 34,349 carry a numeric score. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL dataset. The APL threshold for official sous-densité médicale designation is 2.5 standardised consultations per resident per year. The analysis uses the same geojson source as the medical deserts story.
