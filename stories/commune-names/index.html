What's in a name?

35,014 French communes. One is called Y. Twelve share a name. 3,898 are saints. A short journey through France's most overlooked data.

2026-05-13 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, between Amiens and the Belgian border, there is a commune called Y. Not an acronym. Not a local abbreviation. Just Y — a single vowel, a population of 89, a mayor, a church, and a French postcode. It is the shortest official commune name in France, and quite possibly in the world.

France has 35,014 communes, and their names are a compressed archive of the country's entire history: Catholic devotion, feudal geography, Norman vocabulary, Revolutionary renaming, and a remarkable willingness to live somewhere called Oz or Bû.

The very short

Y is not alone. France has 13 two-letter communes, almost all concentrated in a belt from Normandy to the Alps — old place-names eroded by centuries of oral use. Most are tiny. Some are surprisingly busy.

The two-letter names follow a distinct geography: they cluster in Normandy (Seine-Maritime), Franche-Comté (Haute-Saône, Doubs), and the Pyrenees (Pyrénées-Orientales). Eu in Seine-Maritime (pop. 6,591) is the largest — a market town with a château where Queen Victoria once visited. Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées (pop. 36) is the smallest. Between them, they span everything from a ski resort (Oz, Isère) to a spa town (Gy, Haute-Saône).

The shape of a commune name

The typical French commune name is 10 characters long — median exactly 10, mean just under 12. The distribution peaks around 7–8 characters, then spreads out through a long right tail of hyphenated compound names. That tail is where France gets creative.

The very long

At the other extreme, French communes can accumulate names like freight cars. The longest in the dataset is a small village in the Marne:

45 characters. This is a commune nouvelle — a merged entity created when several villages joined administratively while preserving each former commune's name in the title, hyphenated together. The Marne département, flattened by World War I and since repopulated by mergers, is especially rich in these compound names. The Haute-Saône (département 70) also punches well above its weight: three of the top-ten longest names are there.

The saints

3,898
communes beginning with Saint or Sainte — 1 in 9 French communes

France's commune names are a hagiography. Nearly 11% of the country's communes are named after a saint — roughly 3,900 individual entries beginning with Saint- or Sainte-. The most common saint name, Sainte-Colombe, appears 12 times. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Saint-Pierre each appear 9 or 10 times.

The saints are not evenly distributed. Dordogne (130), Gironde (121), and Isère (106) lead in absolute count, reflecting two different patterns: the densely-churched Atlantic coast of Aquitaine, and the large northern Rhône-Alpes communes that were named before the Revolution and never renamed.

The problem with Sainte-Colombe

There are 12 communes named Sainte-Colombe. They are in Ain, Ardèche, Côte-d'Or, Gers, Gironde, Maine-et-Loire (×2), Rhône, Savoie, Seine-Maritime, and Yonne. France's postal system handles this by appending the département number or a local suffix — but in casual use, the duplicates are simply a puzzle.

In total, 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in France — roughly 1 in 10. Many of these duplicates cluster around religious or feudal names that were independently adopted across different regions.

The archive

The names are not random. They map the deep structure of French history onto the landscape. The saints cluster in the Atlantic coast and Rhône valley — regions of intense Romanisation and early Christianisation. The compound hyphenated names concentrate in the east and the Massif Central, where mergers were most aggressive. The short names are Norman and Franc-Comtois — remnants of older Romance dialects that wore words down to their roots.

And then there's Y, sitting in the Somme, 89 people, holding the record simply by being there. The name probably comes from a Germanic root *ija, meaning "water" — a long vowel carrying 1,500 years of quiet endurance.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the communes-apl.geojson file used for the medical deserts story, which draws on INSEE's administrative commune register (2025 vintage, contours from data.gouv.fr). Centroids were computed as polygon centroid averages. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census embedded in the APL file.
