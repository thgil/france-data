One French commune is called Y. Eighty-nine people live there. Here's what 35,000 names say about a country.

2026-05-20 4 min read

In the département of the Somme, about 130 km north of Paris, there is a commune called Y. That's the entire name — one letter. Population: 89. The local post office can address a letter there. Google Maps can find it. The INSEE knows its code (80829). It is a real place, and it has the shortest official name in France.

At the other end of the alphabet, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson sits in the Marne with 491 residents and a name that runs to 45 characters. It was created in 1973 by the merger of three villages: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. Nobody wanted to be left out.

Y is not the only survivor of the pre-French-standardisation naming era. Thirteen other communes manage with two letters. Most end in -y — an ancient Gaulish suffix meaning "domain of." Gy (Haute-Saône), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes). Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591) is the largest of the two-letter communes. Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées has 36 residents and is almost certainly a Gascon toponym. Oô in the Haute-Garonne has a circumflex and a population of 103.

A nation of saints

France has 3,882 communes whose names begin with Saint or Sainte. That is 11.1% of all communes — roughly one in nine. The most popular saint in the French landscape is not the patron of France (Denis) nor the most famous French saint (Joan of Arc). It is Martin: 202 communes invoke Saint-Martin, spread from Alsace to Aquitaine to the Caribbean. Saint-Jean follows with 155, then Saint-Pierre (144) and Saint-Germain (108).

The twelve Sainte-Colombes

Twelve communes in France share the exact name Sainte-Colombe — one in each of twelve different departments, from the Haute-Savoie to the Gironde, from the Seine-Maritime to the Yonne. They are all named for the same early Christian martyr, a Roman girl executed in Sens in the third century. And they are all, officially, Sainte-Colombe.

The problem of identical names runs through French administrative history. The 1790 law that created the commune system assigned each village its own legal identity but made no rule about uniqueness of names. Many Sainte-Colombes, Saint-Aubins, and Beaulieus coexisted without administrative conflict because mail, births, and taxes were handled locally. When centralisation increased in the nineteenth century, the duplicates were asked to disambiguate — which is why you now also find Sainte-Colombe-de-Peyre (Lozère), Sainte-Colombe-en-Bruilhois (Lot-et-Garonne), and others. But twelve communes still go by the unadorned name, trusting their postal codes to do the work.

The grammar of geography

French commune names are miniature geography lessons. The connective prepositions embedded in thousands of names describe the physical landscape in which the settlement sits.

These prepositions are fossils. When a village first acquired a written name — in a land survey, a church record, a tax roll — it described itself in relation to the landscape: on the river, in the forest, under the escarpment. The landscape changed. The roads moved. The river was dammed. But the name stays.

And so France carries its geography in its addresses — a country where 11% of communes claim a saint, where 1,991 sit on water they named themselves after, and where one village in the Somme decided, at some point in the Middle Ages, that a single letter was quite enough.
