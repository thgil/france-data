35,014 communes — one is named Y

The single-letter commune, the 12 villages of Sainte-Colombe, and a 45-character name buried in the Marne.

2026-05-18 4 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — cities, towns, and villages that form the basic unit of French administrative life. Each one has a name. Most names are sensible: Bordeaux, Lyon, Saint-Étienne. But the full list, read carefully, is a small masterpiece of accident and history.

One commune is called Y. Another — Sainte-Colombe — exists twelve times, scattered across twelve different départements. And somewhere in the Marne, a commune holds the longest official name in France: 45 characters, three saints, one hyphen-joined monument to the complexity of post-revolutionary municipal mergers.

One letter

France's shortest commune name is a single letter. Y, in the Somme (département 80), has a population of 89 and an APL score of 2.78 — technically not a medical desert. The name comes from Old French, meaning water, and the commune has held it since before the Revolution. It is not a postal curiosity or a data error. It is a real place.

Below Y, there are thirteen communes with two-letter names. The range is surprisingly coherent — most end in a vowel, several are in Normandy and the Pyrénées-Orientales, and one, Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop 6,591), is large enough to be an actual market town.

The saint problem

France is a country with 4,437 communes that carry the word Saint or Sainte. That's one in every eight communes — a density that reflects the country's Catholic past more directly than any survey. The word "saint" appears not just as a prefix but as a load-bearing architectural element of the national map.

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears twelve times. Each Sainte-Colombe is its own separate commune, in its own département, with its own mayor, its own school, and its own APL score. One of them (département 05, in the Hautes-Alpes) has a score of 0.0 — no GP access at all. Another (département 77, Seine-et-Marne) has nearly 1,800 residents. They share a name and nothing else.

Name length: a bell curve with extremes

Most French commune names are between 6 and 12 characters — Dijon (5), Bordeaux (8), Montpellier (11). The distribution peaks around 8–9 characters and falls off steadily. At the far right tail, things get interesting.

45 characters

France's longest official commune name is a 45-character record of a specific political moment: the post-revolutionary consolidation of small communes into larger administrative units, carried out incompletely, producing hyphenated monsters that survive to this day.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 45 characters · Population: 491 · INSEE code: 51507

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. Two saints, one marsh, one et. It sits in the Marne (département 51), has a population of 491, and — improbably — is not a medical desert (APL 3.12). It fits on official envelopes. Barely.

What the names carry

A commune's name is a stratigraphic record — layers of Latin, Old French, Occitan, Breton, Alsatian, and Basque, compressed into a label on a tax form. Eu is Old French for water. Y is the same word, compressed further. Oz (Isère) is from the same root. Oô (Haute-Garonne) adds a second vowel to push the absurdity a little further.

The repeated Sainte-Colombes aren't duplicates — each is a different dedication to Saint Columba of Sens, a third-century martyr, made independently by twelve different villages at twelve different points in history. France didn't standardise; it accumulated.

35,014 communes. One letter. Twelve Sainte-Colombes. A 45-character survivor in the Marne. The names are a map of everything France chose not to simplify.
