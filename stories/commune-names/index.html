What's in a name?

35,014 communes, one called Y, and 3,898 saints. France's place names are an encyclopaedia of history, geography, and peculiarity.

2026-05-27 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in the European Union. Germany, with a larger population, has around 11,000. The United Kingdom has roughly 10,000. France has three and a half times as many. Each one has an official name, registered with the Code officiel géographique, unchanged unless the commune merges or votes to rename itself. Taken together, those 35,014 names are a compressed history of the country — Roman origins, Carolingian saints, Viking settlements, the Republic's obsession with geography, and a few things that are just funny.

One letter

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter: Y. It sits in the Somme, in Hauts-de-France, population 89. Its name comes from the Old Picard word for water — the commune sits near the Noye river. In an era before GIS and online forms, a one-letter commune name was unremarkable. Today it breaks nearly every database field that requires a minimum of two characters.

A bimodal distribution

Plot name lengths on a histogram and something unexpected appears: two humps. The first peaks at 7–8 characters — clean, unadorned names like Nîmes, Troyes, Bayeux. The second rises around 17–18 characters, built from hyphenated compounds: Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Fontainebleau, Clermont-Ferrand. The gap between humps is the dividing line between simple names and the hyphenated ones — and 38.3% of French communes have at least one hyphen.

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne, at 45 characters. It honours not one but two saints, plus three formerly separate villages. Population: 491. The name is longer than most Twitter bios and does not fit comfortably on a road sign.

Saint-Martin variants

Nearly one commune in eight starts with "Saint" or "Sainte" — a direct imprint of the medieval Church on French administrative geography. The Christianisation of Gaul from the 4th century onward attached patron saints to parishes, and those parishes became communes after the Revolution of 1789. The Revolution abolished the Church's temporal power but kept the place names.

The Dordogne leads with 130 saint-prefixed communes (26% of its total). The Ardèche (29%), Loire (28%), and Creuse (28%) are close behind. These are old, rural, heavily Catholic territories — the same counties that appear at the fringes of the diagonale du vide in the medical deserts map.

If you count "Saint" appearing anywhere in the name — not just as a prefix — the number rises to 4,443. Saint-Martin alone, in all its forms (Saint-Martin-de-X, X-Saint-Martin, Mont-Saint-Martin), accounts for 250 of them. Martin of Tours, patron saint of France, is the most geographically persistent figure in French civic life.

The duplicate problem

Because so many communes were named independently by their local parish, France ended up with hundreds of exact duplicates. The most common full commune name — shared by exactly 12 unrelated villages in 12 different parts of France — is Sainte-Colombe. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu appear 10 times each. When you look up a commune name in a French administrative database, you have to know the département code too, or you'll get the wrong village.

Le, La, Les, L'

Another 2,220 communes start with a definite article — 758 with Le, 1,054 with La, 328 with Les, 80 with L'. The article is legally part of the name and affects alphabetical sorting: Le Mans is filed under "L", not "M". For decades this caused chaos in official registers, directories, and train timetables. In 1942 the French government introduced an alternative: for sorting purposes, the article is ignored and the noun is used. But official legal names still include the article, and French bureaucracy still occasionally gets confused about which rule applies when.

A geography you can read

Taken together, the 35,014 commune names form a map of French history readable at a glance. The saints give you the medieval Church. The Le / La / Les articles give you the influence of Old French grammar. The hyphenated compounds give you the pragmatic mergers of the 19th and 20th centuries, where two villages decided to share administration but not identity. And the single letter Y gives you the Picard language that predates French itself, preserved in a field on a government form because no one ever felt the need to change it.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the INSEE Code officiel géographique, as embedded in the DREES APL commune-level dataset (2023 vintage, 35,014 communes). Population figures are the INSEE 2021 census counts included in that dataset. Name-length statistics, saint counts, and duplicate tallies were computed from the full list; small communes added or renamed after the 2023 vintage may not appear.
