What's in a name?

35,014 communes. One is called Y. There are 3,898 saints, 12 Sainte-Colombes, and one town whose name has seven hyphens.

2026-05-25 4 min read

France is a country of 35,014 communes — its most local unit of government, the building block below the département and the région. Most of them have perfectly ordinary names: Brest, Lyon, Versailles, Poitiers. Some do not.

Y

Commune de Y — Somme (80) — population 89

Y is a village in the Somme, département 80, with 89 residents and a single-letter name that has baffled the French postal service for centuries. It is the only one-letter commune in France. But it is not alone in its extreme brevity.

The alphabet communes

Alongside Y, France has 13 two-letter communes: Bû, By, Eu, Gy, Oô, Oz, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, and Uz. They're scattered from the Ardennes to the Pyrénées-Orientales. Oô, in Haute-Garonne, is the only one with a circumflex on its second letter, which has been causing typesetting problems since at least the 15th century. Uz, in the Hautes-Pyrénées, has 36 residents — fewer people than letters in some of this story's other commune names.

Shortest commune names in France

The saint army

The single most common first word in French commune names is Saint. By a wide margin. 3,596 communes open with "Saint" as a standalone word; add the 295 that start with "Sainte," and you reach 3,891 — more than 11% of all communes in France. The next most common first word is "La" (1,054 communes), which Saint beats by a factor of 3.4.

The Catholic naming of communes reflects centuries of parish geography. Before the Revolution, most French villages were simply named after their patron saint. The Revolution renamed a great many of them — adding geographic suffixes like -sur-Loire, -en-Provence, or merging names with hyphenated identifiers. The result is visible in today's commune registry: the saint ecosystem, though stripped of ecclesiastical power, remains embedded in the map.

The 12 Sainte-Colombes

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have names that are completely unique. The remaining 2,250 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. The most common name is Sainte-Colombe — 12 communes bear it, spread across six departments from the Ardèche to the Gironde. Ask for directions to "Sainte-Colombe" without a department code, and you have a one-in-twelve shot of being sent to the right place.

Most common commune names (number of communes sharing the name)

The top of the list is almost entirely saints. Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin (10), Saint-Marcel and Saint-Michel (9 each). The one secular outlier in the top five is Beaulieu — "beautiful place" — shared by 10 communes. At the bottom of the duplicate list sit communes like Brion, Fleury, and Mons, each shared by seven separate places. When you tell someone in France you're from Mons, the follow-up question is always: which one?

Seven hyphens

At the other end of the spectrum sits a commune in the Marne whose name was assembled, like many long French commune names, by merging two smaller villages during the administrative reforms of the 19th and 20th centuries. The result:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (51) — 491 residents — 45 characters — 7 hyphens

It is the longest commune name in France. Population 491. APL score 4.09 — so at least it's well-served by doctors, which is more than can be said for many shorter-named places. The second-longest is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters), followed by Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41). Every one of the five longest commune names in France involves a Saint.

The naming conventions of French communes are, in miniature, a history of the country: Roman roots, medieval parishes, Revolutionary republicanism, administrative mergers, and a stubborn attachment to local identity that survives every reform. The 89 residents of Y go to the mairie, pay their taxes, and elect their mayor, exactly as residents of Lyon do. The name is just shorter.
