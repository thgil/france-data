What's in a French commune's name?

35,014 communes. One is called Y. 3,898 start with Saint. And 12 separate places share the name Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-18 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in the European Union. They range from Paris (2.1 million people) to a handful of villages with fewer than ten residents. Their names span Norman Norse roots, Latin ecclesiastical tradition, and fusions born of post-Revolutionary municipal mergers. One commune in the Somme is named Y. Just the letter.

Together they form a naming system that was never really designed — it accumulated. The medieval church stamped its presence across thousands of villages. Latin geography provided mont, val, bois. Germanic settlers left -ville and -court. Every historical wave left a layer. And then the communes nouvelles started merging villages in the 2010s, producing hyphenated masterpieces like the one at the bottom of this page.

The saint republic

The single most dominant pattern in French commune naming is the church. 3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. That's before counting the thousands more where a saint's name appears mid-word, like Beaulieu-sous-Sainte-Colombe or Montoire-sur-le-Loir.

3,898 communes start with Saint or Sainte — one in nine

When you count every instance of the word "saint" anywhere in a commune's name, the tally climbs to 4,090. The most common saint is Martin, appearing in 255 commune names. Jean (170), Pierre (180), and Germain (120) follow. The Gallic church's canon is mapped across the countryside, one village at a time.

The extremes

At one end: the Somme département contains a commune called Y. One character. Population 89. It sits at the end of the list alphabetically, which has presumably confused many a French form designer. The commune has existed since the medieval period; the name may derive from a Gaulish or Germanic root, but the definitive etymology is uncertain. Thirteen French communes have two-character names: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Su, Sy, Ur, and Us.

At the other end of the spectrum, the 2015 municipal merger law (loi portant nouvelle organisation territoriale de la République, or NOTRe) produced a new category of extravagant compound names. The current record holder is in the Marne département.

The 45-character champion was created in 1974 when the communes of Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson merged with Saint-Remy-sur-Voire. Each village insisted on keeping its name. The compromise was hyphenation, and the result is a commune name longer than most English sentences.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

France never built a namespace. The same commune names recur across the country — sometimes a dozen times. The winner, by count, is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 separate communes in 12 different départements, from the Hautes-Alpes to Yonne. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu, 10 each.

Name Communes

For maps and databases, this is a genuine problem: the INSEE commune code (a 5-digit number like 69123) is the true unique identifier. The name alone is not. When someone says they're from Saint-Aubin, there are nine possible answers.

Short names and long names, but not many in between

Plot the length of all 35,014 commune names and you get a curve that peaks around 7–8 characters, drops toward the teens, then rises again for the 15–21-character compound names. The average is 11.8 characters; the median is 10. There are 79 communes with names of 30 characters or more — all products of mergers.

The pattern mirrors the two eras of French municipal geography. Before the 19th century: short, organic names — the village by the woods (Bois-le-Roi), the ford on the river (Gué-d'Issy), the saint on the hill. After the 2010 merger laws: administrative poetry by committee, hyphenated and exhaustive, preserving every village's name in a single, unwieldy string.

The republic has 35,014 communes. Their names are a geological record of how France was built — one saint, one merger, one awkward hyphen at a time.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to the Etalab contours administratifs 2025 file, the same source used in the medical deserts story. 35,014 communes are included; a small number of overseas communes and very recent mergers may not be represented. Name length includes spaces and hyphens as written in the official INSEE registry.
