From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another takes 45 characters to type. In between: 11% are saints, 1,450 names have twins, and Sainte-Colombe appears twelve times.

2026-05-18 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, there is a village called Y. Not an abbreviation. Not an acronym. Just the letter Y — France's shortest commune name and, by most counts, one of the shortest place names anywhere on earth. Population: 89. Department: Somme (80). It sits on the edge of the Viry marshes, east of Amiens. Its APL score is 2.78 — not a medical desert. Beyond that, it is mainly remarkable for being called Y.

The name predates standardised French. It likely derives from ieu or eau, a topographic word for water or low-lying marshy land, which in the local Picard dialect contracted over centuries into a single vowel. The village didn't choose the name. The name chose it.

The fourteen-member club

Y is the sole one-letter commune. But 13 more have names of exactly two characters — a small club of linguistic relics, most of them tucked away in corners of France where old dialects left their marks before standard French swept through.

The list reads like a Scrabble hand: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir, the largest of the group at 2,022 residents), Eu (Seine-Maritime, 6,591 — an actual town, home to the château where Victor Hugo visited), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime — the village thought to have inspired Flaubert's Madame Bovary), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Most have fewer than 500 residents. Eu, at over 6,000, is the outlier.

One in nine communes is a saint

France is officially a secular republic — laïcité is a constitutional principle. The commune registry did not get the memo. 3,891 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That is more saint-named communes than most countries have communes.

The most common opening word in any French commune name is Saint — by a wide margin. It appears 3,596 times as a standalone first word before a hyphen. Add the 295 Sainte- forms and you have nearly 3,900 consecrations. Second place goes to La, at a distant 1,054. The secular third republic renamed almost nothing.

There are twelve Sainte-Colombes

Because saints are finite and France is large, collisions were inevitable. Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times across 12 different departments — from the Hautes-Alpes to the Val-d'Oise. If you receive a letter postmarked "Sainte-Colombe, France" with no postal code, you have a one-in-twelve problem. The total population across all twelve is about 5,600 people. None of them are famous enough to have definitively claimed the name.

Sainte-Colombe tops the list, but the duplication problem runs deep: 1,450 commune names are shared by at least two places. That covers 3,700 communes — more than one in ten. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times. The French postal system earns its keep.

Forty-five characters

At the other end of the scale, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson holds the record for France's longest commune name: 45 characters including hyphens. It sits in the Marne (department 51), with a population of 491 and an APL score of 4.09 — well-served by doctors, at least. The name reflects the 2016 merger of three previously separate communes: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest-et-Isson, and fragments of a local administrative history compressed into a single hyphenated run-on.

Runner-up: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41, Calvados). Many of the longest names are products of post-2015 municipal fusions — France's programme of commune mergers aimed at reducing administrative overhead. The result, unintentionally, was the creation of names that no one can type without looking them up.

A bureaucratic fossil record

Taken together, France's commune names are a geological cross-section of the country's history. The two-letter stubs are Gallo-Roman or earlier — places that existed before French as we know it, abbreviated by time. The saint names are medieval Catholicism, preserved in amber despite two centuries of republican secularism. The duplicate names reflect a fragmented kingdom that was unified administratively but never renamed. The merger monsters at the top of the length chart are a very 21st-century problem: the bureaucratic side-effect of rationalisation.

And then there is Y. Eighty-nine people, one letter, no explanation needed. It has outlasted Latin, French, three republics, a revolution, two world wars, and the postal modernisation programme. The Somme marches are still wet. The name is still Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 file (data.gouv.fr), which contains 35,014 communes with official INSEE codes, names, département codes, populations (INSEE 2021 base), and APL scores.
