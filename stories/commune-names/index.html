What's in a name?

35,014 communes. One is a single letter. More than one in ten start with Saint. The longest takes 45 characters to type.

2026-05-23 4 min read

Every French commune has an official name. 35,014 of them, to be exact. Most names are perfectly ordinary — Beaumont, Les Pins, Villeneuve. But not all of them. One commune gets by with a single letter.

Y

Population: 89. Département: Somme (80). Postcode: 80190. It is a real place. The village pub, if there is one, presumably has a very short address.

Y is not alone at the short end. France has 13 two-letter communes — a set of one-syllable curiosities scattered from Normandy to the Pyrenees: Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591), Oz (Isère, pop. 213), Gy (Haute-Saône), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, pop. 36). Among them, Oô (Haute-Garonne) achieves two letters using only one distinct character — and with a circumflex, no less.

A bell curve, with a very long tail

The typical French commune name is between 7 and 10 characters long. Beaumont, Montfort, Villeray — names that fit comfortably on a road sign. But the distribution has a tail. A long one. As communes have merged over the centuries, some names have compounded into hyphenated chains that no road sign can accommodate.

45 characters

France's longest commune name belongs to a village of 491 people in the Marne:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (51) · population 491

The name is a merger artifact. In 1972, three villages — Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — consolidated under a single commune, concatenating their names with hyphens. The result is a place that takes longer to write than the average tweet hashtag.

The runner-up, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters), is also a merger. The pattern holds all the way down the long-name list: most names beyond 35 characters are administrative fusions.

One in nine commune-dwellers lives in a Saint-somewhere

France has 3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte. That is 11.1% of all communes — more than one in ten. Their combined population is 6.6 million, or roughly 9.8% of the country. The patron saint system was the default naming convention for medieval parishes, and it stuck.

The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times across 10 différent départements. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11 instances. No two are the same commune — they differ in department, size, and administrative code — but if you're trying to find the right one, the postcode is your only friend.

1,991 communes on a river (or at least they say so)

The -sur- particle — meaning "on" — appears in 1,991 commune names. It typically denotes a location on a river, and it's a reliable guide to French hydrology. -sur-Mer leads with 99 communes claiming to be on the sea. The Loire and the Seine are tied for first among rivers, each lending their name to 70 communes. The Marne follows with 55.

Most of France's 35,014 commune names are ordinary words — saints, rivers, forests, hills. But the list is long enough that statistical outliers are guaranteed. A commune called Y. Twelve places called Sainte-Colombe. A 45-character administrative compound in the Marne. The naming system wasn't designed to be rational; it accumulated over eight centuries of parish records, royal edicts, and post-war fusions. Every quirk in the list is a small piece of administrative history that got preserved in an official register and never changed.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL dataset joined to administrative boundaries (35,014 communes including overseas territories, 2023/2025 vintage). This story uses the same commune index file (communes-index.json) that powers several other stories on this site.
