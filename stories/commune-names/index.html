What's in a name?

35,014 commune names — from the one-letter village of Y to a 45-character merger in the Marne. France's naming patterns, counted.

2026-05-16 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Not Yvelines, not Yvetot — just Y. Single letter. 89 residents. It sits in the Somme, east of Amiens, and it is almost certainly the shortest place name in France, and one of the shortest in the world.

The commune of Y has existed since at least the 12th century. Nobody is quite sure what the name originally referred to. A river, possibly. Over the centuries it got shortened and simplified until nothing was left but the letter. It still appears on road signs, in postal codes, on administrative documents. France files paperwork about Y.

The saint epidemic

3,898 French communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. Add in all the communes where Saint appears elsewhere in the name (Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, say, or Villefranche-sur-Saône) and the figure rises to 4,437. Nearly one in eight French places is, in some sense, a saint.

This is the Catholic map of France made visible. Missionaries spread across the country between the 5th and 12th centuries and named everything they found after a patron saint. The names stuck. A thousand years of secularism, revolution, and redistricting have barely dented the count. Open a road atlas at random and odds are good you'll land on a saint.

France's most crowded name

Of the 35,014 communes, 32,764 have distinct names — meaning 2,250 names are shared by at least two different communes. The most crowded name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times across 12 different departments: from the Hautes-Alpes to the Yonne, from the Gironde to the Haute-Savoie. All tiny. None adjacent. Each presumably sure it is the real one.

Eleven of the top 15 most common names are saints. The exceptions — Beaulieu (×10), Beaumont (×8), and Verrières (×8) — follow a different medieval logic: beautiful view, beautiful hill, glass-making valley. The naming conventions are finite. The landscape kept repeating.

After Y: the alphabet soup

After Y (1 letter), France has 13 communes with two-letter names. They cluster in Normandy (Ry, Eu, Ri), the Basque and Pyrenean borderlands (Ur, Uz, Py, Oô), and scattered across the interior (By, Gy, Sy, Bû, Oz, Us). Most are of pre-Latin or Germanic origin — place names that survived precisely because they were short enough to resist corruption over the centuries.

The hyphenated map

French commune naming follows its own grammar. The most common connector is -sur- — over a river, usually — which appears in 1,991 names: Clermont-sur-Lauquet, Bagnères-de-Luchon, Dun-sur-Meuse. The connector encodes geography.

-en- means "in" (a territory, a forest, a plain). -les- comes from Latin for "the" — usually attached to the nearest big town: Fontenay-les-Briis, Boissy-les-Perche. -sous- means "beneath" (a hill, a château, a ridge). -et- joins two merged villages into one improbable bureaucratic name.

The longest name in France

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne · 45 characters · 491 residents

This 45-character commune in the Marne is not an accident — it's a policy outcome. The 1972 loi Marcellin offered subsidies to tiny communes that agreed to merge, on the condition that they keep both original names hyphenated together. Hundreds took the deal. The result: a long right tail on the name-length distribution, where a cluster of 50+ communes have names over 30 characters long.

The runners-up are similarly baroque: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 chars), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41 chars). Each name is a merger certificate. The villages survived; the distances between them have not changed.

The shape of all the names

Most commune names are 7 or 8 characters long. The distribution peaks there and falls off sharply in both directions — quickly toward the very short, slowly toward the very long. That long right tail is the loi Marcellin at work: merge small, hyphenate big, keep both names.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the INSEE geographical contour file (Contours administratifs communes 2025), joined to the DREES APL 2023 dataset used in the medical deserts story. The combined file covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL data. Some very small overseas communes have population = 0 (unpopulated atolls and islands in French Polynesia).

Name-length analysis and duplicate-name detection were computed directly from the dataset. All 35,014 commune names and INSEE codes are available in communes-index.json (2 MB).
