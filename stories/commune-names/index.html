The village called Y

35,014 communes. One has a single-letter name. 3,882 start with Saint. And 89% have names no other village on Earth uses.

2026-05-24 4 min read

Y is a commune. That's its full, official name — one letter, no accent, no article. It sits in the Somme valley about 40 km southeast of Amiens, population 89. It has a mayor, a street address, and a postcode (80190). INSEE gives it the code 80829. It has been called Y since at least the 12th century, probably after the Y-shaped confluence of the Somme and an old canal. It is the shortest commune name in France.

Y, and its slightly longer cousins

Thirteen other communes make do with two letters. Most are in mountainous or heavily forested territory — Py in the Pyrénées-Orientales (79 residents), Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées (36 residents), Oz in the Isère ski country (213). The exception is Eu, a Seine-Maritime market town of 6,591 people that predates the Norman Conquest; William the Conqueror held court there. The smallest is Sy in the Ardennes, with 54 residents.

Saint country

3,882 communes — 11.1% of France — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. This is what happens when a medieval church planting programme runs for eight centuries and nobody ever decides it's enough saints. The champion base name is Saint-Martin: 202 communes across France share it, scattered from Finistère to the Var. Saint-Jean follows with 155, Saint-Pierre with 144.

Dordogne has the densest saint concentration: 130 saint communes in a single département. The Gironde (121) and Isère (106) follow. The pattern reflects how thoroughly the medieval church organised rural settlement in the south and west — fewer in Alsace (where Germanique place-names dominate), more in Aquitaine and Burgundy.

The multiplied names

Even if we look past the saint prefix, the most common full commune name is Sainte-Colombe — it appears 12 times. There are 12 different places in France that could address their envelopes identically and rely entirely on the département code to tell them apart. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times.

Non-saint names duplicate too: Beaulieu (10 communes), Beaumont (8), Le Pin (9), Verrières (8). These are generic landscape descriptors — "beautiful place," "beautiful hill," "the pine" — the kind of name that gets invented independently every century or so when a village needs a name.

89%
of France's 35,014 communes have names that appear exactly once — shared by no other village in the country. Of 32,764 distinct commune names, 31,314 are unique.

The remaining 11% — about 3,700 communes — share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in France. When a French address says "Saint-Aubin" without a département, it could be one of ten different places, in Normandy, Burgundy, the Jura, or elsewhere. The postal code exists largely because commune naming is spectacularly inconsistent over eight centuries of independent village administration.

The other extreme

At the other end of the length scale, the longest commune name in France weighs in at 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

Marne (dept. 51) · formed by merger · population 571

This is a commune nouvelle — a merged municipality created from smaller villages. French administrative reform since the 1970s has created hundreds of these long hyphenated names, as villages formally unite while insisting on remembering exactly who they used to be. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41 characters) show the same logic: every original village name, joined with hyphens and the occasional et.

Y has 1 character. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson has 45. Both are French communes, both appear in the same national register, both have mayors and postcodes and APL scores. France's commune roll is the accumulated naming decisions of a thousand years of settlement, negotiation, piety, and occasional bureaucratic indifference — a 35,014-entry list where every entry tells a different kind of story.
