Y, and 35,013 other names

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another takes 45 characters to write. Between them: 4,443 saints, 13,410 hyphens, and 12 places called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-19 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, 89 people live in a commune called Y. Not Why, not Yé — just the letter Y, one character, the most efficient place name in France. Its residents receive mail addressed to "Y, 80190, Somme." The village has a Romanesque church, a gentle fold of farmland, and the distinction of being an extreme outlier in a country that has historically favoured very long names for very small places.

Y is the answer to Q-039: which commune in France has the shortest official name? But once you start looking at France's 35,014 communes as a corpus, Y turns out to be just the beginning.

The saint avalanche

The single most common first word in French commune names is Saint — it starts 3,596 of them, more than Le (758), La (1,054), and Les (328) combined. Add Sainte (295) and you have nearly 3,900 communes opening with the word for "saint." Include saints embedded anywhere in the name — Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, for instance, has two — and 4,443 communes (12.7%) carry a saint's name.

This is the legacy of Christianisation, medieval parish records, and the Ancien Régime's habit of naming settlements after their patron saint. The Revolution renamed many of these in a fit of secular zeal — Saint-Denis became Franciade; Saint-Étienne briefly became Armeville — but most reverted. The saints stayed.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The single most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — there are twelve of them, scattered across twelve départements with no obvious geographic pattern. Sainte Colomba was a sixth-century Irish nun; the French apparently liked her enough to name a dozen villages in her honour without any of them becoming particularly famous. Saint-Sauveur comes second with 11, Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu tie for third with 10.

From one letter to forty-five

At the long end of the distribution sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne: 45 characters, two saints, one hyphen count of seven. It was formed in 1972 by the merger of three smaller communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — and rather than pick a name, the commune simply concatenated all three. This is a common source of very long names: French communes merge (sometimes reluctantly), and the longest names are usually the traces of multiple mergers with no one willing to give up their piece of the name.

A culture of hyphens

13,410 communes — 38.3% of all French communes — include at least one hyphen in their name. The hyphen is doing important work: it stitches together saints and geography (Saint-Martin-de-Ré), saints and saints (Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson), geography and geography (Roche-sur-Linotte-et-Sorans-les-Cordiers), and occasionally produces names that defy easy categorisation (Oô, a two-character Pyrenean village with a circumflex and a vowel, no hyphen required).

The non-hyphenated names tell a different story: simple single-word names like Gap, Brest, Lyon, Eu. These are the older names, often pre-Roman or Gaulish in origin, whose short forms survived because they predated the administrative habit of specifying-by-saint.

The shape of French place names

Plot all 35,014 commune names by length and the distribution skews right: most names cluster between 7 and 15 characters, with a long tail stretching out past 30. The median is around 12. The mode is 8–10 characters — names like Beaumont (8), Montigny (8), Villeneuve (10). These are the plain vanilla French place names: a landscape word (beau mont — beautiful hill; ville neuve — new town) with no saint attached, no hyphen required.

Y is waiting at the left edge of that chart, a single bar one character wide, alone in its efficiency. The 12 Sainte-Colombes are bunched together somewhere in the 15-character cluster. And somewhere in the Marne, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is at the far right, taking up more horizontal space than it strictly needs, and not particularly bothered about it.

A note on the data. Commune names, codes, and population figures come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to the IGN contours administratifs 2025 — the same source used in Story 2 (medical deserts). The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte). Name analysis was performed on the communeName field from the joined GeoJSON. Character counts include hyphens, spaces, and diacritical marks but not article elisions (apostrophes are treated as characters). Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base used in the APL file.
