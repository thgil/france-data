35,014 Names

One commune is named Y. Eleven percent bear a saint's name. The longest runs 45 characters. France's commune naming obsession, by the numbers.

2026-05-31 4 min read

There is a commune in France named Y.

One letter. 89 inhabitants. Département de la Somme, code INSEE 80829. It sits about 100 kilometres north of Paris, between Amiens and Saint-Quentin, on a bend of the Somme river. Its name is not an abbreviation. It is not a typo in a government database. It is just Y.

The etymology is genuinely obscure — probably a Picard or Gaulish root meaning "water" or "place by a river", eroded by a thousand years of French phonology into a single vowel. But the administrative fact is harder to explain: when France standardised its commune registers in the nineteenth century, somebody looked at the name on the map and entered it as-is.

Y France's shortest commune name. Somme (80). Population: 89. INSEE code: 80829.

Y is an extreme point on a spectrum that spans 45 characters. The distribution of French commune names by length is one of the stranger frequency curves in French geography — a steep mode around 7–8 characters, a long right tail, and a single isolated bar at length 1.

The long right tail of that histogram is largely a product of French administrative history. The 2010 loi de réforme des collectivités territoriales, and again the 2016 loi NOTRe, encouraged — sometimes pressured — small communes to merge. When two or three neighbouring communes fused, they often hyphenated their names together rather than choose one. The result: "Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne" (40 characters, 381 inhabitants, Côte-d'Or) and "Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur" (43 characters, 923 inhabitants, Haute-Saône).

The saint problem

France has 35,014 communes. 3,898 of them — 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. If you're looking for a Saint-Martin, there are nine of them. Same for Saint-Pierre. Saint-Michel: nine more.

3,898 Communes starting with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of France's total.

This is not simply a religious phenomenon. Medieval settlement names were largely formed around the parish church, and the parish was named after its patron saint. So the density of saint-names in French administrative geography reflects the density of medieval parishes — which reflects, in turn, the density of medieval settlement itself. The diagonale du vide that shows up in medical deserts and bar density also appears here: the sparsely settled central diagonal has fewer saint-names per square kilometre, because it had fewer parishes.

Martin, Pierre, and Jean are the three dominant saints. Martin alone appears in 255 commune names — as Saint-Martin, but also in compounds: Saint-Martin-de-Bréthencourt, Saint-Martin-en-Bresse, Saint-Martin-Boulogne. Pierre appears in 180 names; Jean in 170.

The twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most-shared name in France is Sainte-Colombe. Twelve communes share it, spread across twelve different départements: from the Gironde (33) to the Yonne (89), from the Manche (50) to the Var (40).

None of them is particularly large. None is particularly famous. They share a patroness — a Gallo-Roman martyr of disputed historicity — and little else. A letter from any one of them would need a département code to reach its destination.

The long names

At the other end of the spectrum from Y stands the reigning champion of length:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters · Marne (51) · Population: 491

This is a commune in the Marne, formed by the merger of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont and Saint-Genest-et-Isson. It has 491 inhabitants, a church, and the longest official commune name in France. The name contains two saints and four hyphens. Writing it by hand on an envelope must be a minor exercise in commitment.

What a name tells you

A French commune name is a compressed archaeology. Saint- means a medieval parish. -sur- or -en- places it in geography: on the Seine, in the Bresse, at the ford. Compound names separated by -et- signal a merger. An article — Le, La, Les — often indicates a later topographic settlement rather than an ancient village.

France has 2,220 communes with Le or La or Les at the start. Bois (wood, forest) appears in 217 names. Château in 95. Pont (bridge) in 86, Rivière in 65.

And then there is Y, 89 people on a bend of the Somme, whose name tells you everything and nothing at once.
