Y, Oô, and Sainte-Colombe

France has 35,014 communes. One of them has a one-letter name. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. And the longest name is a 45-character monument to bureaucratic mergers.

2026-05-31 4 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. That's the full name — one letter, pronounced like the French word for the letter itself. It sits in the Somme, département 80, with 89 residents and a mayor who has probably signed more confusing official documents than anyone in the Republic. Every French address system, every form that asks for nom de commune, encounters Y and briefly panics.

Y is the shortest commune name in France. But it isn't alone in its brevity: there are 13 communes with two-letter names — Eu, Gy, Uz, Oô, Bû, and eight others. They survive as placename fossils, pre-Roman roots that never acquired the suffixes and saints that lengthened most French commune names over the centuries.

At the other extreme: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters, seven hyphens, two saints, one et. It's in the Marne — Champagne country — with 491 residents. The name dates to a 1972 municipal merger that fused three separate hamlets and kept all three names, joined in a compromise that nobody was willing to shorten. The result is officially the longest commune name in France, a small bureaucratic epic.

The runner-up is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), followed by Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados). All three are merger products — France's long history of municipal fusion, preserving local identities in hyphenated negotiation.

The Sainte-Colombe problem

France has 35,014 communes, but only 32,764 distinct names. The gap — 2,250 shared names — exists because French communes were named independently, by villages that had no knowledge of each other, all reaching for the same saints, the same landscapes, the same Latin roots.

If you mail a letter to Sainte-Colombe, France, the postal system needs more information. There are twelve of them: in the Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. Their populations range from 59 to 1,953. They share a name and a patron saint — Colombe of Sens, a third-century martyr — and nothing else.

The saint franchise

The most striking pattern in French commune names isn't the duplicates — it's the saints. Of 35,014 communes, 3,882 begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That's 11.1% — roughly one in nine. The Catholic Church was the primary civil authority in much of France for over a thousand years, and its naming conventions are still visible in the administrative geography.

Dordogne leads with 128 Saint- communes — and with only 503 communes total, that means one in four Dordogne communes is named after a saint. Gironde follows with 121. The pattern maps onto the old Gallo-Roman and Merovingian zones of early Christianisation, where bishops had the most power and the longest time to stamp their patron saints onto village identities.

Hyphens as history

38% of French communes have at least one hyphen in their name. The hyphen is the tell of a municipal merger — two or three villages brought together administratively, often during the Revolutionary period or the 1970s fusion reforms, each insisting on keeping its own name. The result: 1,991 communes containing -sur- (sitting beside a river), 974 with -en- (set within a region), 341 with -et- (joined to another).

The distribution peaks at seven and eight characters — Béziers, Orléans, Bordeaux, Avignon. But the long right tail stretches to 45, populated by merger products where nobody wanted to be the village that disappeared. The average commune name is 11.8 characters. Y pulls it down by exactly one letter.

Every name in this dataset is an administrative decision — about saints, rivers, landscapes, and the politics of not being forgotten. France has 35,014 of them, and almost all of them are, in their way, arguing for something.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories), supplemented with commune boundary polygons from the Contours Administratifs 2025 file. The commune list used here contains 35,014 entries after geometry processing. Name analysis performed on the full set; no names were excluded or normalised.
