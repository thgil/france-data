A commune called Y

35,014 commune names — shortest, longest, most common, and the peculiar saint at the top.

2026-05-26 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, 89 people live in a commune with the shortest possible name: Y. One letter. No abbreviation, no article — just the letter Y, officially. It has a mayor, a church, and a postcode (80190). The post office has had to deal with it for centuries.

Y is not an anomaly. France has 35,014 communes with official names, and the names are, collectively, a small linguistic universe — ranging from one-letter Y to the 45-character monster in the Marne. Between those extremes, a set of patterns repeats stubbornly: the saints, the articles, the rivers, the hills.

The alphabet's edge

After Y, the shortest commune names are two letters long — and there are a dozen of them. Most are in the north and east of France, relics of pre-Roman and Frankish place-name roots that never acquired a suffix. Sy (Ardennes, 54 people). By (Doubs, 86). Gy (Haute-Saône). Eu (Seine-Maritime, 7,800 — the largest two-letter commune by population). Oz (Isère, a ski resort at 1,500 m). Oô (Haute-Garonne, 103 people — and yes, two ôs).

The saint at the top

3,882 communes — 11.1% of all of France — carry the name of a saint. The most common: Saint-Martin, with 202 communes named after the fourth-century bishop of Tours. He donated half his cloak to a beggar, became the patron saint of France, and now has more communes named after him than any other figure in the country. Saint-Jean follows with 155, Saint-Pierre with 144.

The saints are not evenly distributed. Saint-Martin communes cluster in Normandy, Bretagne, and the Loire; Saint-Jean is more southern; Saint-Pierre appears wherever there was a Romanesque church with a patron. The name is a fossilised record of parish geography — each commune was once a parish, and each parish had a patron.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 different communes across 11 départements. Then Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), and Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each). In total, 10.6% of communes share their exact name with at least one other commune — a persistent headache for postal services, GPS systems, and anyone trying to book a holiday cottage without specifying the département.

The duplicates problem was supposed to be solved in 1790, when the Constituent Assembly created the département system and asked each commune to adopt a unique name. Many declined. A few merged their names to distinguish themselves — hence the long hyphenated compounds. Most simply didn't bother, which is why the post office still requires a département code on every envelope.

The grammar of place-names

Beyond saints and articles, a handful of templates account for a large share of the 35,014 names.

The -sur- formula — Saint-Rémy-sur-Durolle, Bar-sur-Seine, Vaux-sur-Mer — names the commune by its river or water feature. There are 1,991 of them. The -en- form (974 communes) does the same for enclosed territory: Abbeville-en-Dombes, Saint-Nicolas-en-Forêt. Together these two prepositions appear in nearly 3,000 commune names — about 8.5% of all communes in France.

Distribution of name lengths

The median commune name is 11 characters long. Most names cluster between 7 and 16 characters — the range of a simple three-word compound. The right tail stretches into territory that strains letterheads and form fields alike.

The longest name

The longest commune name in France — at 45 characters — is:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · population 491 · formed by the merger of three communes in 1972

It was created when three villages merged: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. Rather than choose a new name or abandon the old ones, the maire at the time did the obvious thing: hyphenate all three. The result is a name that takes a full breath to say aloud and occupies more than half a line of text on a standard envelope. In second place: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), formed by the same logic.

Y and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson are both official. Both have mayors. Both appear in the same INSEE commune register, sorted alphabetically, one near the end, one at the very top of the Ys. The gap between them is 44 characters and about 900 years of naming conventions.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are derived from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (DS-APL), which covers all 35,014 French communes and overseas territories with 2021 population estimates. Centroids were computed as the simple average of polygon vertices from the Contours administratifs 2025 (1000m simplification). The analysis covers names as given in the INSEE commune register as of that vintage; a small number of communes may have been renamed or merged since. The compiled index is available as communes-index.json (3 MB, 35,014 entries).
