A country of 35,000 names

From Y (population 89) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — every commune name in France, analysed.

2026-06-05 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — far more than any other country in the European Union. Each one has a name. Some are stately: Versailles, Bordeaux, Lyon. Some are poetic: Beaulieu, Beaumont, Fleury. Some are just a letter.

89.4% of communes have a name that belongs to them alone. The rest share. And when you share a name with eleven other villages scattered from Normandy to the Pyrénées, the question of identity becomes interesting.

The saint problem

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. That's one in nine communes named after a Catholic saint. They come in 3,392 distinct forms, which means the church ran out of saints and started adding geography to tell them apart: Saint-Aubin-de-Caubel, Saint-Aubin-en-Bray, Saint-Aubin-sur-Mer. Saint-Aubin alone appears in ten communes.

Ardèche leads the country with 29% of its communes named after saints — 97 out of 335. The department sits in the Rhône valley heartland of early French Catholicism, and the naming shows. Dordogne (24%) and Gironde (23%) follow.

On the density of saints 3,898 saint-named communes cover every department in France. The most duplicated saint name is Saint-Sauveur (11 copies), Saint-Aubin (10), and Saint-Marcel (9). The church's administrative reach from the 10th to 17th century is still visible in the commune map today.

The single-letter commune

In the Somme, east of Amiens, there is a commune called Y. That's it. One letter. Population 89. The name has been there since at least the 12th century — derived, probably, from the Old Norse ey (island) or a Picard dialect term for water. Whatever its origin, Y has the shortest commune name in France, and probably in the world.

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is perhaps the most well-known — a medieval port town, site of William the Conqueror's marriage. Oz (Isère, pop. 213) sits in the Alps at 1,500 metres. Oô (Haute-Garonne, pop. 103) is high in the Pyrénées. Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales, pop. 348) shares its name with the ancient Sumerian city.

The merger machines

The longest commune name — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters — is a merger artifact. France's communes nouvelles programme (accelerated after 2015) encouraged small communes to merge for administrative efficiency. When they merged, many kept both names, hyphenated. The result is a layer of very long names that didn't exist 20 years ago.

Of the top 10 longest names, most follow this pattern: two or three old village names stitched together with hyphens and conjunctions, preserving the identity of each absorbed community. The hyphen is France's concession to local memory.

A note on the data. Commune names and approximate centroids were extracted from the APL medical access dataset (DREES, 2023), which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Name analysis was performed on the full dataset. Centroid positions are polygon-centroid approximations from the 1000m-simplified administrative boundary file.
