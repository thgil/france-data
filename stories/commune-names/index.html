What's in a name

One commune is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. One in nine bears a saint's name. France's 35,000 commune names are a history book in hyphenated form.

2026-06-01 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Each one has a name — a single, official, legally registered name. The range of what constitutes an acceptable French commune name spans from a single letter to a 45-character compound of two saints, three villages, a preposition, and a conjunction.

Y (officially: Y, full stop) sits in the Somme valley, northeast of Amiens. It has been called Y since at least the 11th century — the name may derive from the Old French word for water, or from a pre-Roman root. Postal address: Y, 80830. If you want to write to someone there, you write a single letter. The opposite end of the spectrum is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne, a fusion commune created in the 1970s to join three villages under one hyphenated roof. Its name has outlasted the merger's original rationale.

There are 13 other communes with names of just two letters: Bû (Eure-et-Loire, pop. 2,022), Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591), Gy (Haute-Saône, pop. 1,001), and ten others.

The typical commune name is 10 characters

Across all 35,014 communes, the median name length is 10 characters — about the length of Versailles or Montpellier. The mean is 11.8 characters. The distribution is right-skewed: a few very long names pull the average up, while the bulk of communes cluster between 6 and 15 characters.

That "39+" bucket — 492 communes with names longer than 38 characters — represents a specific French administrative tradition: the commune nouvelle. When neighbouring villages merge, they often hyphenate their names rather than choose one. The record-holder, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, is the product of this logic taken to its logical extreme. Four more top the 40-character mark: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41), Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne (40), and La Vacquerie-et-Saint-Martin-de-Castries (40).

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

If you tell a French person you are from Sainte-Colombe, they will ask: laquelle? Which one? There are twelve — in Gironde, Haute-Garonne, Aveyron, and nine other départements spread across the country. Saint-Aubin: ten. Saint-Sauveur: eleven. This is not a bug. It is a consequence of every medieval parish naming itself after the same limited canon of Catholic saints, independently, centuries apart, without any central coordination and with no reason to think anyone else was doing the same thing.

The duplicate names are almost all saints or generic descriptive words — Beaulieu (10 communes: "beautiful place"), Le Pin (9 communes: "the pine tree"), Saint-Georges (8), Beaumont (8), Verrières (8). The pattern is medieval Catholic + agricultural French. Nothing has been updated since the Revolution.

One in nine communes is saintly

Exactly 3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. France has been baptizing its map since the 5th century. The Church planted itself in village after village, named the parish, and the parish name became the commune name after the Revolution. The saints survived the revolution; the monks who named them didn't always.

The count is almost certainly an undercount. Many communes with saints' names have shed the Saint- prefix over the centuries, or use local variants: San in Corsica, Sant', Sain-. Add those and the saintly share climbs higher still.

France loves a hyphen

13,410 communes — 38.3% — include at least one hyphen in their official name. The French administrative tradition of hyphenating compound place names is deeply embedded: Saint-Jean-de-Luz, Aix-en-Provence, Boulogne-sur-Mer. The hyphen signals a compound without quite committing to a single fused word. It is the typographic equivalent of a Gallic shrug at the tension between local specificity and national naming convention.

The most hyphenated commune, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, has eight hyphens. That's one hyphen for every 61 residents.

The other 61.7% of communes — the ones without hyphens — include the simplest and most ancient names: Paris, Lyon, Nice, Rennes, Nantes, and, of course, Y.

The two-letter club

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. They are mostly found in the north and the Pyrenees — regions where pre-Latin or pre-Romance place names survived long enough to be absorbed into the administrative record. Several are in Seine-Maritime (Eu, Ry), suggesting an old Norman stratum. The Pyrénéen ones (Py, Ur, Uz) likely come from Basque or Gascon roots.

The 35,014 names were fixed, more or less, by the census of 1793 — France's first systematic attempt to inventory its own territory after the Revolution abolished the old parishes and replaced them with communes. Many names were already old then. Y was old. Sainte-Colombe was old. The saints were old. The hyphens came later, as villages merged, but the core naming logic is medieval. What changes is the population living inside each name. What doesn't change is the name itself.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. The source GeoJSON includes the commune's INSEE code, population (INSEE 2021 census base), and official commune name. Name-length counts, duplicate analysis, saint/hyphen tallies, and the charts above were all computed directly from this dataset. communes-index.json (2.1 MB) is a derived flat index of all 35,014 communes bundled with this story for reuse.
