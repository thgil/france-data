What France calls itself

35,014 commune names — one single letter, 3,898 saints, and one name shared by twelve different villages scattered across the whole country.

2026-06-04 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, between Amiens and Saint-Quentin, there is a commune with a population of 89 and an official name of exactly one letter.

Y.

Commune de Y, département de la Somme (80). Population: 89.

It is the shortest commune name in France — and almost certainly in Europe. The name is pre-Roman, possibly Gaulish, and means nothing in particular in modern French. It has been confusing postal workers for centuries.

Y is not alone. France has 13 communes with two-letter names: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, and Uz. Most are ancient topographical names — Gaulish river words, Old Norse traces, the odd pre-Celtic remnant that survived every administrative reform since the Revolution. Together, France has 35,014 communes. They carry 35,014 names, with almost as many stories behind them.

A nation of saints

The single most distinctive feature of French commune names is their saints. 3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — carry a saint's name. France doesn't just name its churches after saints; it names its geography after them.

The most popular is Saint-Martin with 202 communes. Martin of Tours (316–397) was the first bishop of Gaul, the patron of soldiers, beggars, and winemakers, and apparently the patron of commune founders too. He is followed by Saint-Jean (155 communes), Saint-Pierre (144), and Saint-Germain (108). In total there are 1,023 distinct Saint-X and Sainte-X variants across France — a complete hagiography embedded in the map.

The twelve Sainte-Colombes

If you filter France's 35,014 communes down to the single most common exact name, you land on Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them. Not twelve Saint-Martin variants with different second words — twelve communes named exactly Sainte-Colombe, full stop, spread across twelve different départements from Manche in the north to Hautes-Alpes in the south.

Colombe (Columba) was a 3rd-century virgin martyr from Sens. She has been popular enough to name villages in Brittany, Burgundy, Gascony, Normandy, and the Rhône valley. The twelve don't know each other. They share a saint and nothing else.

The monsters

At the other end of the spectrum, French commune names can run to 45 characters. The record holder is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a Marne commune that is officially the sum of three historic villages hyphenated together. This isn't a freak: France's longest names are almost all the result of 19th- and 20th-century fusions, when neighbouring communes were administratively merged and both (or all three) names were kept, joined by hyphens and ets.

    The hyphen is France's administrative glue. Of 35,014 communes, 8,545 have hyphenated names — more than one in four. They range from the simple (Aix-en-Provence) to the spectacular.

    Four kinds of name

    French commune names fall into roughly four structural patterns. Saint names dominate in certain bands of the country — particularly in the west, the centre-west, and the Midi. Plain single-word names cluster in the Alps, Alsace, and the older Celtic territories of Brittany. Hyphenated names mark historic merger zones. Article names (Le, La, Les) tend to describe geography — the rivers, the hills, the forests that the commune sits in.

    A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes) supplemented by the Contours administratifs 2025 file (35,014 entries after including communes with no APL score). Name analysis covers all 35,014 entries. Population figures are INSEE 2021 estimates as included in the APL dataset.
