What's in a name?

35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. Nearly one in eight has Saint somewhere in the name.

2026-05-08 5 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — an extraordinary number, more than the rest of the European Union combined. Each has an official name, registered in the INSEE commune code directory. Some names run to 45 characters. One runs to a single letter.

That letter is Y.

Y is a real commune in the Somme (département 80), with 89 residents and a mayor and a war memorial and everything a French commune has. Its official INSEE code is 80829. It is not an abbreviation. The full name is not hiding somewhere. The commune is simply called Y, and has been for centuries — derived, linguistically, from the Gallo-Roman root Ica, via Old French. The shortest commune name in France is also, in its way, the most French.

At the other extreme sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 45 characters in its name and roughly 200 residents. It's a product of France's long history of communes nouvelles — mergers of smaller villages that keep every original name, joined by hyphens. The result is a kind of administrative archaeological record: each hyphenated section is a place that once stood alone.

One in eight communes has a saint

France is a secular republic, but you would not know it from the commune directory. Across the 35,014 communes, 4,437 — nearly one in eight — include Saint or Sainte in their name. That is 12.7% of all French territory, sanctified nominally at least, by centuries of Catholic geography.

The Saint- prefix accounts for 4,056 of those. Sainte- adds another 359. Together they form the dominant pattern in French toponymy — more common than any geographical feature, river, colour, or tree.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The single most repeated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — shared by twelve different villages in twelve different départements. They have nothing in common except their dedication to the same third-century martyr. The largest, in the Rhône, has 1,953 residents. The smallest, in the Hautes-Alpes, has 59.

If you search for "Sainte-Colombe" in a French GPS, you will be offered twelve options. The correct one depends on which département you're in. This problem repeats itself for Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin (10), Beaulieu (10), Saint-Michel (9), and several dozen other names.

The grammar of place

French commune names are not random — they follow a consistent grammar that encodes geography. Prepositions carry the most information. A name with -sur- tells you the village sits on a river. -lès- (from the Latin juxta, "next to") means it neighbours a larger town. -en- places it within a historic region. -sous- puts it literally under a hill or château.

Relational prepositions in commune names

Nearly 2,000 communes use -sur-, tracing the river networks of France as faithfully as any hydrological map. The 1,007 -lès- communes cluster around major medieval towns — the name is a fossilised address, still pointing at a city that may have shrunk or merged since. The 257 -sous- communes are rarer and more poetic: Écurey-en-Verdunois is simply "under the fortified hill."

A country of 3,700 name collisions

Of France's 35,014 communes, 31,314 have a name that belongs to them alone. The remaining 3,700 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. This creates the postal and administrative puzzle that the five-digit INSEE code exists to solve: the code is unique; the name often isn't.

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The distribution is wide. More than a dozen names are just two characters — Oô, Uz, By, Eu, Ry — all real, all ancient, all shaped more by centuries of spoken contraction than by any committee. Y is simply the one that contracted furthest.

The commune system is a product of the Revolution — 1789 swept away parishes and seigneuries and replaced them with communes, roughly one per village. France had around 44,000 then; mergers have trimmed it to 35,014 now. Each commune kept its name. Many of those names are a thousand years old. The names are not decorative — they are documents, encoding rivers, saints, hills, and the long memory of who lived next to whom.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to the 2025 administrative boundary file — the same source used in the medical deserts story. 35,014 communes are represented, including overseas territories. Populations are from the 2021 INSEE census estimates embedded in the APL data.
