France has a town called Y

35,014 communes share just 32,764 names — and one of them is a single letter.

2026-05-30 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, 100 kilometres north of Paris, with a population of 89 people and an official name that takes up exactly one character on a road sign: Y. It is, as far as records go, the shortest place name in France — and one of the shortest in the world. The post office works fine. The mayor signs documents. The TripAdvisor page has reviews.

Y is not an anomaly. France has 13 more communes whose names are just two letters: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, and Uz. Some are ancient — Eu in Seine-Maritime traces its Latin roots to Augum, a Roman settlement. Others are the surviving syllables of names worn smooth by centuries of use. All of them confuse postal workers in other countries.

The identity crisis

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names. That means 3,700 communes — roughly one in ten — share their name with at least one other place. The record holder is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in twelve different départements, from Hautes-Alpes to Yonne, from Gironde to Seine-et-Marne. Each is its own village with its own mayor and its own problems. None of them are the same place. Getting mail addressed simply to "Sainte-Colombe, France" must be someone's recurring nightmare.

Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin ten. Beaulieu ten. The pattern is clear: these are the generic nouns of French geography — holy names, beautiful views, pleasant hills — applied independently by communities hundreds of miles apart over centuries of Christian settlement.

One in nine communes is a saint

Zoom out further and the pattern is overwhelming. 3,898 French communes — 11.1% of the total — have a name beginning with Saint or Sainte. That's the entire population of a medium-sized city's worth of villages, all named after Christian martyrs and holy figures, spread across every corner of the country. If you pick a commune at random in France, there's roughly a one-in-nine chance it's dedicated to a saint.

The effect is not evenly spread. Dordogne leads with 130 Saint-* communes — that's 38% of its 341 communes bearing a saint's name. Gironde (121) and Isère (106) follow. The Vendée and the Normandy départements are deep in sainthood too. Paris, predictably, has zero: the capital's arrondissements are numbered, not named after anyone's patron.

The longest name

At the other end of the spectrum from Y sits a commune in the Marne that stretches its name across 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

Marne (dept. 51) · population 317 · 45 characters

It was formed in 1972 by the merger of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — three villages that refused to let any of their names disappear. France has dozens of these hyphenated monuments to reluctant consolidation. The second-longest is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône (43 characters), another four-way merger. The third, Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados (41), merges only two villages but compensates with syllable count.

Naming a place is a political act. When France's post-war governments encouraged rural communes to merge and cut administrative costs, the resistors built their stubbornness into the hyphen. Some of those names have since been reversed — communes that merged in the 1970s are quietly separating again. Y, meanwhile, has been Y since the medieval period and shows no signs of elaborating.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL dataset (2023), which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. The APL dataset was originally assembled for the medical deserts story and is reused here for its commune-name field. Name counts and duplicate analysis were computed from the communeName field of the GeoJSON; population figures are from INSEE 2021. A small number of communes share codes across French administrative reorganisations (fusions, séparations) and may affect precise duplicate counts at the margin.
