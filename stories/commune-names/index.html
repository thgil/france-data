What's in a name?

France has 35,014 commune names — one is just the letter Y. One in ten starts with Saint. Twelve different places are all called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-29 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme with 89 residents, a mairie, a church, and a name that crashes most computer forms: Y. Just Y. One letter. A medieval contraction of Iaca, a Picard water-word, whittled down by centuries of oral use until there was nothing left to drop. It is France's shortest commune name and, arguably, its most useful in a game of trivia.

Y is an outlier — but not as extreme as it looks. France's 35,014 communes carry names ranging from 1 to 45 characters, with an average of 11.8. The distribution is highly skewed: the short names are ancient survivals, the long ones mostly 20th-century municipal mergers where the committee couldn't agree on which village to drop.

The length distribution

Most commune names cluster between 6 and 13 characters. The very short ones — single letters, two-letter survivals like By, Gy, Uz, Eu — are pre-Norman relics, often Celtic or Germanic roots compressed into sounds that resisted French orthography. The very long ones are a different phenomenon entirely: France's policy of fusions de communes encourages merging villages into single administrative units, and the merged entity typically hyphenates all the old names together.

How many communes have names this long?

The survivors: one and two letters

There are 14 communes with names of two characters or fewer. They are concentrated in Normandy, Picardy, and the Pyrenees — regions where pre-French phonology left residues that later standardisation couldn't quite erase. Y (Somme). Eu (Seine-Maritime), a Norman port town with a name older than France itself. Oô (Haute-Garonne), a hamlet of 200 near the Spanish border where the double-o is Gascon dialect. Typing any of these into an online form with a minimum-length validator is a minor act of rebellion.

The saint flood

11.1% of French communes — one in nine — begin with Saint or Sainte. That is 3,882 place names, each one carrying the name of a Christian saint from the medieval parish system. When a village was founded around a church, the church's patron became the village's name, and it stayed. The practice was so pervasive that the French Revolution briefly tried to abolish it — decree of 1792, largely ignored.

The most popular saint by a wide margin is Martin — as in Saint-Martin-de-…, Saint-Martin-sur-…, Saint-Martin-en-…. There are 202 communes with Saint-Martin in their name. Jean (152) and Pierre (128) follow. The top 15 saints account for over 1,000 commune names between them.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The saint system produced an immediate problem: the same saint was venerated in hundreds of places, and there was no central registry to prevent duplication. Result: France has 12 communes called Sainte-Colombe, in 12 different départements. Eleven called Saint-Sauveur. Ten called Saint-Aubin. The post system coped by appending qualifiers — Saint-Aubin-des-Bois, Saint-Aubin-le-Dépeint — but many bare duplicates survive. When the French railway system was being built in the 1840s, routing a train to "Saint-Pierre" required a disambiguation that would feel familiar to anyone who has ever tried to navigate an online address form in France today.

The hyphen as administrative policy

38.3% of French commune names contain at least one hyphen — 13,410 out of 35,014. France hyphenates place names by convention where English would use a space: Saint-Germain-en-Laye, not Saint Germain en Laye. But the real hyphen explosion is in the merger names. Since the loi Marcellin of 1971 and its later iterations, France has actively encouraged village mergers. When two or three communes merge, the new entity often joins their names with hyphens, producing constructions like Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, four villages, Haute-Saône). The longest of all is a Marne commune in Champagne.

Longest commune names (top 10)

The record-holder — 45 characters, no spaces — is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune of 491 people in the Marne. It holds three former village names, two saints, a geographic qualifier (en-Bouzemont), and an et that was probably added to avoid any appearance of Saint-Remy annexing Saint-Genest. The commune's post office has presumably adapted.

The 45-character name is an extreme, but the pattern is everywhere. France has 341 communes whose names contain -et-, almost all of them mergers where the hyphen-and-connector formula was used to signal equality rather than absorption. A small act of diplomacy, encoded in geography.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers 35,014 communes (including arrondissements of Paris, Lyon, and Marseille treated as communes). Name statistics were computed from this dataset in May 2026. The communes-index.json file is 3 MB.
