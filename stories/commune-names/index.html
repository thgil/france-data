What's in a name?

France has 35,014 commune names — one is a single letter. 3,891 start with "Saint." And 12 different places are called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-13 4 min read

France keeps an official register of every town, village, and hamlet with a legal identity. There are 35,014 of them. Their names run from the 45-character behemoth of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson down to a commune in the Somme whose entire official name, as registered with INSEE and the postal service, is a single letter.

Y

Commune of the Somme (80190). Population: 89. The name is believed to derive from a Gaulish word for yew tree. You can mail a letter to "Y, 80190, Somme, France" and it will arrive.

Y is not alone in brevity. France has 13 two-letter communes — Sy (Ardennes, pop. 54), By (Doubs, pop. 86), Bû (Eure-et-Loir, pop. 2,022), Oô (Haute-Garonne, pop. 103), Oz (Isère, pop. 213), Py and Ur (both Pyrénées-Orientales), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, pop. 36), and a handful more. At the other end of the spectrum, Bû is almost aggressively French: a circumflex on a two-letter word.

The confusion problem

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 carry a truly unique name. The remaining 2,250 names are each used by two or more places. Sainte-Colombe tops the list with twelve: one each in Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. If you Google "Sainte-Colombe, France" without a département, you're on your own.

The pattern is almost entirely driven by saints. Of the top 15 most-duplicated names, 13 start with Saint or Sainte. The exceptions — Beaulieu (10 communes) and Le Pin (9) — are generic landscape descriptions with the same wide-distribution logic: a "beautiful place" or a "pine grove" could be anywhere in France, and often is.

The saint epidemic

3,891 French communes start with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of the total. The French Republic declared itself officially secular in 1905. Its address book did not get the memo.

The saint density varies sharply by région. In the Massif Central and the Atlantic southwest, more than a quarter of all communes carry a saint's name. Ardèche leads nationally: 97 of its 335 communes start with Saint, a rate of 29%. Creuse (28.2%) and Loire (27.8%) follow. These are rural, historically Catholic, slowly depopulating territories where the village name was set by the parish church centuries ago and never revised.

The single most common Saint variant: Saint-Sauveur (11 communes). Saint-Aubin and Saint-Marcel tie at 10 each. Saint Colombe, apparently, was popular enough to name 12 parishes in quite different corners of France — the Jura, the Gironde, the Manche — and all of them are still here, legally distinct, all called Sainte-Colombe.

The long and the short of it

The longest commune name in France has 45 characters: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne. It is itself a product of municipal fusion — three villages merged in 1973, each insisting on retaining its name in the compound. The result is a commune that takes up nearly a full line of a standard address label. Second place goes to Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône, at 43 characters — also a merger. The pattern holds across the top ten: nearly all long names are hyphenated compounds, and most are saints.

Shortest names

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. Most names cluster between 6 and 20 characters — enough for a saint's name and a topographic qualifier (-sur-Loire, -en-Vexin, -les-Bains). The very short names are almost always relics: single syllables from pre-Latin languages, stripped down by centuries of oral use until only a consonant cluster remained. Ry in the Seine-Maritime may have inspired Flaubert — it's the village most commonly cited as the real-life model for Yonville in Madame Bovary. Its name is two letters.

France's 35,014 commune names are an accidental archive — of saints venerated in medieval parishes, of rivers and hills described in Gaulish or Latin, of mergers that nobody wanted, and of 89 people who have lived their whole lives in a place called Y. The name on the map is never just a name.
