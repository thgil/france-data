What's in a name?

35,014 communes — from Y (one letter, 89 residents) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters, 491 residents). The complete naming of France.

2026-05-27 5 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. Not Yé, not an abbreviation, not a mistake on a census form. Just Y. It sits in the Somme, département 80, at latitude 49.8°N. Population: 89 people. It has a mayor, a postal code, and an INSEE commune code. It has been called Y since at least the 11th century — the name derives from an old Picard word for water, ige, which the landscape apparently warranted.

France has 35,014 communes. Each one has a name. They range from one letter to 45. The average — if you average commune names — is 14 characters. But that average hides a distribution that skews toward saints, hyphenation, and a medieval naming logic that is still, officially, the law of the land.

The two-letter club

Below Y, there are 13 communes with two-character names. They are all short for different reasons — some are ancient Celtic roots, some Occitan, some plain Norman compression. Oô (Haute-Garonne) carries a circumflex on the second letter, making it arguably the most typographically demanding commune in France per capita. Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591) is the biggest of the group. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, population 36) is the smallest.

The church named the country

The single most striking pattern in French commune names is the dominance of the saints. Christianity didn't just build cathedrals in France — it named the landscape.

The saints are not evenly distributed. The most popular: Saint-Aubin (10 communes), Saint-Marcel (9), Saint-Michel (9), Saint-Pierre (9). Saint-Sauveur — the Holy Saviour — gives 11 communes the same name. And then there is Sainte-Colombe, a fifth-century martyr from Sens: 12 communes bear her name, making her the single most duplicated commune name in France.

There are 12 Sainte-Colombes

If you search for Sainte-Colombe in France, you will find yourself in trouble. There are twelve of them — in the Ain, Ardèche, Corrèze, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Maine-et-Loire, Puy-de-Dôme, Rhône, Saône-et-Loire, Seine-Maritime, Val-d'Oise, and Yonne. Each has its own mayor, its own postcode, its own administrative identity. They share nothing except a name and a shared devotion to an obscure Burgundian saint who was beheaded in 274 CE.

This is the deeper logic of French commune names. France has 35,014 communes, but only 32,764 unique names — meaning 2,250 names are shared by two or more places. The top of the list is dominated by saints, but also by descriptive landscape terms: Beaulieu (10 communes — beautiful place), Beaumont (8 — beautiful hill), Le Pin (9 — the pine tree).

Eight words, 491 residents

At the other end of the scale from Y is a commune in the Marne with 491 residents and 45 characters in its name.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-
Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters · 8 words · Marne (51) · Population 491

The name is a compound of three medieval localities — Saint-Remy, Saint-Genest, and Isson — fused when the communes merged, as hundreds of French communes have done since the 1970s Marcellin reforms. Each merger appended a name rather than dropping one, which is why Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados) are now on the map. The French state allowed places to fuse without asking them to forget where they came from.

The distribution

Most commune names are between 6 and 9 characters long — Beaumont (8), Villiers (8), Fontaine (8). The distribution is right-skewed: there's a clean peak at 7–8 characters, then a long tail of multi-word compound names that stretches all the way to 45. The long tail is almost entirely made of merged communes — places that kept both names and added a hyphen.

The 38.3% of commune names that contain a hyphen are almost all products of the same logic: a saint's name, a landscape term, a preposition, another noun. Saint-Clément-de-la-Place. Fontaine-sur-Avre. Villeneuve-lès-Maguelone. The hyphen is a grammatical decision that France made once and never revisited. It is a filing system for a country with 35,000 addresses and a strong opinion about history.

What the names say

Y is 89 people and one letter. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is 491 people and 45 characters. Between them, 35,012 other communes spell out a thousand years of French geography, Christianity, feudal administration, and municipal stubbornness. The names were given by monks and lords and cartographers and prefects. They persist because French communes are remarkably resistant to being told to change.

There are 12 Sainte-Colombes. Each one insists it is the only one.
