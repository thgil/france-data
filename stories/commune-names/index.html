What France calls its villages

35,014 communes. One called Y. 3,882 named after saints. One with 45 characters. The full census of French place names.

2026-06-07 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes, from the tiniest hamlet in the Pyrenees to the arrondissements of Paris. Each one has an official name registered in the code officiel géographique. And somewhere in the Somme, département 80, with 89 residents and a perfectly straight-faced INSEE code, sits a commune called Y.

That's it. One letter. No suffix, no article, no saint. Just Y. It is the shortest official place name in France, possibly in Europe — and it has been recorded in administrative documents since the Middle Ages.

At the other end of the spectrum: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, Marne, 491 residents, 45 characters. France has an official preference for hyphenated names that reference two saints, a place-within-a-place, and a medieval fief, all at once.

A saint for every occasion

France is a republic with a deeply Catholic address book. Of 35,014 communes, 3,882 — 11.1% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. Add in the saints embedded mid-name (Saint-Julien-en-Vercors, Besse-et-Saint-Anastaise, and hundreds more) and the share climbs further.

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times. It isn't the same place named twice by mistake — it's 12 genuinely separate villages, spread across 12 different départements, each independently named after Saint Columba at some point in the last millennium. They share a name the way France shares a flag: with complete indifference to the possibility of confusion.

Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin, 10. The pattern holds: medieval Christianity produced a naming system with a limited vocabulary and no namespace management. France has lived with the collisions ever since.

The hyphen as national institution

38.3% of French commune names contain a hyphen — 13,410 out of 35,014. This is a structural feature of French administrative naming, not a quirk. When two villages merged, when a saint's name needed a geographic qualifier, when the commune des eaux voulait se distinguer — a hyphen was added. Then another. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson has six.

Articles add another layer: 1,054 communes start with La, 758 with Le, 328 with Les. And 835 names include an apostrophe — L'Abergement-Clémenciat, L'Île-Rousse — where the article fuses with a vowel in the old way. The system is consistent in its inconsistency, which is itself very French.

The shape of a name

The average commune name is 11.8 characters. The distribution isn't symmetric: short names are genuinely rare (only 14 names have 3 characters or fewer), while the long tail extends all the way to 45. Most names cluster between 6 and 20 characters, with a peak around 7–8 — short enough to fit on a road sign, long enough to carry meaning.

89.4% original

Despite the saint-collision problem, 31,314 communes — 89.4% — have a name that appears nowhere else in France. Every Villemontoire, every Sougé-le-Ganelon, every Bussac-Forêt is one of a kind. The postal service depends on this. So does the IGN. So does the traveller trying to explain which Saint-Martin they mean.

The 10.6% that share a name with at least one other commune are spread across 1,450 names. Sainte-Colombe (12 copies) is the most extreme case, but most shared names appear just twice — two villages that made the same choice a thousand years apart and have been generating administrative headaches ever since.

France has mapped, numbered, and named every one of its 35,014 communes. The naming system is partly medieval Christianity, partly revolutionary bureaucracy, partly centuries of hyphenated mergers. Y has been Y for longer than most nation-states have existed. And Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson has been waiting patiently for someone to count its characters.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes are drawn from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which uses the INSEE code officiel géographique (COG) as its commune identifier. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte). Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base included in the APL dataset. Name-length analysis and frequency counts are computed from the communeName field in the APL GeoJSON. The communes-index.json data file produced for this story — 35,014 entries with name, INSEE code, département, population, and centroid coordinates — is available in the story's source folder.
