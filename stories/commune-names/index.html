What's in a name?

35,014 commune names — from the one-letter village of Y to a 45-character merger that contains two saints and a river.

2026-05-31 5 min read
Y Population 89 · Somme (80) · Commune code 80829

France has a commune called Y. One letter. Not an abbreviation, not a nickname — the official INSEE name, the one on the road signs, the one in the administrative record. 89 residents live there. The Somme département. The postman knows where to go.

France's 35,014 communes carry names that range from this single vowel to elaborate hyphenated assemblies of villages, rivers, and saints. The list, taken from the 2023 DREES medical-access dataset, is a complete census of the national naming register. Analysed as text, it tells its own kind of story about French history, geography, and administrative imagination.

The shortest names

Y has company at the low end. Thirteen more communes carry names of just two letters: Oô in the Haute-Garonne (a Pyrenean village of 103), Eu in Seine-Maritime (6,591 residents, the largest of the tiny-named), Gy in Haute-Saône. Each one sounds like something a crossword compiler invented. None of them did — these names trace back to Gaulish, Norse, or Frankish roots worn smooth by a millennium of usage.

The saint machine

At the other extreme of the frequency distribution sits the word saint, which appears in 4,090 commune names — a figure roughly equal to all the pharmacies in France, or about one commune in nine. When you add sainte (360 further occurrences), the full hagiographic tally reaches 4,450 name tokens. France has been naming communes after saints since the early Middle Ages and has never really stopped.

The most common single word after saint is sur — as in sur-Seine, sur-Loire, sur-Mer. Rivers are the second great organising principle of French place-names, prepositions included. After that: martin (255 communes), pierre (180), chapelle (178), jean (170).

France's doppelgänger communes

Of the 35,014 commune names, 32,764 are distinct — which means 2,250 names appear more than once, accounting for 3,700 communes that share their name with at least one other. If you mail a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France" without a département or postcode, it could arrive at any of twelve different villages. Sainte-Colombe in Yonne (pop 185). Sainte-Colombe in Rhône (pop 1,953). Sainte-Colombe in Gironde (pop 426). Twelve communes, twelve différent départements, all strangers to each other.

"Sainte-Colombe" exists 12 times in France. The postman needs the département code.

Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin 10 times. Beaulieu — meaning simply "beautiful place" — is shared by 10 communes, none of which would claim the others are as beautiful. Saint-Michel, Saint-Pierre, Sainte-Marie: each name carries its own minor galaxy of villages.

The longest name in France

At the opposite end of Y sits a commune in the Marne (département 51) with a name that stretches 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

Marne (51) · Population 491 · 45 characters · The result of a mid-20th century administrative merger.

The name is what it looks like: two saint-named villages welded together when France consolidated its commune map. Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont merged with Saint-Genest-et-Isson sometime in the postwar era, producing a name that takes longer to say than to drive through. The commune has 491 residents and an APL score of 4.09 — well-served for healthcare, at least.

The second-longest is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), followed by Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados). All three are products of the same administrative logic: when villages merge, their names merge too. The result is France's longest administrative compound nouns, each one a small historical record of the merger that produced it.

A census of words

Taken together, the 35,014 commune names contain 101,863 word tokens. 4,090 are saint. 2,007 are sur. The most common letter is, predictably, e. The rarest commune name is a single letter. The longest is a minor epic of saints and rivers. In between, France's administrative geography reads like a poem in a language assembled from a thousand years of migration, conquest, and stubbornness — each name a small record of the people who stayed.

A note on the data. Commune names were extracted from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 34,886 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories at the time of its publication. The APL file was joined with administrative contours (Etalab/data.gouv.fr, 2025 vintage) to produce the 35,014-commune GeoJSON used across this site. Name analysis was performed in Node.js using the communeName field from that file.
