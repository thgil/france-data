What's in a French place name?

35,014 communes, one called Y, twelve called Sainte-Colombe. The patterns in French place names compress a thousand years of history into a few characters.

2026-06-04 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y.

That's the complete name — one letter, in the Somme département, 89 residents. It defeats search engines, confuses GPS units, and has been confusing cartographers since the Middle Ages. The name probably derives from a Picard word for a body of water, but the documentation isn't great, possibly because it's hard to file anything under a single vowel.

Thirteen more communes manage just two characters: Bû (Eure-et-Loir, 2,022 residents, the most populous of the micro-named), Eu (Seine-Maritime, 6,591), Gy (Haute-Saône, 1,001), and ten others scattered across the country. Together they account for 14 communes whose full address fits in a text message with room to spare.

The saint factory

At the other end of the cultural spectrum: 3,882 communes whose names begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That's 11.1% of all French communes — roughly one in nine — named in honour of a Catholic saint. It's the single dominant naming pattern in the country, a legacy of medieval parish organisation where the local church and the village were the same thing.

The most common French commune name is Sainte-Colombe: twelve different villages share it, spread from Gironde to Savoie, none of them particularly famous, all of them presumably devoted to a 3rd-century martyr from Roman Gaul. Saint-Sauveur comes second (11), followed by Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each).

Dordogne leads all départements with 130 saint-named communes — roughly one for every 43 km² of territory. Gironde (121), Isère (106), Saône-et-Loire (105), and Charente-Maritime (101) round out the top five. These are all historically Catholic, rural départements where the parish network ran deep and administrative reforms in the 19th century mostly left the names alone.

The twenty most-repeated names

Of 35,014 communes, 32,764 have names that appear nowhere else in France. The remaining 2,250 share their name with at least one other commune. Most repetitions are modest — seven or eight — but a handful of names recur everywhere. The chart below shows the twenty most-repeated.

The hyphen nation

French commune names love a hyphen. 38% of all communes — 13,410 of them — have at least one. The pattern comes from mergers: the 1971 loi Marcellin encouraged neighbouring villages to combine, and many chose to hyphenate both names rather than pick one winner. The result is a long tail of compound names that reads like a diplomatic compromise.

The longest name in France belongs to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 491 residents and a 45-character name. It contains two saint names, a river reference, and a conjunction. The second-longest, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), wasn't content with one saint either.

How long is a French commune name?

The median name is 10 characters. Most fall in the 6–9 range — short enough to be practical, long enough to be distinctive. But the distribution has a long right tail, and a visible secondary bump around 15–22 characters from the hyphenated merger names. The leftmost bar, at 1 character, is a population of exactly one.
