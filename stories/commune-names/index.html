What's in a commune name?

35,014 communes, 32,764 distinct names — and 202 places called Saint-Martin.

2026-05-16 5 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union — 35,014 of them, compared to 8,000 in Germany and 8,100 in Spain. Most were fixed into law during the Revolution in 1790, when every parish became a commune. Their names were already centuries old. What patterns are baked into 35,014 names that predate the Republic?

The republic of saints

One in every eight French communes carries a saint's name — 4,443 out of 35,014. The pattern is older than France itself. When Merovingian missionaries converted Gaul, they planted dedications to saints in every parish. The Revolution renamed a few hundred to Libre, Montagne, Fraternité, but most slipped quietly back within a decade. The saints stayed.

Not all saints are equally popular. Saint-Martin, bishop of Tours in the 4th century, was the most-celebrated saint of medieval France — patron of soldiers, vintners, and the poor. He shows up 202 times. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow. The top 15 saint patterns together account for 1,401 communes.

The most common names

If we look at exact full names — not just saint prefixes — the rankings shift. Sainte-Colombe leads with 12 communes, all in different parts of France. Saint-Sauveur has 11, Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each have 10. The 2,250 "duplicate" names are spread across all 96 metropolitan départements — France has no obvious system for disambiguation beyond adding a suffix like -sur-Mer or -lès-Paris.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter: Y — a village of 89 residents in the Somme, département 80. Its name predates French itself; the Y is thought to derive from a Germanic or Old Norse word for a bend in a river. Thirteen other communes manage two letters: Gy, Eu, Us, Bû. These shortest names cluster in the north, in Normandy and the historic Frankish heartland.

The longest is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, in the Marne (dept 51), population 491. It earned its hyphenated bulk through a series of 19th-century commune mergers. Several runner-ups push past 40 characters, each a hyphenated concatenation of villages that were joined administratively but refused to drop their separate identities from the official name.

The geography of saints

Saint names are not evenly distributed across France. The south-west and the Massif Central are most "saintified" — Creuse leads at 30.6%, with nearly a third of its 255 communes carrying a saint's name. Ardèche (29.3%) and Dordogne (28.8%) follow. These are the old Celtic and Gallo-Roman territories where early Christianity took hold deeply and whose commune borders were drawn around medieval parishes with unusually strong patron-saint identities.

By contrast, the départements with dense urban grids — particularly in the north and east, colonised by Frankish, then Flemish and Alsatian naming conventions — have far lower saint proportions. Bas-Rhin and Haut-Rhin average below 5%.

Prepositions as geography

After saints, the two most productive naming particles are -sur- (on / beside) and -lès- (near). There are 1,991 communes named "X-sur-Y" — something beside a river, a lake, or a hilltop — from Montmartre-sur-Saulx to Bagnères-de-Bigorre-sur-Adour. The 1,034 communes using -lès- (an archaic form of les, still meaning "near") often reference a larger neighbour: Neuilly-lès-Dijon, Villers-lès-Nancy. The particle is a medieval fossil — lès dropped from everyday French before the Renaissance — but commune names are conservative things. Once they're set, they rarely change.
