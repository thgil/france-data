What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes and only 32,764 distinct names. One of them is just the letter Y.

2026-05-24 5 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. Not an abbreviation. Not a village nicknamed after a letter. Just Y — one character, one letter, the 25th in the Latin alphabet. It sits in the Somme, département 80, about 100 kilometres north of Paris. Its official INSEE code is 80829. It has 89 residents, a mayor, and presumably a sign at the edge of town that reads simply: Y.

Y is the shortest commune name in France. But it's not alone in being remarkable. France's 35,014 communes carry names that span a single letter to 45 characters, a millennium of naming history compressed into a searchable database. The patterns that emerge from that database are, by turns, theological, geographic, bureaucratic, and occasionally absurd.

A country of saints

The dominant fact about French commune names is also the most obvious one: the church named everything. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,891 — just over 11% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. Every medieval parish was named after its patron saint. The Reformation changed the theology but not the map. The Revolution tried to rename hundreds of communes (Saint-Étienne briefly became Armeville; Saint-Malo became Port-Malo), but most reverted within a decade.

After Saint- comes the geography of the medieval landscape: La Chapelle- (147 communes), Villers- (103), Villeneuve- (64), Pont- (61), Fontaine- (55). These aren't just repeated names — they're repeated types of place. Every town near a bridge, every village beside a fountain, every hamlet built around a chapel got the same prefix and then a local qualifier to distinguish it from its neighbours.

The same name, twelve times

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names. That gap — 2,250 — represents names reused across the country. In all, 1,450 names appear in more than one commune, covering 3,700 communes total. Every one of those municipalities co-exists with a namesake somewhere else in France, sharing its name but not its postcode, mayor, or département.

The most reused name is Sainte-Colombe — twelve different villages, spread from Normandy to Provence to the Pyrenees. None of them are famous. None are large (the biggest has under 2,000 residents). They are simply twelve medieval hamlets that each decided their patron was Sainte Colombe of Sens, a 3rd-century Roman martyr, and filed the paperwork accordingly.

From one letter to forty-five

Below Y on the length ladder, thirteen communes share the two-character tier: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Many of these short names come from Norman or pre-Latin origins — the Norsemen who settled Normandy in the 10th century brought monosyllabic place-name conventions that the French administrative system preserved without question.

At the other extreme: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters, in the Marne (département 51), population 491. Long names like this almost always result from fusions de communes — administrative mergers that kept every village's name hyphenated rather than choosing one. The result is a bureaucratic archaeology: you can read the merger history directly off the sign.

A millennium of paperwork

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. Most names fall in the 6–15 character range — long enough to be specific, short enough to fit on a sign. But the distribution has a long right tail, stretched out by the merged communes that refused to lose any syllables.

What the data shows, when you look at it all together, is that France's commune names are a sediment layer of every administrative era the country has passed through. Latin church names from the 4th century. Norse monosyllables from the 10th. Double-hyphenated fusions from the 21st. And one commune in the Somme — population 89, postcode 80190 — that somehow ended up being just the letter Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the APL communes dataset (DREES, 2023), which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Population figures are from the same dataset (INSEE 2021 base). The communes-index.json file generated for this story is 3 MB and contains the name, INSEE code, département, population, and centroid coordinates for every commune.
