France's name problem

35,014 communes. Only 32,764 distinct names. And one commune called Y.

2026-05-17 5 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — its smallest unit of local government, from Alpine hamlets of a dozen people to cities of a million. But give those 35,014 communes a name and something awkward happens: you run out of ideas. Of all commune names in use today, only 32,764 are distinct. The other 3,700 communes share their name with at least one neighbour, somewhere else in the country.

That's not a clerical error. It's the natural result of naming places after the same saints, the same rivers, the same landscapes — for a thousand years, in thousands of different villages that had no reason to know about each other.

Sainte-Colombe wins

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — a name borne by twelve separate communes, spread across twelve different départements from Haute-Garonne to Yonne. Saint-Sauveur comes second with eleven. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear ten times. The top twenty is almost entirely saints and landscape words. France named its villages the same way, everywhere.

The saint problem

France has 3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of all communes. That's an enormous saint-load. No other country has outsourced this much of its toponymy to the Catholic calendar.

The collision problem isn't hypothetical. There are twelve communes called Sainte-Colombe. Before the age of national administration, this was fine — your Sainte-Colombe was the only one that mattered to you. But modern prefectures, post offices, and GPS coordinates require a decision. France's solution was elegant: add a preposition and a landscape feature.

Saint-Martin becomes Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-sur-Armançon, Saint-Martin-en-Gâtinois — the same saint, disambiguated by geography.

Today, 7,372 communes — more than one in five — carry a preposition connector in their name (-de-, -sur-, -en-, -lès-, -sous-) as a disambiguation suffix. The saint stays; the river or forest does the distinguishing work.

When communes merge but don't want to

The longest commune name in France — 45 characters — is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne (pop. 491). It reads less like a place name and more like a legal document. Which it is: it's what happens when multiple historic communes merge administratively but each insists on keeping its identity in the name.

Y

On the other end of the spectrum: Y. One letter. A commune in the Somme, département 80, population 89. It is the shortest place name in France and, by most accounts, one of the shortest in the world. Its origins are debated — possibly from Old Picard or a water-word — but its peculiarity is not. Y has a mayor, a post office, and a Wikipedia article that begins, with admirable directness: "Y is a commune."

Beyond Y, France has thirteen more communes with two-letter names: Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591), Ur and Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées), Oz (Isère) — mostly short Pyrenean or Basque-influenced words that predate the French language's vowel-heavy norms.

32,764 names for 35,014 places

Every French commune has an official INSEE code — a five-digit number that uniquely identifies it regardless of what it's called. In practice, the name is what matters: it goes on the sign at the village entrance, on the letterhead, on the birth certificate. So France maintains a system where twelve places are officially called the same thing, and one place is officially called Y, and another is officially called Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. The registry handles the collisions. The names carry the history.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the APL dataset (DREES 2023) joined to Etalab's commune contours, as prepared for Story 2. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Population figures are INSEE 2021 estimates. Short-name communes in overseas territories may not appear if their geometry produced no centroid.
