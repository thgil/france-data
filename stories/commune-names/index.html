A town called Y

35,014 commune names, from a single letter to 45 characters wide — and 3,882 of them dedicated to a saint.

2026-05-20 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, 120 km north-east of Paris, there is a commune called Y. That is its entire name. One letter. Population 89. The commune of Y has existed since at least the 14th century, its name thought to derive from a Picard word for the yew tree. Postal workers know it well. Its postcode is 80829.

France's 35,014 communes carry names ranging from that single letter to a 45-character tongue-twister in Marne. The names are a compressed record of medieval religion, feudal geography, and two centuries of administrative mergers. Running through them reveals a few patterns that don't show up on any political map.

The shape of a French commune name

Most commune names sit between 6 and 12 characters, with the peak at 7–8. The average is 11.8 characters — longer than you might expect, because so many names are hyphenated compounds. Saint-Germain-des-Prés style. Once the saints arrive (more on them shortly), names stretch considerably.

The extremes

At one end: Y (1 letter, Somme). Then 13 two-letter communes, most of them short Occitan or Breton syllables — Sy, By, Oô, Bû, Uz. At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne (45 characters, 7 hyphens), a 2016 merger of three villages that diplomatically kept all three names.

The saint census

3,882 of France's communes — 11.1% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. Among them, Saint-Martin is by far the most widely venerated: 202 communes carry some form of his name, from plain Saint-Martin (five exact matches in five different départements) to Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados. Saint Jean, Saint Pierre, and Saint Germain round out the top four.

The Sainte- feminine form is much rarer: only 295 communes out of 3,882, roughly 1 in 13. The saints are mostly male. The most common feminine dedication is Sainte-Marie (9 communes) and Sainte-Colombe (12 communes — the single most duplicated commune name in France).

The post office problem

2,250 names are shared by more than one commune. Send a letter to Sainte-Colombe, France and it could arrive in any of 12 villages spread from Charente to Seine-et-Marne. Saint-Sauveur exists 11 times across 8 départements. Beaulieu — beautiful place — turns up 10 times; apparently 10 different villages all thought their spot was the most beautiful in France.

What a name carries

The 32,764 distinct commune names that France has accumulated over a thousand years of settlement, revolution, and administrative reform contain more geography than most maps show. Saint- prefixes mark the spread of Carolingian Christianity. Hyphenated compound names mark the modern consolidation of tiny villages — France had 38,000 communes in 1800 and still has 35,000 now, partly because merging them requires keeping both names to avoid political argument.

Then there is Sor, a commune in Ariège with a population of 27. And Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées, population 36. And Y in the Somme, population 89. The smallest places have the shortest names. The merged ones have the longest. The saints are everywhere in between.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which contains 35,014 French communes at their 2021 population boundaries. A small number of recent mergers may not be reflected. Name-length statistics count Unicode characters, so accented letters (é, è, ô) each count as one character.
