What France calls its villages

35,014 commune names, from Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. One is a single letter. Twelve share the same name. Many carry a whole history in their hyphens.

2026-05-21 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Every one of them has a name registered with the INSEE national code system — a name that has survived centuries of wars, mergers, revolutions, and bureaucratic reforms. The average name is 11.8 characters long. But averages, as always, lie.

Y

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter: Y, in the Somme département (dept 80), population 89. It sits on a plateau above the Noye valley, roughly halfway between Amiens and Saint-Quentin. The name — pronounced like the letter — likely derives from a Picard-Frankish root for water or small stream. It has been written this way since at least the 10th century. The post office has coped.

The shape of French commune names

Plot the 35,014 commune names by character length and a clear pattern emerges: a peak at 7–8 characters, then a long right tail that thins slowly toward the record-holders in the 40s. The peak represents the compact monosyllabic core of old rural France — short names that got short because they were spoken every day, passed down without modification, worn smooth by repetition. The tail is something different.

The long tail is the fingerprint of French administrative history. France periodically merges its communes: after World War I, after the Marcellin Law of 1971, and again under the 2010 communes nouvelles reform. When villages merge, they often keep both names, joined by a hyphen. The result: names like Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne (40 characters, three formerly separate villages in the Côte-d'Or) that are essentially a sentence.

The saint problem

11.1% of French communes — 3,898 out of 35,014 — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. The Church's administrative grip on medieval France stamped itself onto the map and has never been fully removed. Even the post-Revolutionary renaming efforts of the 1790s (which briefly produced Sans-Culottes-sur-mer and Montagne-du-bon-air) did not stick; almost all the saints came back.

Sainte-Colombe × 12

The single most reused commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — twelve communes in twelve different départements, from the Hautes-Alpes to the Rhône, from the Manche to the Gironde. Combined, they hold just over 7,600 residents. Each one is presumed to venerate the same saint: Colombe of Sens, a 3rd-century Christian martyr. Colombe means dove.

The runner-up is Saint-Sauveur, at 11 communes. Then Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu, tied at 10. The pattern holds: these are old dedicatory names with no specific attachment to a local feature, so there was nothing to prevent them from being chosen independently across the country over centuries.

What makes a commune name? Geography, religion, local dialect, administrative accident, and the slow erosion of spoken language over centuries. The result is a corpus of 35,014 names that covers one letter and forty-five, a saint for every occasion, and a mergers register that reads like a peace treaty. The map is already the archive.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which includes all 34,886 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories with APL (doctor access) scores. An additional 128 communes with name records but no APL score are included in the name analysis, giving 35,014 total. Name lengths are computed on the INSEE official commune name as recorded in the Code officiel géographique. The communes-index.json companion file (3 MB) contains names, department codes, populations, and centroid coordinates for all 35,014 communes.
