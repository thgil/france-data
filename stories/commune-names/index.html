What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve share the name Sainte-Colombe. More than a third have hyphens. An accounting of French toponymy, in numbers.

2026-05-20 4 min read

France is a country of 35,014 communes. Each one has an official name — a short string of text registered with the state, stamped on road signs, typed into tax returns. For 32,764 of them, that name is entirely unique. For the remaining 2,250, the name is shared with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. And for one commune in the Somme, the entire official name is the single letter Y.

Y

The commune of Y (population 89, Département 80, Somme) holds the distinction of having France's shortest place name. It predates the French state, the hyphen convention, and — probably — recorded history. Medieval documents reference it as Y or Yi; the current single-letter form was standardised in administrative registers sometime in the 19th century. It has a mayor. It has a budget line. It receives visitors occasionally, mostly to photograph the road sign.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — borne by 12 different communes spread from Ardèche to Haute-Garonne to Seine-et-Marne. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11, then Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu at 10. In total, 1,450 names are shared by at least two communes — meaning roughly one in twelve French communes has a namesake somewhere on the map.

This creates real administrative headaches. Two communes called Saint-Aubin-du-Perron exist, in neighbouring Manche and Seine-Maritime. Seven separate communes bear the name Saint-Pierre. GPS systems cope by falling back to département codes and postal zones. Residents cope by explaining, every time, which one they mean.

The saint economy

3,882 communes — 11.1% of France — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. The saints come from every century of Catholicism: local martyrs, pilgrimage bishops, Roman missionaries, popular cults attached to springs and hills. The most widespread saint family is Saint-Martin: 250 communes incorporate Saint-Martin somewhere in their name, making the Bishop of Tours the undisputed patron of French address lines.

The masculine Saint- prefix dominates heavily — 3,587 communes against 295 for Sainte-. Medieval hagiography was not especially equitable, and neither, it turns out, is French cadastre. Still, Sainte-Colombe has the last word on frequency.

The other extreme

If Y is the floor, the ceiling is considerably higher. At 45 characters, the longest commune name in France is:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (51) · 45 characters · formed by the merger of three communes

This kind of compound name is the product of France's commune fusion history. Since the 1971 Marcellin law encouraged mergers, hundreds of villages agreed to join administratively while insisting that every original name remain in the official title. The result is an unwieldy but historically faithful record — a hyphenated census of compromises.

The hyphen habit runs deep across the country: 13,410 communes (38.3%) carry at least one. The connector words are characteristic: -sur- (on the), -en- (in the), -les- (the), -de- (of). Geography, preposition, geography. France in a formula.

Mostly original

Despite the 1,450 shared names and the 3,882 saints, the bigger story is the opposite: 32,764 of France's 35,014 communes — 93.6% — have a name shared by no other commune in the country. The rivers, forests, hills, and lords that baptised these villages in the Middle Ages were, mostly, local enough that each place ended up with something its own. France is a country of very particular places. Most of them just haven't come up with a shorter way to say so than Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are from the DREES APL 2023 commune dataset, covering 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Commune codes follow the INSEE COG (Code Officiel Géographique). Name comparisons are exact and case-sensitive after normalisation.
