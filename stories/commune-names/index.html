What's in a French name?

35,014 communes, 32,764 distinct names — and one of them is a single letter.

2026-06-03 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, 120 kilometres north of Paris, with a population of 89. It sits between wheat fields and the A1 autoroute. Its name, in full, on every official French document, is Y. One letter. No hyphen, no suffix, no saint. Just Y.

France has 35,014 communes. Most have layered names — a saint, a river, a feudal lord, a compass point. A handful have three letters. Thirteen have two. One has one.

Most names are unique — but 3,700 aren't

Of those 35,014 communes, 32,764 have a name no other commune shares. That leaves 3,700 communes — about one in nine — whose name belongs to at least one other village somewhere in France. If your GPS says Saint-Aubin, it could mean any of ten places in ten different départements. If it says Saint-Sauveur, eleven. And if it says Sainte-Colombe? You're picking from twelve.

The saint empire

The pattern at the top of the chart isn't a coincidence: of the 25 most-duplicated commune names, nearly all are Saints. That's because France has a Saint problem. Or a Saint abundance, depending on your perspective.

Across the 35,014 communes, 3,898 — exactly 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. One in nine communes is named after a saint. The Catholic Church has roughly 10,000 saints; France has done its best to match them. The most common first word in any commune name is Saint, appearing 3,596 times. The second most common is La at 1,054. The gap is not close.

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long

The distribution of name lengths peaks at 7 and 8 characters — Beaulieu, Montigny, Verrières. But it has a long right tail. Some communes, through decades of administrative mergers, have accumulated names that read like legal documents.

The longest in France: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne, at 45 characters. It got there the only way possible: by fusing three separate villages in 1972 and keeping all three names. The commune still exists. Its road signs are presumably wide.

Y — and the other 13

At the opposite extreme: 14 communes whose entire official name fits in two letters or fewer. Their brevity is not an accident. Linguists trace most of them to pre-Latin roots — Gaulish, Frankish, Norse — that passed through a millennium of French phonology until there was almost nothing left. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is one of the oldest place names in Normandy. Oô (Haute-Garonne, pop. 103) sits at the foot of the Pyrenees, its name derived from a Gascon word for water.

And then there is Y. Pop. 89. No known saint. The Latin igia is sometimes cited. Whether that etymology is correct or folk legend, the result is the same: a French village, a French mairie, a French postcode, and one letter.

Every country has place names like these — compressed, ancient, barely surviving in form but still alive in function. What France has, in unusual abundance, is a system that treats them all equally: the same legal status, the same mairie, the same INSEE code, whether the name is 45 characters long or one. That's the thing about France's 35,014 communes. The variety is very nearly complete.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the APL 2023 dataset (DREES), joined to administrative boundaries from data.gouv.fr Contours administratifs 2025. The 35,014 communes in this dataset include overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte). Population figures are INSEE 2021 base. Name analysis uses the communeName field exactly as supplied — including accents and hyphens.
