35,000 names for 35,000 villages

France has more communes than any country in the EU. Most were named in the Middle Ages — and 3,898 of them are still called Saint-something.

2026-05-18 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — more than Germany, Italy, Spain, and Poland combined. Most of them are tiny: thousands have fewer than 200 residents, and a few hundred have fewer than ten. What they nearly all have in common is a name that dates back centuries, usually to the Church, a geographical feature, or both at once.

There are 35,014 names on that list. But only 32,764 of them are distinct. 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other — a fact that keeps French postal workers and administrative systems perpetually busy. At the extreme, one name appears twelve times.

The name France keeps reusing

Twelve communes are called Sainte-Colombe. They are in Charente-Maritime, Côte-d'Or, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne — twelve departments, zero ambiguity intended. The pattern holds across the top of the list: almost every repeated name is a saint's name, a landscape feature, or a combination of both.

The saint machine

More than one in ten French communes — 11.1%, or 3,898 in total — starts with Saint or Sainte. That's not even counting communes that carry a saint's name in the middle, like Javerlhac-et-la-Chapelle-Saint-Robert. The Church had a long head start on French cartography.

Among the saints, Martin is king: 202 communes begin with Saint-Martin. Jean, Pierre, and Germain follow. The list maps a rough medieval religious calendar — the saints most venerated in the Frankish church eight centuries ago are the ones most likely to appear on your GPS today.

One letter. Forty-five.

France's commune naming range is extraordinary. At one end: a single letter. At the other: a name longer than a tweet.

Y is a village of 89 people in the Somme. It received its name from an old Celtic word meaning "place near the water." It has been officially called Y since at least the 13th century, which means it predates the French Republic's entire administrative apparatus by about five hundred years. It is, by one measure, the shortest place name in the world.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is what happens when three medieval parishes are merged into one commune and no one wants to lose their name. The 45-character result sits in the Marne département with a population of 491. Sending a letter there is, by one measure, a form of calligraphy.

Thirteen communes manage their entire identity in two letters — Eu, Bû, Us, Gy, Ry, Ur, Oz, Ri, Oô, By, Py, Sy, and Uz — each a syllable that once meant something precise in Old French, Gaulish, or Latin.

Name length follows a curve

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. Most fall in the 6–9 character range — short enough to say quickly, long enough to carry some history. The distribution has a long tail: once you get past 25 characters, the names tend to be hyphenated amalgamations of former parishes, the result of 19th- and 20th-century administrative mergers that nobody quite wanted but everyone agreed to.

The administrative cost of history

The 3,700 communes that share their name with at least one other are not just a curiosity. Every time a French administration needs to disambiguate — mail, tax records, voter rolls, health data, COVID statistics — it has to append a département code or regional qualifier. The 12 Sainte-Colombes are probably well-practiced at this by now.

France periodically merges small communes to reduce the count. The programme communes nouvelles, which began in earnest in 2015, has already absorbed several hundred villages into larger administrative units. When two communes merge, one of the oldest questions in France resurfaces: whose name survives?

For now, the answer is usually: both. The result is a hyphenated compound that adds a few more characters to an already full list. The longest names will keep getting longer.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the communes-apl.geojson dataset compiled from DREES APL 2023 and INSEE 2021 population data — the same source as Story 2 (medical deserts). It covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. A small number of communes in overseas departments (Martinique, Guadeloupe, Réunion, Guyane, Mayotte) are included. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base. The raw commune list is available as communes-index.json (2.1 MB).
