35,014 names

France's communes run from Y (one letter, 89 people, Somme) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. In between: 3,882 saints, twelve Sainte-Colombes, and a place called Montcuq.

2026-05-15 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. That's more communes than any other country in the EU — a legacy of the 1789 Revolution, which turned every parish into its own municipality and gave each one the right to keep its own name. The result is a national gazetteer unlike anything else: 32,764 distinct place names, many of them centuries old, a few of them magnificently strange.

One republic, under the saints

Eleven percent of all French communes — 3,882 of them — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. France is famously secular, but its geography is devotional. The most popular patron is Saint-Martin (202 communes), followed by Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144). Saint-Martin's appeal makes historical sense: he was a 4th-century soldier-bishop who split his cloak with a beggar outside Amiens and became the patron of France itself. The country named a lot of real estate after him.

Twelve places called Sainte-Colombe

Of the 35,014 communes, 32,764 have a name no other commune shares. The remaining 2,250 share a name with at least one neighbour — across the country, not necessarily next door. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times in 10 different départements. Colombe was a 3rd-century martyr from Córdoba; her name spread slowly across Gaul and kept multiplying. If you write a letter to Sainte-Colombe, France, you need to know your postal code.

Y, and the art of the short name

The shortest commune name in France is simply Y. It sits in the Somme, département 80, with 89 inhabitants. The postman delivers to streets in Y. The administrative address forms have a field for commune name; it says Y. It is, objectively, perfect.

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. Oô in the Haute-Garonne sits in the Pyrenees and sounds like surprise. Eu, in Seine-Maritime, is the largest two-letter commune (6,591 people) and was a favourite royal residence of Louis-Philippe. Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales) shares its two letters with one of the oldest cities in human history; it has 348 people and is near Bourg-Madame.

Longest names

At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters, in the Marne. Like many of the longest names, it's the result of a commune merger — what France calls a fusion de communes. When two villages join administratively but neither wants to disappear from the map, their names get hyphenated together. The longer the name, the more likely it is to be a love letter to a compromise.

The ones that made people look twice

France's historical naming conventions were set centuries before anyone worried about how a town name sounds in a different language, or in a different era. Some results are memorable. Montcuq-en-Quercy-Blanc (Lot département, ~1,200 residents) became a national punchline in 1976 when a French TV presenter had to say its name on air — montcuq is a plausible Occitan derivation of mons cucus, meaning "hill of the cuckoo," but it sounds like mon cul, "my arse." It now leans into the joke. Condom, in the Gers, gave its name to the bishop Bertrand de Got before he became Pope Clement V; it was not named after its most famous modern homonym. Brest is a perfectly dignified Breton city. The name means "promontory."

Behind every one of these 35,014 names is a millennium of local history: a patron saint's day, a river's old name, a hill, a forest, a Roman camp, a Frankish lord. The naming archive is open and searchable because it's public infrastructure — every commune's code, name, and administrative record is maintained by INSEE and published under an open licence. France treats its own geography as a dataset. That's what makes it fun to map.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which is built on the INSEE commune reference and population figures from the 2021 census.
