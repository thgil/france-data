The village called Y

35,014 communes, one alphabet, and 3,882 saints — what French place names reveal about the country.

2026-05-26 5 min read

In the département of the Somme, between Amiens and Péronne, there is a village of 89 people. It has a mayor, a postal code (80829), and a name that is just one letter long. The village is called Y.

Y is not an abbreviation. It is not a typo. It is the entire official name, recognised by INSEE, used on road signs, and postmarked on letters. Among France's 35,014 communes, it is the only one with a single-character name — and by most accounts one of the shortest village names on earth.

The short end of the alphabet

Y has company at the short end. Thirteen other communes manage their entire identity in two letters. They are scattered across the country — from Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées to Eu in Seine-Maritime, from Oô (two o's, one circumflex) in the Haute-Garonne to Sy, population 54, in the Ardennes.

Some of these names are relics of Gaulish, Frankish, or Occitan roots so compressed by time that their original meaning is lost. Others, like Eu (6,591 inhabitants — by far the largest of the short-name communes), are old enough to appear in medieval chronicles. None of them fit on a normal address label without looking like a typographical accident.

The saint factory

3,882 communes whose name begins with Saint- or Sainte- — 11.1% of all French communes

No other country has industrialised the naming of saints quite like France. One in nine French communes is dedicated to one. The practice goes back to the early medieval period, when parishes were named for their patron saint — a naming convention that survived the Revolution, survived administrative reforms, and survived the twentieth century almost entirely intact.

The distribution is not random. The departments of south-central France are the most saint-dense: Ardèche leads the country, with 29% of its 335 communes bearing a saint's name. Creuse and Loire follow closely. The north and east — Alsace, Lorraine, the Nord — have far fewer, reflecting different medieval settlement patterns and, in Alsace, centuries of German influence.

The saints themselves tend to repeat. Saint-Martin, Saint-Pierre, Saint-Michel, Saint-Paul — these appear dozens of times each across the country. And somehow, in an irony that is very French, even the longest commune name in France starts with one.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters — Marne, dept. 51

The name tells a story in itself: two medieval parishes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont and Saint-Genest-et-Isson — that were merged into a single commune and simply hyphenated together. France created over 700 such communes nouvelles by merging smaller ones, and the naming convention, where both names are preserved, has produced some remarkable results.

The name collision problem

If you send a letter to Sainte-Colombe, France, you have a problem. There are twelve of them — in twelve different départements, stretching from Yonne to Gironde, from Manche to Rhône. Sainte-Colombe is the most duplicated commune name in France, but it is far from alone.

Most duplicated commune names (communes sharing the same name)
1,450 commune names shared by more than one commune — 3,700 communes cannot be identified by name alone

This is a genuine administrative headache. The postal system resolves it with département codes. INSEE resolves it with five-digit commune codes (the same codes used in the GeoJSON behind this site). When French people say they are from Saint-Martin, they almost always add "dans le 66" or "en Charente" — the département disambiguates what the name cannot.

The shape of a name

The median French commune name is 12 characters long. The mode — the most common single length — is 7 or 8 characters: names like Villars, Courcelles, Beaulieu. But the distribution has a long right tail, driven by hyphenated mergers, double-barrelled saint dedications, and the baroque tradition of appending a river name, a neighbouring commune, or a geographic feature to distinguish two places with the same base name.

Distribution of commune name lengths (characters)

The tail matters. At the far end sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters). Just behind it: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43), and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41). Each one a bureaucratic accordion, stretched wide by history.

And then, at the other end of the accordion, population 89, one letter, somewhere in the Somme: Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset used in Story 2 (medical deserts). That dataset covers 35,014 communes — essentially all of metropolitan France plus DOM-TOM. Name length analysis, duplicate counting, and saint-prefix analysis were all performed on this extract. Centroids in communes-index.json are polygon barycentres derived from the IGN commune boundary GeoJSON.
