The town called Y

France has 35,014 communes and 32,764 distinct names. One commune is named Y. Twelve are named Sainte-Colombe. The longest name is 45 characters.

2026-05-15 4 min read

France's communes are a naming legacy dating to the Revolution. In 1789, the National Assembly redrew the country into about 44,000 communes, mostly inheriting names from the parishes and seigneuries they replaced. More than two centuries later, mergers and reorganisations have trimmed that count to around 35,000 — each with an officially registered name, unique or not.

Not unique, it turns out. Of the 35,014 communes in France's 2023 commune registry, 32,764 carry a distinct name. That leaves 1,450 names shared by 3,700 communes. Somewhere in France there are twelve places all called the same thing.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. It appears in twelve different départements — from the Hautes-Alpes to Gironde, Seine-et-Marne, Yonne, and Seine-Maritime. They are separate places, governed separately, taxed separately. They share only a name and, presumably, a patron saint. Saint-Sauveur comes second with eleven communes; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu tie at ten each.

The Saint index

3,898 communes — 11.1% of all French communes — begin with Saint or Sainte. The administrative map of France is also a Catholic atlas.

The pattern runs deep. After centuries of parish geography, saints became the default naming convention across nearly every region. The Saint- prefix attaches to thousands of different suffixes — Martin, Pierre, Aubin, Loup, Michel, Paul — creating a naming landscape that is simultaneously monotonous and varied. Strip away the prefix and you'd still have thousands of distinct places; keep it, and you're navigating an enormous hagiography.

The single-letter commune

Then there is Y.

Y is a commune in the Somme, département 80, with a population of 89 people. Its official INSEE code is 80829. Its name is one character long. It has been called Y since at least the 12th century — the name almost certainly derives from a Germanic or Old French root meaning "water" or "island" (the town sits near marshland) — but stripped of its etymology, it reads like a placeholder.

Y is not alone in the very-short tier. Thirteen two-letter communes exist across France. Some read like minimal poetry — Oz, Uz, Ry, Eu — and several have plausible linguistic roots in Celtic, Basque, or Germanic substrates. Eu in Seine-Maritime is actually a sizeable town with over 6,500 residents, despite its two-letter name. Most two-letter communes, however, are tiny: the thirteen average around 820 residents.

The shape of French naming

Plotted by character count, the distribution of commune names has a clear shape: a peak at seven and eight characters, a long tail to the right, and a lonely bar at the far left. The median commune name is ten characters long. The mean is a bit higher — pulled up by the long tail of hyphenated merger names. Most names sit in the comfortable middle range of a single word or a simple compound.

The 45-character champion

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is a commune in the Marne with 491 residents. Its name is the longest in France at 45 characters — a legacy of a 1972 merger of three previously separate communes. It is not an outlier in this respect: most of the very long names are merger artifacts, the kind that accumulate when administrative convenience requires two or three distinct identities to coexist inside a single municipal budget. The second-longest, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône.

Together, Y and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson bracket the full range of French commune naming — one syllable and one etymological root at one end, a small paragraph of administrative history at the other. The 35,014 communes in between tell a version of French history that no textbook covers: Catholic geography, revolutionary cartography, German and Celtic substrates, and two centuries of mergers and reorganisations, all crystallised into a single indexed field.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the APL commune dataset published by DREES (2023), joined to administrative boundary data from data.gouv.fr. The 35,014 communes include metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name deduplication is exact-string matching on the communeName field, which uses the official INSEE-registered name including accents and hyphens. Arrondissements of Paris, Lyon, and Marseille appear as separate entries in the source data.
