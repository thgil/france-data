What's in a name?

35,014 communes, from Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. What French place names reveal about a thousand years of Catholic mapping.

2026-05-18 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, 89 people live in a commune with the most economical address in the country. The commune is called Y. One letter. Population 89. Département 80. Y has its own mayor, its own INSEE code (80829), and — one assumes — considerable postcode confusion. It is the shortest official commune name in France.

At the other end of the alphabet, and the tape measure, sits a commune in the Marne that took the brief "shortest name wins" and decided it wasn't interested. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. 45 characters, three historical hamlets fused into one administrative unit in 1972. Population 491. The name arrives before the person has finished asking what it's called.

The bell curve of French place names

French commune names follow a roughly normal distribution around a median of 10 characters. There are 13 two-letter communes — By, Eu, Gy, Ry, Us, Oz, Ur — most of them Norman or Burgundian survivals from pre-medieval settlement. There are 154 three-letter names. At the long end, 79 communes stretch past 30 characters, nearly all of them products of the fusions de communes of the 1970s under the Marcellin law, when France tried — partially, reluctantly — to consolidate its 36,000 administrative units.

The saint invasion

3,898 communes — 11.1% of France — start with Saint or Sainte. One in nine. The Catholic toponymy runs so deep that it shapes entire départements: in Ardèche (07), 29% of communes begin with a saint's name. Creuse and Loire both hit 28%. The pattern follows the old Massif Central heartlands of rural Catholic France, the country that kept faith longest and named its settlements accordingly.

The most common saint is Martin — 255 communes have Martin somewhere in their name, successor to a fifth-century bishop of Tours whose cult spread across the whole of Western Europe. Jean appears 170 times. Pierre 180 times. The frequency list reads like a medieval liturgical calendar.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most repeated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times across eight différent départements. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11, Saint-Aubin with 10. None of these duplicates are near each other — they are genuine independent settlements that all, at some point in the early medieval period, dedicated themselves to the same patron and wrote it into their name permanently.

The grammar of geography

Beyond saints and lengths, French commune names carry a hidden grammatical structure that indexes the landscape. 2,007 communes sit sur something — a river, a ridge, a ford. 545 are lès something — an old French preposition meaning "near," now archaic everywhere except in place names. 257 are sous (under) something, typically a cliff, a forest, or a larger town. Read the prepositions and you're reading the topography.

2,007 -sur- on / above
545 -lès- near (archaic)
257 -sous- under / below

France has been trying to consolidate its 36,000-odd communes for fifty years, with limited success. The communes nouvelles programme, relaunched in 2015, has merged several hundred — each merger tending to produce longer, hyphenated names as the constituent villages insist on their continued symbolic existence. The administrative map shrinks. The name gets longer. Somewhere in the Marne, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson waits to be displaced as the record-holder. It's probably not worried.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the INSEE APL dataset (DREES, 2023 vintage), which covers 35,014 metropolitan communes and overseas territories. Name lengths are character counts of the official INSEE libellé field. Saint/Sainte counts use exact prefix matching on the communeName field. Length histogram bins each name by character count.
