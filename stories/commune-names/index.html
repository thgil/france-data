What French communes are called

35,014 communes, one called Y, 3,882 named after saints, and one that takes 45 characters to write.

2026-06-04 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. That's the full name — a single letter, population 89, sitting in the Somme in the north of France. It probably has the shortest postal address in the country: 1 rue de la Mairie, 80829 Y.

Y is not a typo or an abbreviation. It's a genuine medieval place name, derived from the Old Picard word for water (ive or aive), and it has been Y for centuries. The commune council did not rename themselves as a joke. They simply never had reason to change.

The short end of the spectrum

Y is the only one-letter commune in France. Thirteen others make do with two. All but one are in the north or east — the legacy of Germanic and Norse settlement that left short, blunt syllables on the map long before the Académie française decided French should be elegant.

The standout is Eu in Seine-Maritime — population 6,591, which makes it by far the most-populated of the short-name clubs. Its name comes from the Latin Augia, meaning a water meadow. It gave its name to a royal château and hosted several French kings; it never needed more letters to be important.

The shape of a French commune name

Most French commune names are 7–9 characters long. The average is 11.8 characters — but that average is pulled upward by a long tail of hyphenated monsters. The median sits at 10. Half of France's communes have names under 10 characters; the other half don't.

The long end: hyphen country

The longest commune name in France has 45 characters and 7 hyphens:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · population 491 · 7 hyphens · 45 characters

This isn't an accident — it's a scar. After the Revolution, France merged hundreds of small hamlets into single administrative communes to simplify tax collection. When two or three villages refused to be swallowed by the largest, they sometimes negotiated a compromise: keep both names, join them with a hyphen, argue about the result forever. The longest names are all products of this 19th-century administrative fusion fever.

The Saint empire

The single most common first word in a French commune name is Saint — by a large margin. 3,882 communes begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That's 11.1% of all communes in France. One in nine places is named after a saint.

The Church was the dominant administrative power in medieval France, and parishes gave their names to settlements. When the Revolution tried to de-Christianize France in the 1790s, it renamed many of these places — Saint-Tropez briefly became Héraclée, Saint-Malo became Port-Malo. Most reverted within years. The saints won.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 different communes across 12 different départements. Second is Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), third is Saint-Aubin (10). Beaulieu — the "beautiful place" — appears 10 times.

Altogether, 3,700 communes share their exact name with at least one other. The postal code system was partly designed to resolve this: when you write a French address, the five-digit code does the disambiguation that the name cannot.

These 35,014 names are a compressed history of France — Roman water words, Norman syllables, medieval saints, Revolutionary fusions, and at least one single letter that refused to become anything else. The longest name needs 45 characters to describe two hamlets that couldn't agree. The shortest needed just one, and has had it for 900 years.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Centroids are calculated from the commune boundary polygons in the same source. Name lengths and duplicate counts are computed from the official INSEE commune name field (communeName). The communes-index.json file (3 MB) contains all 35,014 commune names, codes, departments, populations, and centroids — available for reuse in future stories.
