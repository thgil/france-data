What's in a name?

35,014 communes, 32,764 unique names — and one place in the Somme called Y.

2026-05-25 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme called Y. One letter. No article, no hyphen, no saint, no geographical qualifier. Its postal code is 80190. Its population is around 100. Every address in it ends with the line Y, France — which confuses postal routing software, form validation, and occasional visitors who assume they've made a typo.

Y is an outlier, but not by much. France has 13 communes with two-letter names, and 147 communes whose names have three characters or fewer. Each is a real place, with a mayor and a school and a morning market. The French administrative tradition of preserving every historical commune name intact — however abbreviated, however ancient — is what keeps Y on the map.

The saint invasion

The most striking pattern in French commune names isn't length — it's religion. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,596 begin with Saint and 295 with Sainte. That's 3,891 communes — 11.1% of the total — named after a Christian saint. More than one in nine. You would need to visit a different saint-named commune every day for more than ten years to see them all.

The runner-up prefix — La (1,054 communes) — doesn't come close. Le (758), Les (328), and Villers (105, a Gallo-Roman suffix meaning "farm") round out the top five. France names its settlements after saints, definite articles, and vaguely Latin agricultural nouns, in that order.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — twelve communes bear it, spread from Gironde to Haute-Savoie. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each ten. Altogether, 1,450 unique names are shared by 3,700 communes — meaning more than one commune in ten shares its name with at least one other.

The problem is solved with hyphens. Sainte-Colombe becomes Sainte-Colombe-de-Duras, Sainte-Colombe-de-Peyre, Sainte-Colombe-en-Bruilhois. The qualifier is usually geographic: a river, a hill, a neighbouring settlement. You can read the landscape of France from its suffixes — -sur-Mer, -en-Vexin, -les-Bains.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. The distribution has a familiar asymmetry: it peaks around 7–8 characters and has a long right tail of very long names. A significant proportion of communes have names exceeding 20 characters — nearly 11% have names longer than 20 characters.

The longest names are almost all products of commune mergers. France began consolidating its smallest communes in the 1970s under the loi Marcellin, but the law made mergers voluntary and name-preservation the default. The result is compound names that read like a roll-call: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41, Calvados). The longest of all, at 45 characters, is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne — itself the product of a 1972 merger of four communes.

A commune name is not just a label — it's a compressed record of how France settled and resettled its territory over a thousand years: Gallo-Roman farms, Frankish saints, medieval geography, twentieth-century mergers. The name Y, for all its brevity, is doing exactly what every other commune name does. It's just doing it with one letter.

A note on the data. Name analysis uses commune names from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, covering 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Names were taken from the communeName field, which is sourced from the INSEE Code Officiel Géographique (COG). The communes-index.json file generated for this story provides names, centroids, population, and département for all 35,014 communes and is available for reuse by other stories in this project.
