The commune called Y

France has 35,014 communes. One is named Y. Another runs to 45 characters. In between: 3,898 saints, 13 two-letter villages, and one name that appears in 12 different départements.

2026-06-07 5 min read

In the Somme, département 80, there is a commune called Y. Just Y. One letter. Population 89. It has a mayor, a postal code (80190), and a centuries-old history — the name is thought to derive from a Germanic root meaning water, filtered through generations of Old Picard dialect until nothing remained but a vowel. It is the shortest commune name in France, and quite possibly the shortest legal place name in Europe.

Y is not an outlier so much as an extreme point on a very wide distribution. France's 35,014 communes — metropolitan territory plus overseas — carry names ranging from one character to 45. The average is 11.8 characters. The median is 10. The histogram below shows the full shape.

The two-letter communes are scattered — By in the Doubs, Bû in Eure-et-Loir, Eu in Seine-Maritime, Gy in Haute-Saône, Oz in Isère, Oô in Haute-Garonne (those are two letter O's), Py and Ur in Pyrénées-Orientales, Ri in Orne, Ry in Seine-Maritime, Sy in Ardennes, Us in Val-d'Oise, Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées. Many sit in river valleys or mountain folds where ancient names compressed over time. Ry, in Normandy, is thought to be the real-world model for Yonville-l'Abbaye in Flaubert's Madame Bovary.

The long end: communes nouvelles

France's longest commune name — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne — runs to 45 characters and contains two saints, three hyphens, a preposition, and a conjunction. It is a commune nouvelle: in 2016, three villages merged under a single administrative umbrella and hyphenated their names together. The result is a mouthful that fits on no standard form.

The long tail of French commune names is largely a record of 21st-century municipal mergers. France launched a major fusional policy in 2015 with the loi NOTRe, encouraging neighbouring villages to consolidate. Many chose to keep both names, joined by hyphens. The result is an ongoing ratchet: the list of 40-plus-character communes grows with each wave of mergers, and France's administrative vocabulary grows a little stranger each time.

One nation, 3,898 saints

The most striking pattern is not at the extremes but in the middle: 3,898 of France's 35,014 communes — 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. This is a direct imprint of medieval parish geography. Every diocese planted saints in the landscape, and when the Revolution created communes from those parishes in 1789, the names came with them. Attempts to de-Christianise place names during the Terror renamed a few hundred, but most reverted within a decade.

The most-duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times — in 12 different départements, from Haute-Garonne to Yonne. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin, 10. The chart below shows the full top 15.

La, Le, Les

Beyond saints, the dominant pattern is the definite article: La leads all first words with 1,054 communes — La Chapelle-something, La Tour-something, La Croix-something. Le follows with 758, and Les with 328. Together, articled communes number 2,140, or 6.1% of the total. This is a landscape still narrated by medieval French grammar, where every hill, wood, ford, and chapel found its name in a gendered article and a common noun.

The full 35,014 names form a kind of dictionary of French rural life: Beaulieu (beautiful place, 10 occurrences), Beaumont (beautiful hill, 8), Verrières (glassworks, 8). The same names repeat across the country because they described the same features — a spring, a forest edge, a Roman road — that medieval settlers found everywhere and named the same way everywhere.

What France's commune names preserve, across 35,014 entries, is a compressed archive of a millennium of settlement. The single letter Y in the Somme is as much a historical document as the 45-character hyphenated merger in the Marne. One encodes a Germanic root through centuries of erosion; the other encodes a 2016 vote in a regional council chamber. Both end up on the same administrative list, alphabetically sorted, equally official.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are drawn from the APL commune dataset published by DREES (2023 vintage, INSEE 2021 population base), which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Name lengths are computed on the official commune name string as published in the dataset. A small number of overseas commune names contain non-Latin characters; all are counted by character length as stored in UTF-8.
