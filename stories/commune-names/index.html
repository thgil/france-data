The 35,014 names of France

One commune is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. The longest name has 45 characters and seven hyphens. France's naming system is a historical archive hiding in plain sight.

2026-05-27 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes — and 35,014 names. Most fall in the comfortable middle: seven or eight characters, one or two syllables, a root that traces back to Roman, Gaulish, or Frankish origins. But the tails of the distribution are where history gets weird.

At the short end sits Y. One character. Population 89. Located in the Somme, département 80. Y is believed to be one of the shortest place names in the world — the remnant of a Picard word meaning "water" or "place near water," worn down by a thousand years of erosion until only the vowel remained. Nobody who lives in Y has a long commute to type their own address.

Thirteen communes have two-letter names: Sy, By, Bû, Oô, Oz, Ri, Uz, Py, Ur, Gy, Eu, Ry, and Us. Some are Breton, some Norman, some Pyrenean. All of them were presumably named at a time when brevity was valued over uniqueness.

The marathon names

At the other end, length is almost always the product of merger. French administrative history is full of communes that were fused together — sometimes willingly, sometimes under pressure — and kept both names, joined with a hyphen. The longest name in France, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, is 45 characters and was created by merging three separate villages in the Marne.

The runner-up, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône — four villages, one name, and a nightmare for anyone who has to fill out a form. A commune's name is a compressed family tree: each hyphenated segment is a place that once existed independently.

The distribution has a long right tail: 492 communes have names of 26 or more characters. Most of them are in regions where the 1972 and 2016 waves of communal mergers left the longest paper trail — Normandy, Burgundy, and the Marne in particular.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them, spread across twelve different départements, from the Hautes-Alpes to the Yonne. Each one is named after Saint Columba — a martyred Christian girl from third-century Spain whose cult spread across Gaul in the early Middle Ages. The church followed the trade routes. The names followed the churches.

Coming second is Saint-Sauveur (eleven communes), followed by Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (ten each). In total, 1,450 distinct names are shared by two or more communes — meaning 3,700 communes (10.6%) share their name with at least one other place in France. When you search for "Saint-Marcel" on a French map application, you will get nine results.

The Saint-Martin empire

Above the level of exact duplicates, the naming patterns are even more dramatic. There are 202 communes named exactly Saint-Martin — more than any other single base name. When you add communes where Saint-Martin appears as a component (Saint-Martin-d'Ardèche, Beauchery-Saint-Martin, and so on), the family swells to 250. One in every 140 French communes has "Martin" in its name.

1,991 communes on a river

1,991communes contain the syllable -sur- — as in "on the [river]." Neuilly-sur-Seine. Vitry-sur-Seine. Saône-en-Armançon. The pattern is old: medieval settlements named themselves by their most useful geographic feature, and the river usually won. Every one of those 1,991 hyphens is a small piece of hydrological history.

The rivers that gave their names most often are the Seine (dozens of communes), the Loire, the Rhône, and the Marne. Smaller rivers appear too — the Oise, the Sarthe, the Dordogne — each anchoring a cluster of villages that oriented their identity around the water that made them viable.

A name is a document

France's commune names are a compressed archive of the country's administrative, religious, and geographic history. The saints chart the spread of Christianity from the third century onward. The articles (Le, La, Les) mark landscapes — Le Puy is the hill, La Roche is the rock, Les Baux are the bauxite cliffs that gave bauxite its name. The hyphens trace the mergers, acquisitions, and political pressures of eight centuries of French administration.

And somewhere in the Somme, with a population of 89, a commune called Y continues to exist — quietly proving that the shortest answer is sometimes the most durable one.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from INSEE's administrative boundaries dataset (contours des communes, 2025 vintage), joined to DREES APL data that was pre-loaded for the medical deserts story. The analysis covers all 35,014 communes with valid names. Name length is measured in Unicode characters. The communes-index.json file (3 MB) compiled for this story indexes all communes with name, INSEE code, département, population, and centroid coordinates.
