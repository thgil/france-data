From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont

France has 35,014 communes. Their names span one letter to 45 characters — and 11% of them begin with "Saint."

2026-05-29 4 min read

Every French commune has an official name — a name that appears on road signs, in postal addresses, and in the INSEE code register that underpins everything from tax records to electoral rolls. There are 35,014 of them. Some have names centuries old, worn smooth by time. Others were coined in 2010 when two or three villages merged and no one wanted to lose their name. The results range from a single letter to an obstacle course of hyphens and saints.

The extremes

The shortest commune name in France — and, for what it's worth, in the world — is a single letter. Y sits in the Somme, département 80, population 89. Nobody knows exactly how it got its name; the leading theory traces it to a pre-Latin root meaning "water" or "flat land," but by the time it entered written records the name had already been reduced to one character. It has a mayor. It has a post office. It has a commune code (80829).

At the other extreme is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne (département 51) formed by a 2010 merger that kept all three village names. At 45 characters, it holds the record for France's longest commune name. The runner-up, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters), also dates from a merger. Municipal nostalgia is expensive in syllables.

The shape of French place names

Most commune names fall between 5 and 20 characters, with a peak around 7–8. The distribution has a long right tail — the hyphenated saint-towns and merged municipalities that pile up past 20 characters. The average is 11.8 characters. The median is 10.

The saint epidemic

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. This is not a coincidence. It reflects the Carolingian-era practice of naming settlements after their parish patron saints, which spread unevenly across France: more common in the west and south, sparser in Alsace (where German naming patterns dominate) and in older Celtic territories.

The most duplicated commune name is Sainte-Colombe, with 12 instances spread across the country. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times. When you write a letter to "Sainte-Colombe," you need the département or the postal code — the name alone is not enough.

When names repeat

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have unique names. That means 2,250 communes share their name with at least one other commune — and in total 3,700 communes cannot be identified by name alone. For letters, GPS, or government databases, the INSEE 5-digit commune code is the only unambiguous identifier. The nom field in any dataset is best treated as a label, not a key.

France's commune map is a palimpsest — layers of Latin, Frankish, Gaulish, Occitan, Breton, Alsatian, and the occasional acronym all squeezed into the same national database. Y is just the most extreme example of a story that's true for every name on the list: each one is a compressed history, waiting for someone to ask where it came from.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. The commune names are the official INSEE designations as of the 2023 vintage. Name lengths are computed on the full official name string including hyphens, spaces, and diacritics.
