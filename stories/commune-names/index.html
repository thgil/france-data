What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. One has 45 characters and 7 hyphens. One in nine starts with "Saint."

25 May 2026 5 min read

Every French commune has an official name — registered with the INSEE, printed on road signs, stamped on administrative documents. There are 35,014 of them, and together they form a catalogue of medieval saints, geographical features, feudal lords, Gallic geography, and at least one improbable single letter.

The distribution of name lengths follows a rough bell curve centred on 10–11 characters. Most communes have names somewhere between Pau and Montpellier in length. But the tails are where things get interesting.

The shortest: one letter

Y

A village of 89 people in the Somme (département 80). Named after a fork in a river — the French word i grec (the letter Y) describes the shape where two waterways meet. Cartographers needed exactly one character. The commune obliged.

Y has existed since at least the twelfth century. It sits on the edge of the Santerre plateau, 22 kilometres east of Amiens. Its post code is 80190. Giving someone your address there — Y, Somme — reads like a bad joke. The commune does not appear to find this funny.

The two-letter club

Beyond Y, France has thirteen communes whose names contain exactly two characters. They're scattered across regions with no obvious connection except an apparent indifference to syllables.

The Pyrénées cluster — Py (79 people), Ur (348), Oô (103) — reflects Occitan and Basque root words compressed into final syllables. Eu, in Seine-Maritime, is the largest of the group at 6,591 residents and the site of a royal château where Queen Victoria visited Louis-Philippe in 1843. The visit was notable enough; the name remained short regardless.

The Church founded or renamed thousands of rural parishes between the ninth and fifteenth centuries. The pattern it left behind is now cartographic infrastructure: if you filtered every Saint/Sainte commune off the French map, the north and west of the country would lose entire clusters of place names at once. Brittany, Normandy, and Poitou-Charentes are particularly dense.

"Saint" is far and away the most common first word in French place names — appearing as a compound prefix in 3,587 communes. The next most common prefix, "Sainte," has 295 occurrences. After that comes "La Chapelle" with 152 — which itself means chapel, so even the secular version of this phenomenon is ecclesiastical.

Sainte-Colombe: twelve versions of the same village

The single most repeated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them, spread across twelve different départements, from Gironde in the southwest to Yonne in the north.

Sainte Colombe was a third-century martyr whose cult spread through Burgundy and Provence. Twelve separate medieval parishes chose the same patron. Today, if you ask for Sainte-Colombe without a département, France's postal system requires further information. The smallest has 59 residents (Hautes-Alpes); the largest, 1,953 (Rhône). The Rhône version is the only one incorporated into a metropolitan area — it's a suburb of Lyon.

Saint-Sauveur (11 communes) and Saint-Aubin (10) round out the top three. Beaulieu, a secular name meaning "beautiful place," also appears 10 times — proof that some naming traditions needed no bishop to spread.

The longest: seven hyphens, three villages

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 45 characters · 7 hyphens · population: 246

France's longest commune name was created on 1 January 1972 when the communes of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson were merged by administrative decree. The resulting name concatenates all three, joined by hyphens and the conjunction et. Road signs in the area are reportedly long.

It beats its nearest rival, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters), by two characters. Both are products of the 1970s municipal consolidation wave, which created multi-hyphen names by simple concatenation rather than coinage. The 1,313 communes with 4 or more hyphens are almost all merger products from that decade.

Notes on the data

Name counts and length statistics are derived from the DREES APL dataset commune index. A small number of entries (Paris, Lyon, Marseille arrondissements) are recorded as separate communes with ordinal suffixes; these are counted individually. Some merged communes (fusion-association) may carry hyphenated joint names not reflected in the original medieval foundation.

Prior work

Commune naming patterns are well-documented in French toponymy literature. This story draws on the data rather than the literature — every number cited here is computed directly from the INSEE commune register.
