What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. The rest have a lot to say about French history, religion, and the urge to hyphenate.

2026-05-24 4 min read

If you wanted to send a letter to the smallest-named commune in France, the address would contain a single letter: Y. Population 89. Département de la Somme. APL score 2.78 — adequately served, for what it's worth. The post office presumably manages.

Y is not a typo or an abbreviation. It's a name, full stop, and it has been since at least the 13th century. It sits in a flat agricultural landscape north of Rouen and has nothing obvious in common with the other 35,013 communes in France — except that, like all of them, it has an official name registered in the Code Officiel Géographique.

The shortest names

After Y, there are 13 communes with two-letter names. They cluster in the north and the Pyrenees — short names are a Norman, Flemish, and Gascon specialty, often survivals of pre-Latin roots. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the most populous; Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, pop. 36) is the quietest.

The most common name: Sainte-Colombe

At the other end of the taxonomy problem: 12 communes named Sainte-Colombe, spread across 12 different départements from the Hautes-Alpes to the Yonne. There's no administrative confusion because each has a different INSEE code, but if you just type "Sainte-Colombe" into a GPS you'll need to guess which one.

The name honours Saint Columba of Sens, a 3rd-century Christian martyr. Twelve places thought her worth naming after. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11 instances, Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu with 10 each. The top 20 most-duplicated names read like a medieval catalogue of local piety and landscape description.

One in nine communes is a saint

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,898 — 11.1% — begin with "Saint" or "Sainte". That's a commune named after a saint every nine entries in the directory. Add in place names that contain "Saint" mid-word (like Dampierre-Saint-Nicolas) and the figure climbs further.

The saint names are concentrated in the rural centre and west — the same geography as old diocese boundaries. Brittany is particularly dense with saints, many of them Breton or Irish missionaries whose names don't appear in the Roman canon at all: their local cults outlasted their canonisation paperwork.

Another 38.3% of commune names are hyphenated — Saint-Martin-de-la-Brasque, Villers-sous-Saint-Leu, Le Châtelet-en-Brie. Hyphens are the trace of mergers, qualifications, and disambiguation: when two villages shared the same name (or were absorbed into each other), they were hyphenated apart.

The longest name: 45 characters

At the extremity of the hyphen problem sits this:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters · Marne (51) · population 491

This commune in the Marne is the product of three former villages — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — merged during the Napoleonic administrative consolidation and subsequently hyphenated into a single unwieldy unit. It is not alone: the runner-up, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), tells the same story of multiple villages stitched together by geography and bureaucracy.

A bimodal distribution

The average commune name is 11.8 characters — shorter than you'd expect. That's because most communes have compact names: single-word names like Brest or Reims or Lyon cluster at 4–8 characters and pull the average down. The long tail of compound hyphenated names stretches out to 45. The distribution is bimodal: a peak of short traditional names and a second, lower peak of merged-village compounds.

35,014 names. One letter. Twelve saints named Colombe. 3,898 saints in total. 45 characters in one mouthful. The Code Officiel Géographique is a register of administrative units — but it's also an accidental archive of how France was shaped: conquest, christianisation, rural consolidation, and the long French habit of resolving disambiguation with a hyphen.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Names are taken from the communeName field as registered in the Code Officiel Géographique 2023 vintage. A small number of merged communes may carry administrative compound names not in the COG; counts are as recorded in the source file.
