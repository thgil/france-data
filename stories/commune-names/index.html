What France is called

35,014 communes — one named Y, 202 named some variant of Saint-Martin, and the longest stretching to 45 characters. The names of France are a compressed map of its history.

2026-05-26 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. That is more than Germany, Italy, Spain, and Poland combined. It is a record so extreme that it sounds like a mistake — a relic of the 1789 Revolution, when the new republic drew administrative lines around every village, hamlet, and parish that already knew itself to be a place. Most of those lines have never moved. The communes have simply kept existing.

Each one has a name. Those names are not arbitrary. They encode centuries of settlement, religion, geography, and the particular French compulsion to describe exactly where you are in relation to everything around you.

The republic of saints

The single biggest naming convention in France is Christianity. Exactly 3,882 communes — one in nine — carry a saint's name. The most common patron: Saint-Martin. There are 202 communes named after the 4th-century bishop of Tours, though no two carry exactly the same full name. Saint-Martin-de-Crau (Bouches-du-Rhône), Saint-Martin-d'Hères (Isère), Saint-Martin-Boulogne (Pas-de-Calais) — each suffix pins it in place. The saint holds them together; the geography tells them apart.

Saint-Jean follows with 155 communes, Saint-Pierre with 144. Together, the top three saints account for more communes than the entire département of Moselle.

The problem with Sainte-Colombe

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have unique names. The remaining 2,250 share a name with at least one other commune. If you search for Sainte-Colombe without a département, you get twelve choices, spread across twelve different regions. Saint-Aubin appears ten times. Beaulieu — "beautiful place," appropriately generic — appears ten times too.

This is why French addresses use the five-digit INSEE commune code rather than just the name. The code is unambiguous. The name, frequently, is not.

Y

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter. Y is a commune in the Somme with 89 inhabitants, a church, and a name that predates legible etymology. The most likely origin is Germanic — from *iu or a related root meaning "water" or "river bend" — but the written record is sparse enough that linguists hedge their bets. What's certain is that Y has existed as a recognised place since the Middle Ages, and that it survived every attempt at rationalisation — the Revolution, the Napoleonic reforms, the endless 20th-century debates about municipal consolidation — with its single letter intact.

After Y, there are thirteen two-letter communes: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). The Pyrénées-Orientales alone contributes three — Py, Ur, Uz — a cluster of Catalan-origin names that have been worn down to almost nothing by centuries of French orthography.

The architecture of French place names

One of the most distinctive things about French commune names is how explicitly structural they are. The language insists on telling you where something is relative to everything else.

1,991 communes placed on something
(-sur-: a river, a hill, a road)
974 communes placed in something
(-en-: a valley, a forest, a plain)
257 communes sheltered
under something (-sous-)

The -sur- construction is everywhere: Vitry-sur-Seine, Conflans-Sainte-Honorine-sur-Seine, Bagneux-la-Fosse. Rivers do an enormous amount of structural work in French place names. Seine, Loire, Rhône, Garonne — each one organises dozens of communes along its banks, all of them politely indicating which bank, and at what feature, they occupy.

Then there are the 341 compounds joined by -et-: two former villages legally fused into a single name, the ampersand preserving the ghost of the smaller one. Escueillens-et-Saint-Just-de-Bélengard (Aude) sounds like a tongue-twister, but it records an actual administrative merger. The longer the name, the more history it contains.

All 45 characters

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne, at 45 characters including hyphens. It is the result of a mid-20th-century merger between three villages. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont kept its identity; Saint-Genest-et-Isson was absorbed alongside it. The resulting name is technically unwieldy but legally precise: no part of it is accidental, and each segment marks a place that existed before France decided to count its communes.

The runner-up: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône), at 43 characters. Four former communes, one name. A small town you can only describe at length.

The letter S

If you sort all 35,014 commune names by their first letter and plot the frequency, one column towers above the rest. S has 5,598 communes — more than A, B, and C combined, and more than any other letter by a wide margin. The reason is entirely the saints: most of the S-names are Saint-something or Sainte-something.

At the other end: X has only 21 communes. K has 77, mostly in Bretagne (Ker-, Kervignac, Kerdéniel) and Alsace. The letter W, appearing 196 times, is almost entirely Alsatian-origin names — Wintzenbach, Wœrth, Wissembourg. The linguistic substrates of France — Celtic, Germanic, Basque, Occitan, Latin — are all still visible in the first letter of a commune name, if you know what you're looking at.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures come from the DREES APL 2023 commune dataset, which covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories. A small number of communes may be missing or reflect recent municipal mergers. Name analysis was performed from the communeName field in the APL GeoJSON source file, which was originally built for the medical deserts story.
