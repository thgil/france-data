What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Over 4,400 mention a saint. And the longest name runs to 45 characters — with two saints in it.

2026-06-03 5 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the EU — 35,014 of them, each with its own name, mayor, and municipal council. Most of those names are quietly ancient: a saint, a river, a hill, a feature visible from the church door. Together they form a topographic and liturgical history of the country, compressed into a list.

Most of the names are unique. But not all. The name Sainte-Colombe belongs to twelve different communes scattered across twelve different départements, from Hautes-Alpes to Yonne. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu, ten times each. Of the 35,014 communes, 31,314 have a name that belongs to them alone. The other 3,700 share.

The shortest and the longest

Shortest name in France Y Somme · population 89
Longest name in France Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne · 45 characters · population 491

Y is a commune in the Somme with 89 residents. It is pronounced like the French letter i-grec (the Greek Y, as in the Greek settlers who may have given the area its name — though etymologists debate this). It shows up on maps as a single orthographic anomaly. There is also a commune called Eu in Seine-Maritime, and thirteen two-letter communes in total.

The other extreme is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 491 residents. It contains two saint names and three topographic references, strung together with hyphens. Names this long are almost always the product of a fusion de communes — two or more villages merged by administrative decree and forced to carry all their histories at once.

One commune in eight is named after a saint

France's communes were named, for the most part, in the medieval period, when the Catholic church structured daily life. The result is that 4,443 communes — one in eight — have saint somewhere in their name. That's 12.7% of the country's administrative geography dedicated to hagiography.

Among the saints, Martin leads by a wide margin: 202 communes begin with Saint-Martin. Martin of Tours, the 4th-century bishop who cut his cloak to share with a beggar, became France's most beloved patron. He is followed by Jean (155), Pierre (144), and Germain (108). Geography in the grammar

After the saints, the most common elements in commune names are prepositions that locate things in the landscape. -sur- (on, near a river or feature) appears in 2,007 commune names — Montpellier is technically short for Mons Pestellarius, but the more typical pattern is Nogent-sur-Marne, Joinville-sur-Marne, Pont-sur-Sambre. The preposition tells you where the water is.

2,007 communes use -sur- (on, near water) · 1,000 use -en- (in a region or forest) · 257 use -sous- (under a hill or château) · 2,140 start with Le / La / Les

-sous- is the most evocative: Neuilly-sous-Clermont, Nuits-sous-Ravières, Sainte-Sabine-sous-Longchamp. The "under" here is medieval: the village lying in the shadow of the château on the hill above, legally and literally beneath its lord. The preposition survived the lord by centuries.

The topographic words are also revealing. mont- or -mont (hill, mountain) appears in 1,529 names. val- (valley) in over 600. mer (sea) in 376 — not always coastal; sometimes it's an old word for a marsh. bois (wood) in 338. France's commune names are a pre-satellite gazetteer of the landscape.

The most common names

Among the 35,014 communes, 32,764 distinct names exist — meaning about 2,250 names are shared. The most duplicated names are predictably saintly. Sainte-Colombe (12 communes), Saint-Sauveur (11), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each). When you're naming a village after the local patron saint, your options are limited.

Name length distribution

The name length distribution skews short — the median is 10 characters, and the single biggest bin is 5–9 characters (15,582 communes). But the tail is long: 79 commune names exceed 25 characters, and one reaches 45. The average of 11.8 characters hides a lot of hyphenated saints.

35,014 windows onto medieval France

France has more communes than Germany, Spain, Italy, and Poland combined. They date mainly from the Revolution, when the Assemblée nationale constituante converted every parish into an administrative unit in 1789–1790 — roughly 44,000 communes at their peak, before decades of mergers reduced the number. The names they were given were, mostly, the names they already had.

Which means: when you look at the list, you're looking at what people in 800 CE thought was worth noting about a place. The saint they prayed to. The river they drank from. The hill that blocked the wind. The château that collected their grain. It's the longest-running open dataset in France. The government has been updating it, slowly, since the year the Bastille fell.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes are drawn from the APL dataset published by DREES (Direction de la recherche, des études, de l'évaluation et des statistiques), which covers 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base used in that dataset. Name analysis and statistics were computed from the communeName field in that source; a small number of Marseille and Lyon arrondissements are included in the total count.
