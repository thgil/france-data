What's in a Name?

35,014 French communes, 35,014 names — from Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. A linguistic portrait of the pays.

2026-06-02 5 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — its fundamental unit of local government, unchanged in structure since the Revolution. Each one carries an official name registered in the INSEE code géographique officiel. When you collect all 35,014 of them, something interesting appears: there are only 32,764 distinct names. About 2,250 names are shared by more than one commune. Which names repeat, which are unique, and which names push the limits of what a place name can be?

A seven-letter country

The most common length for a commune name is seven or eight characters. The distribution peaks there — not because of any rule, but because French place-name patterns (a saint name, a geographical feature, a river) tend toward that range naturally. The tail is long: over 5,000 communes have names longer than 20 characters, mostly due to the hyphenated compound names that describe location (-sur-, -sous-, -en-, -lès-) or the result of administrative mergers.

Y, and the alphabet's edge

One commune has a name that is exactly one letter long: Y, in the Somme (département 80), with 89 residents. It may be the shortest officially recognised place name in any country. Its origin is old Germanic — not the letter Y, but a monosyllabic root that survived two millennia of linguistic drift. Below it, thirteen communes share two-letter names: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz. All are pre-Latin or early Germanic survivors — sounds that existed before French existed.

At the other extreme sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters long, in the Marne (département 51). This is the longest commune name in France. It was created in 1972 by the administrative merger of three separate villages — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — whose councils could not agree on which name to drop. The result is all of them, hyphenated together. The merger increased the count; none of the names disappeared.

Saint Martin, everywhere

The single most visible pattern in French commune names is the saints. 3,898 communes — 11.1% of all communes — begin with Saint or Sainte. This is not a coincidence of history: it reflects the parish system that organised rural France for a thousand years. Every village had a patron saint; many villages were named after their patron. When the Revolution reorganised administrative units, those names survived.

Saint-Martin leads with 202 communes — the patron of soldiers, beggars, and, apparently, French geography. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow. The top fifteen saints account for about 1,300 communes between them.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most common single commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 communes in 12 different départements. Each one is its own village, its own mayor, its own administrative entity — they share nothing except a name and a long-dead patron saint. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu appear 10 times each.

The repetition is less surprising once you understand the naming logic: there are only so many saints, only so many geographical features. A country of 35,000 villages naming themselves after the same finite pool of saints and landscapes will inevitably produce duplicates. What's notable is how many names are unique — 32,764 out of 35,014. Even with 3,898 saint-prefixed names drawing from a small list of hagiography, the communes found ways to distinguish themselves: a river, a valley, a cardinal direction, a hill.

The grammar of French places

Beyond saints, there are patterns. 1,991 communes are named with a -sur- construction — Pont-sur-Yonne, Neuvy-sur-Loire, Crèvecoeur-sur-l'Escaut — signalling their position beside a river. The river becomes part of the name, distinguishing one Pont from another. 1,005 communes end in -ville (Old Norse by, meaning settlement) — a ghost of Viking settlement patterns in Normandy that spread inland. 700 end in -court (a Germanic hof, farmstead) — concentrated in the northeast, where Frankish settlement was densest.

These suffixes are linguistic strata: Gaulish roots, Roman additions, Frankish overlays, Viking coastlines, medieval Latin saints. The commune name is an archaeological record. Every syllable is a layer.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the INSEE code géographique officiel (COG), via the commune boundaries and APL data used in Story 2 (medical deserts). The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name lengths are measured in Unicode characters; the length of Oô is 2. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL dataset.
