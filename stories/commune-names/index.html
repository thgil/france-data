What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another has a 45-character name. Between them lies the whole history of French settlement, one saint at a time.

5 Jun 2026 4 min read

France names things twice: once when the village is founded, and again every time two communes merge and nobody can agree on what to call the result. Thirty-five thousand communes later, the naming record runs from a single letter to a hyphenated chain that barely fits on a road sign.

Y — just the letter, just the one — sits in the Somme valley between Amiens and Saint-Quentin. Population 89. It has been Y since before the modern French state existed; the name probably derives from the Old French word for water, aigue, worn down by a thousand years of local pronunciation. The name predates spelling committees. Thirteen other communes have two-letter names: Sy, By, Bû, Oô, Oz, and eight more, each a small etymological fossil.

At the other extreme, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (Marne, 491 residents) is the product of three villages deciding to merge in 1972 without any of them agreeing to disappear from the name. The second-longest, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters), follows the same logic: every hyphen is a negotiation.

The shape of French place names

The median commune name is 10 characters long; the mean is 11.8. The distribution has a clear mode around 7–9 characters — the natural length of a simple French toponym — and then a long right tail of hyphenated compounds that extends out to 45. Short names are survivors; long names are mergers.

That long tail matters. France has 43 communes with names exceeding 35 characters. All of them were formed by administrative mergers — communes nouvelles or older fusions — where the participants kept their names. The longest names cluster in Normandie, Bourgogne, and Champagne-Ardenne, regions that saw a wave of voluntary mergers in the 1970s. The short names cluster in the north and Alsace, where medieval settlement patterns persisted with less bureaucratic intervention.

One in eight communes is a saint

The single most common word in French commune names is saint, appearing in 4,090 of 35,014 communes — 11.7%. Strip the direction prefixes (La, Le, Les, Sur-) and the saints dominate even more clearly: Saint is the single most common first word in French commune names, appearing 3,596 times as a prefix alone.

The saint geography is uneven. In Ardèche (dept 07), 29% of communes carry saint names — nearly one in three. The Loire (42) runs at 27.8%, Creuse (23) at 28.2%. The pattern broadly follows the Massif Central and the Atlantic southwest: historically Catholic, early-Christianised, and with a fine-grained medieval settlement pattern that preserved village-level saints' dedications. The Paris Basin and the northeast, where parishes were larger and secularisation came earlier, have proportionally fewer.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The Catholic calendar had a finite number of saints. France had an almost infinite number of parishes. The result: the same names appear across the country, assigned independently by different parishes with no coordination. Sainte-Colombe is the most repeated commune name in France, with 12 occurrences spread across 9 different départements. No two are adjacent.

Saint-Sauveur (11 times) and Saint-Aubin (10 times) follow close behind. The pattern repeats across the top 20: saints, saints, and the occasional topographic term like Beaulieu (beautiful place, 10 occurrences) or Beaumont (beautiful hill, 8). These names were not chosen for distinction — they were chosen for piety, or for the landscape feature everyone agreed was most visible from the village.

Sitting on rivers, below mountains

After saint, the second most common word in commune names is sur — "on" or "atop" — appearing 2,007 times. French communes love to locate themselves relative to a river: Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, Villers-sur-Mer, Pont-sur-Yonne. The preposition sous ("below" or "under") appears only 258 times. French toponymy is aspirational: you are on the river, not under the mountain.

The full top-word list is a compressed geography of France: bois (forest, 217 times), val (valley, 130), mer (sea, 109), mont (mountain, 102), château (95), ville (93). Taken together they sketch the landscape that French settlers found, named, and then kept naming the same way for a thousand years.

A note on the data. Commune names, codes, and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which uses the INSEE commune reference at the 2021 population base. Name-length and frequency analysis is derived directly from the communeName field, which carries official INSEE spellings including diacritics and hyphens.
