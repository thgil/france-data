What's in a name?

From a hamlet called Y to a 45-character tangle in the Marne, France's 35,014 commune names span the accidental and the baroque.

Culture June 2026 35,014 communes

Somewhere in the Somme, there is a commune called Y. Not Why — just Y. One letter. Population 89. It has a church, a war memorial, and the shortest official place name in France.

The name is almost certainly derived from a local water feature — in Old French, y sometimes referred to a river or wetland, a usage that has vanished everywhere except on this one signpost. Eight centuries of administrative reform, two world wars, and five republics later, the village of Y still answers to its one-letter name.

It is not quite alone. France has thirteen other communes whose names run to just two letters: Bû in Eure-et-Loir, Eu in Seine-Maritime, Gy in Haute-Saône, Oô in Haute-Garonne (that second vowel carries a circumflex, adding a small topographic flourish to its minimalism). Together they form a strange roll of honour — names so short that an untrained eye assumes a typo.

The fourteen shortest commune names

The terse ones are mostly very small. Sy in the Ardennes has 54 people; By in Doubs, 86; Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées, 36. The exception is Eu in Seine-Maritime — a port town in Upper Normandy with 6,591 residents and a castle where Queen Victoria once stayed.

The most common name, twelve times over

At the other extreme is the question of repetition. France's 35,014 communes share just 32,764 distinct names — meaning about 2,250 names are used by more than one place. The title of "most common commune name in France" belongs to Sainte-Colombe, a name carried by twelve separate villages scattered across twelve different departments, from Hautes-Alpes to Charente-Maritime.

The list reads partly as a catalogue of medieval piety — saints Colombe, Sauveur, Aubin, Marcel, Michel, Hilaire — and partly as a pragmatic inventory of landscapes: Beaumont (beautiful hill), Beaulieu (beautiful place), Le Pin (the pine). Names that described a patch of ground so generically that a dozen different patches ended up with the same one.

One in nine communes bears a saint

3,898 communes — 11.1% of all communes — begin with Saint or Sainte. That is roughly one in nine.

The Christianisation of French place names runs deep. Under Charlemagne and through the feudal era, a new church or monastery became the anchor of a settlement, and the church's patron saint became its name. Some saints proved vastly more popular than others:

Saint-Martin's dominance is no accident. Martin of Tours (316–397 AD) was arguably the most popular saint in medieval France, patron of soldiers, vintners, and the poor. His name attached itself to hills, valleys, and crossroads all the way from Brittany to Provence — 203 of them, by the current register.

La, Le, Les — the definite article countryside

Beyond the saints, the definite article is France's other great naming shortcut. More than two thousand communes start with La, Le, or Les — the feminine, masculine, and plural forms of "the":

Many of these "the ___" names are hyper-local descriptors that once made immediate sense: Le Breuil (the marsh), La Chapelle (the chapel), Les Champs (the fields). Repeated a thousand times, they form a landscape vocabulary that is opaque to outsiders and instantly legible to anyone who grew up in the pays.

The other extreme

Then there is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne. It was created in 1972 by the merger of three communes — Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — and rather than choose a new name, the fusioning villages simply concatenated all three originals into one. The result is the longest commune name in France:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters  ·  Marne (51)  ·  Pop. 491

Five other communes have names of 40 characters or more — all of them formed by post-war mergers that chose the same additive solution. The average commune name in France is 11.8 characters. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is nearly four times that.

Somewhere between Y and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson lie 35,012 other names: 93.6% of them unique, 11% of them saints, and a handful carrying articles, circumflexes, and hyphens that encode a thousand years of French agricultural and religious geography.
