What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in Europe. One is called Y. There are 202 Saint-Martins. And the longest name runs to 45 characters.

2026-05-29 5 min read

Germany has around 11,000 municipalities. Italy has fewer than 8,000. Spain, 8,131. France has 35,014. More communes than all three combined, for a country roughly the same size. Each one is a legal entity, a postal code, a seat of local democracy — and each one has a name filed with the INSEE. Those names, in aggregate, are a compressed census of French history.

Y

In the Somme, 140 km north of Paris, sits a commune called Y. That is its full official name: one letter, registered with the INSEE since before the Revolution, population 89. It is the shortest commune name in France — possibly in the world.

Y is not alone in its brevity. France has 14 communes with names of two letters or fewer. They cluster geographically: the Pyrénées send three (Py, Ur, Uz), the Seine-Maritime two (Eu, Ry). Their names are mostly relics of Gaulish, Norse, or Occitan roots ground down over centuries of oral transmission until only a syllable remained.

One in nine communes is a saint

3,898 communes — 11.1% of France — begin with Saint or Sainte. This is the single most visible trace of the medieval Church in French geography. Every parish that survived the Revolution as a separate administrative unit kept its patron's name in the registry. They haven't changed since.

The most common saint's name, by far, is Martin. There are 202 Saint-Martins in France. Jean comes second with 155, Pierre third with 144. Together, the top three account for 501 communes — more than the entire commune count of Luxembourg.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

If you set aside the saint prefix and look for the most-repeated exact name, Sainte-Colombe wins with 12 communes. Then Saint-Sauveur (11), Saint-Aubin (10), Beaulieu (10). The secular names — Beaulieu, Le Pin, Beaumont, Verrières — reflect a different layer of French naming: the landscape itself. Beautiful hill, beautiful place, the oak grove, the ridge.

Driving from one Sainte-Colombe to the next would take you across eight different départements and about 1,200 km. You'd never visit the same commune twice — but you'd never quite leave the same name.

The communes that mean something

Some commune names are ordinary French words, stranded in the gazetteer when everyone stopped noticing they meant something. A town in Pas-de-Calais is called Bus (population: 133). The Somme has both Y and Rue (population: 3,111 — a real town, an actual rue named Rue). In Loir-et-Cher there is a commune called Mer. In Corrèze, Bar. In Charente, Ars.

Gap (Hautes-Alpes, population 40,500) is perhaps the least self-aware. It is the préfecture of its département — a genuine city — with a name that scans in English as an absence. In French it doesn't mean anything in particular; it's pre-Latin. The language has moved on. The map hasn't.

The 45-character name

At the other extreme: compound names, created when two or three communes merged but neither was willing to disappear entirely. The longest in France sits in the Marne:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (51) · population: 282

This commune was formed in 1972 by the merger of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. The name is the compromise: everyone kept their name, nobody was absorbed. The result is a postal address that barely fits on an envelope, and a commune that took three separate histories and insisted they coexist in alphabetical order.

It's followed closely by Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41, Calvados). France has at least a dozen communes with names over 35 characters — almost all of them the legacy of 20th-century mergers that nobody wanted to fully commit to.

The commune name is one of the most stable data points in French life. Arrondissements have been reorganised. Departments have been created and split. Regions have been merged by decree. But the communes — and their names — mostly persist. Y has been Y for longer than France has been a republic. Sainte-Colombe will be Sainte-Colombe in twelve different places when whatever comes next is built on top of this one.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes with INSEE commune codes and 2021 population estimates.
