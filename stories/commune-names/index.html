35,014 names

From Y (one letter, population 89) to a 45-character behemoth in the Marne — the hidden patterns in how France names its communes.

2026-05-15 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Every one of them has an official name — the kind printed on road signs, registered in the national code officiel géographique, and stamped on every birth certificate, marriage record, and property deed issued within its boundaries. These names range from ancient Celtic roots to Latin saints to bureaucratic fusions of hamlets that were stapled together in the 19th century. Pull them all into a list and the patterns are hard to miss.

One letter

Y
Commune de Y · Somme (80) · Population: 89

That is a complete French commune name. One letter. Y sits in the Somme département, about 20 kilometres east of Amiens, and has a population of 89. Its name is of uncertain etymology — most likely from a Frankish or pre-Roman root meaning something like "water" — and it holds the record as the shortest official place name in France. There are thirteen two-letter communes (By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz), but none beat the Somme's single letter.

At the other extreme sits the Marne:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 45 characters · Population: 491

Forty-five characters. The name is the administrative product of a 19th-century merger of two hamlets — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont and Saint-Genest-et-Isson — which were stitched together but kept their identities, hyphen and all. France has 29 communes with names over 30 characters, all of them compound mergers of this kind. The average commune name runs to 11.8 characters — long enough to be distinctive, short enough to fit a road sign without drama.

The Saint problem

France has 3,882 communes whose names begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That is 11.1% of all communes — one in nine — named after a Christian saint. If you add communes where "saint" appears elsewhere in the name (Saint-Denis-de-la-Réunion, Fontaine-Saint-Martin), the count rises to 4,443. No other country in Europe approaches this density of saintly place names at the commune level.

The saint names create a genuine navigation problem. There are 12 communes called Sainte-Colombe — spread across Ardèche, Calvados, Gard, Gironde, Haute-Garonne, Ille-et-Vilaine, Jura, Maine-et-Loire, Rhône, Saône-et-Loire, Seine-Maritime, and Vienne. Eleven communes share the name Saint-Sauveur. Ten go by Saint-Aubin. The code officiel géographique resolves this with a five-digit code (75056 for Paris, 80829 for Y), but the names themselves remain collision-prone. Ask anyone who has ever tried to book a venue in "Saint-Martin" with no further context.

Why names matter

A commune name is not just a label — it is the key to every administrative record France produces. The DREES medical access dataset, the INSEE census, the SIRENE business registry, the electoral rolls: all of them resolve to a commune code, and every commune code maps back to a name. The 35,014 names in this dataset are the address space of French civic life. The fact that 89 people live at address "Y" is not an accident of history — it is the system working exactly as intended, right down to the single letter.

A note on the data. Commune names and centroids are derived from the DREES APL commune-level dataset (2023 vintage), which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name strings are the official communeName field from the APL GeoJSON, sourced from the code officiel géographique (COG) maintained by INSEE. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL file. The communes-index.json file in this story folder contains the full list of 35,014 communes with names, codes, département, and polygon centroids.
