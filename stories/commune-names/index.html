What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes — but only 32,764 unique names. One is a single letter. Twelve share the same name. And nearly one in eight contains the word "saint."

2026-05-17 5 min read

The Code Officiel Géographique lists 35,014 communes in France — a number that includes every village, town, arrondissement, and overseas territory. Each one has an official name, assigned by the state and maintained by INSEE. When you count those names, something becomes clear: France ran out of ideas sometime around the tenth century and leaned heavily on the saints.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,700 share their name with at least one other. That's not a rounding error — it's 10.6% of all communes navigating the same identity problem. When you get a letter addressed to Mairie de Saint-Aubin, the postal service has nine options to choose from. Saint-Sauveur offers eleven.

The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, claimed by twelve separate communes scattered from Hautes-Alpes to Seine-et-Marne. Each one is real, each one is distinct, and none of them seems to have noticed the competition.

One in eight communes is a saint

France has 3,587 communes whose name begins with Saint- and 295 that begin with Sainte-. Add the ones where "saint" appears mid-name — like La Vacquerie-et-Saint-Martin-de-Castries — and the total reaches 4,443. That's 12.7% of all communes in the country.

This isn't purely religious heritage. Many of these names date from the revolutionary and post-revolutionary period, when communes were officially registered. A village that had informally called itself chez les Saint-Martin for centuries simply became Saint-Martin on paper. The proliferation wasn't devotion — it was bureaucratic inertia. By the time France noticed it had accumulated several hundred Saint-Martins, it was too late to do anything about it.

Y

France's shortest commune name is a single letter. Y is a village of 89 people in Somme (département 80, commune code 80829). It sits between Roye and Saint-Quentin, and its name — pronounced roughly like the English letter — has been official since at least the 13th century. It is not an abbreviation. It is not a typo. The village is simply called Y.

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. Most are in the north and east — consonant-heavy, monosyllabic, the kind of names that suggest an ancient language for which we've lost the dictionary. Oô in Haute-Garonne (31) is 103 people between two circumflexes. Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées (65) has 36 residents. The smallest by population is By in Doubs (25), a commune of 86 people whose entire existence fits on a standard licence plate.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

At the other extreme, France's longest commune name runs to 45 characters. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is in Marne (département 51), population 491, and it contains — naturally — two saints. The name formed when three smaller communes merged in 1972, each insisting on keeping its identity. The result is a name that won't fit in most database fields and has been causing trouble with forms ever since.

The distribution of names

The median commune name is about 12 characters. Most cluster between 8 and 20 characters — the length of a saint, a preposition, and a landscape feature. Short names (under 5 characters) are rare: only 168 communes have names that brief, and they're almost exclusively in the north and east. The very long names (over 30 characters) are nearly all merger names — the kind that result when two or three small communes fuse but nobody wants to be erased.

38.3% of communes have a hyphen in their name. 2,140 start with Le, La, or Les. The naming conventions are those of a landscape settled over a millennium: rivers, hills, patron saints, and the names of lords who've been dead for 700 years but whose geography persists.

None of this is administratively inconvenient in the way that matters. France navigates the twelve Sainte-Colombes fine — the five-digit commune code (the INSEE code) uniquely identifies every place in the country, and has since 1943. The names are for everyone else: for the road signs, the postmarks, the sense of place. Y is the shortest name on a road sign anywhere in France. Somewhere in Somme, that sign exists, and it says one thing: Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. The name analysis uses the communeName field as provided — official names as registered in the Code Officiel Géographique. Commune merge histories and population figures are from the same dataset (INSEE 2021 population base). The communes-index.json file (3.1 MB) in this story folder contains code, name, département, population, and centroid coordinates for all 35,014 communes.
