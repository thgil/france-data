What's in a commune name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Eleven percent start with Saint. And somewhere in the Marne, a village has a 45-character name.

2026-05-13 5 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. Not "Wy" or "Aye" — just the letter Y, written alone on envelopes and tax forms and road signs. It sits in the Somme, département 80, population 89. If you send a letter there, you write "Y" on the address line, and France understands.

Y Somme · pop. 89

Y is the shortest commune name in France. But it is not the only terse one. Thirteen communes make do with two letters. The two-letter club has a certain minimalist elegance: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz.

The shape of a commune name

The 35,014 communes of metropolitan France and its overseas territories carry 32,764 distinct names — which means 2,250 are duplicates. Most commune names are between five and twelve characters long, with the distribution peaking sharply at seven and eight. Below six characters, names become rare curiosities. Above twenty, they start to look like bureaucratic accidents.

The saint invasion

3,882
communes whose name begins with Saint- or Sainte-
— that's 11.1% of all communes in France

Roughly one in nine French communes is named after a saint. The practice runs deep: when the Catholic church planted parishes across medieval France, villages took their patron saint's name and kept it through every revolution and republic that followed. The 1793 déchristianisation briefly renamed many of them — "Saint-Hilaire" became "Montagne-la-Bonne-Eau" in some dossier — but most names crept back within a decade.

The most popular saint, by far, is Saint-Martin, patron of France and of the Frankish kings. The full roster of saint prefixes spans the Christian calendar from Advent to All Saints' Day. Saint-Sauveur — Holy Saviour — appears eleven times. Sainte-Colombe appears twelve: twelve separate communes, in twelve different départements, each one named after an early Christian martyr who is scarcely remembered anywhere else.

The name-sharing problem

If you address a letter to Sainte-Colombe, France will need more information. There are twelve of them. Twelve communes, spread across Ain, Ardèche, Aveyron, Côtes-d'Armor, Gard, Gironde, Haute-Saône, Isère, Lot, Rhône, Saône-et-Loire, and the Yonne — each one convinced it is the real Sainte-Colombe.

Across France, 1,450 distinct commune names are shared by at least two communes. That accounts for 3,700 communes — more than one in ten — who must distinguish themselves by appending their département, a river, a cardinal direction, or a hyphenated neighbour.

The long end of the tail

If the two-letter communes represent Gallic concision, the long end of the histogram tells a different story. These are place names assembled by merger, by committee, by the slow bureaucratic accretion of hamlets that refused to disappear into each other completely. They hyphenated their way into coexistence.

The longest commune name in France clocks in at 45 characters. It is in the Marne. It has six hyphens. It contains two saints.

The name exists because the commune was formed by the merger of several former villages, each one requiring its toponym to survive the union. A 45-character administrative address is the price of a successful merger negotiation.

Names as a document

Commune names are a geological record of French history. The Roman fundus at the origin of a Latinised suffix. The Frankish chieftain who donated land to the church and got a village named after his patron. The Revolutionary committee that renamed everywhere — briefly. The 19th-century merger that hyphenated two villages into one uneasy alliance.

35,014 communes. 32,764 names. One of them is just the letter Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are drawn from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to the Etalab administrative commune boundaries (2025 vintage), covering all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. The communes-index.json file (3.1 MB, 35,014 records) was derived from this data. Name lengths are computed from the official communeName field; special characters (accents, hyphens, apostrophes) are counted. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 base used in the APL dataset.
