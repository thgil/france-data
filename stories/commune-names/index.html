What's in a name?

35,014 commune names, from a single letter to 45 characters — and 1,450 names that belong to more than one place at once.

2026-05-28 5 min read

France has a commune called Y. One letter. It is in the Somme, département 80, a flat agricultural canton 25 kilometres east of Amiens. Population: 89. Its APL score (doctor-access) is 2.78, which makes it medically adequate. What it lacks in length it compensates for in administrative tidiness — the INSEE code is 80829, perfectly ordinary, and the commune gets its own prefect-certified stamp like any Sainte-Colombe or Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne. The French state does not discriminate by syllable count.

Thirteen communes manage two letters: Eu, Uz, Gy, Ur, Oô, Ri, Bû, Us, By, Oz, Ry, Sy, Py. Then 154 communes at three characters. The length distribution is roughly normal with a peak at seven to eight characters and a very long right tail — a few compound names that read more like legal descriptions than addresses.

The Saint problem

Of 35,014 communes, 3,882 — 11.1% — open with Saint- or Sainte-. France is, administratively, organized by the liturgical calendar. The saints arrive in the usual order: Sauveur, Aubin, Marcel, Michel, Pierre, Hilaire, Rémy, Clément, Loup, Georges. These are not random choices — they reflect the patron saints of the earliest Christian foundations in each region, written into administrative memory when the communes were formalised under the Revolution and confirmed by the decree of 1801.

The broader pattern: 38.3% of all communes — counting Saints, hyphens of all kinds — contain at least one hyphen. That's a structural feature of how compound topographical and hagiographic names were standardised in the 19th century. A commune with a hyphen is usually two things that merged: a village and a saint, a geography and a dynasty, or two former communes fused by a prefect who thought they were close enough.

The name collision

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 unique names. The missing 2,250 are duplicates: 1,450 different names each shared by two or more communes, covering 3,700 places in total. France Post had to invent the postal code partly because of this problem — you cannot put just "Saint-Aubin" on an envelope and expect it to arrive anywhere specific.

The record holder is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times, in 12 different départements: Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, Yonne. Twelve towns named for the same saint. Twelve mayors. Twelve sets of accounts. Twelve Fête de la Sainte-Colombe in September.

The champion of length

At the other end of the scale from Y, there is this:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) — 491 residents — 45 characters — 7 hyphens

This is a commune in the Marne, département 51, with 491 residents and an APL score of 4.09 (well-served). The name is the result of several historical fusions: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont was already a compound when it absorbed Saint-Genest-et-Isson at some point in the 19th or 20th century. The French administrative system did not require the new entity to pick a shorter name. It simply appended.

Runners-up: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 chars, Haute-Saône), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 chars, Calvados), and Roche-sur-Linotte-et-Sorans-les-Cordiers (40 chars, Haute-Saône). The Haute-Saône is evidently proud of its compound heritage.

The names are a historical document. They record which parishes were merged under which prefect's desk, which saints the local bishop favoured, and which river-bend or ridge-top gave the village its landmark before anyone thought to abbreviate. The French state has been indifferent to length for two centuries. The result is a dataset that runs from a single letter in a Somme field to a 45-character legal identifier in the Champagne chalk — and somewhere between them, 35,012 names that mostly make sense if you know what you're looking at.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes), which uses 2021 population figures. The dataset covers metropolitan France and overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte). Name analysis is based on the communeName field as supplied — official names including accents, apostrophes, and hyphens as standardised by INSEE. Population figures are from INSEE's 2021 commune-level estimates.
