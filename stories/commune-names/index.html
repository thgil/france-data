What's in a name?

35,014 communes, one letter, twelve Sainte-Colombes, and a 45-character administrative marathon.

2026-05-17 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Every village, every city, every commune nouvelle stitched together from mergers has an official name, recorded in the national INSEE registry with its own five-digit code. Most names are forgettable — Les Granges, La Plaine, Le Bourg — but the full list, taken together, is a compressed history of the country: its saints, its nobles, its rivers, its terrain, and occasionally its bureaucratic exhaustion.

Start with the shortest.

Y
Commune in the Somme (dept. 80) — population 89 — the shortest place name in France

The village of Y sits in the Somme, 120 km north of Paris, and has been called Y since at least the 12th century. Nobody is entirely sure why — the leading theory is that it's a corruption of an older word meaning "water" or "island," but the documentary record is thin and the locals appear unbothered. Population: 89. One letter. It's the only commune in France with a one-character name, and it has presumably been causing postal sorting problems for centuries.

The brief ones

Beyond Y, France has 13 communes with two-letter names — mostly in the south and east, where Basque, Occitan, and Alsatian roots produce compact syllables that don't expand well into French orthography. Some are the result of older names being administratively shortened; a few are simply how the place has always been known.

Longest commune names

The longest name belongs to a commune in the Marne:

45 characters. The name is the product of a 1972 merger that welded three separate villages — Saint-Rémy, Bouzemont, and Saint-Genest-et-Isson — into a single administrative unit. France created thousands of these communes nouvelles throughout the 1970s and again after 2010, with names that often concatenate the originals rather than coin something new. The result is a national address book full of compound nouns nobody can fit on a postcard.

Saint country

The single most dominant pattern in French commune names is the prefix Saint- or Sainte-. Of the 35,014 communes in the dataset, 3,882 — roughly one in nine — begin with a saint's name. France's Catholic history is literally inscribed in its administrative geography.

Same name, different place

2,250 communes share their exact name with at least one other commune somewhere in France. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe — there are twelve of them, scattered from the Loire to the Pyrénées to Burgundy. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin, ten.

This is how the French postal system earned its reputation. When you send a letter to Saint-Martin, you have to include the département code, because there are nine Saint-Martins and the postcode is the only thing that tells them apart.

35,014 names. 32,764 are unique. The rest are shared, doubled, tripled — the same word appearing in a dozen landscapes simultaneously, a dozen postmasters adding a département code with a sigh. The names are a layer of history you can read at a glance: saints for the faith, rivers for the terrain, -ville and -bourg and -mont for the settlers who named what they built. And then Y, for whatever reason Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes (metropolitan + overseas) with APL scores and population. The communes-index.json file in this story folder is a compact name + code + population index derived from that source. Name-length and duplicate analysis was done in Python.
