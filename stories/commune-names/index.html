The place called Y

France has 35,014 communes — more than the rest of the EU combined. Their names are a 1,000-year archive of saints, rivers, forests, and occasional mystery.

2026-05-21 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme whose official name is a single letter. It has 89 residents. Its INSEE code is 80829. It sits on land that saw some of the heaviest fighting of the First World War, and its name — Y — probably derives not from the alphabet but from a Picard dialect word for water, related to the Latin aqua. However it arrived, it stuck. Today, the commune called Y is the shortest place name in France.

France has 35,014 communes. Germany has about 11,000. Italy around 7,900. Spain under 8,000. The Netherlands fewer than 350. France's municipality count is an accident of the Revolution: in 1789, the Constituent Assembly turned every parish — every village with a church and a register — into a commune. Most other European nations spent the 20th century aggressively merging theirs. France largely didn't.

11% of France is named after a saint

The most immediately visible pattern in any sorted list of French communes is the saints. 3,882 of the 35,014 communes — 11.1% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. Every Catholic diocese maintained a roster of local patron saints; every parish took a name. The pattern holds most densely in the old ecclesiastical heartland of western and central France.

Ardèche leads, with 97 of its 335 communes (29%) named after saints — Saint-Agrève, Saint-Félicien, Saint-Martin-de-Valamas, dozens more. Creuse and Loire are close behind. The Alsace départements (Bas-Rhin, Haut-Rhin), by contrast, absorbed a Germanic naming tradition heavy on -heim and -willer suffixes. Paris — 20 arrondissements, all named by number — contributes nothing to the saint count at all.

Twelve villages called Sainte-Colombe

Because the saint names arose independently in each diocese, collisions were inevitable. Of the 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have a unique name: 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. The most common name in France is Sainte-Colombe — twelve different villages in eleven different départements. Saint-Sauveur comes second with eleven. The postal service has coped by appending disambiguation suffixes, which is why you have Saint-Aubin-des-Bois, Saint-Aubin-de-Médoc, and Saint-Aubin-sur-Mer all existing at the same time, all distinct communes.

A grammar of the landscape

The hyphen is the most productive element of French commune nomenclature. Of the 35,014 communes, 13,410 are hyphenated. And those hyphens typically carry a preposition that encodes a geographic relationship:

  • -sur- (on, by): 1,991 communes. Paris-sur-Avre, Boulogne-sur-Mer, Nogent-sur-Seine. The name says exactly where the town sits — on the river.
  • -lès- or -les- (near): 1,008 communes. Plessis-lès-Tours, Villiers-les-Hauts. The lès spelling is the medieval form, preserved by administrative tradition.
  • -en- (in, within a region): 974 communes. Bar-en-Fanges, Montoire-en-Brie. Usually a historical pays or natural region.

These prepositions weren't decorative: they were disambiguation tools, added to distinguish a town from its near-namesake. Today they're frozen in place, locked by INSEE codes and administrative history.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont

The range of name lengths — from 1 character to 45 — reflects two very different historical moments. The shortest names are pre-Latin, or Norse, or simply worn smooth by oral use over a thousand years: Y, Eu, Gy, Oz. The longest are almost all administrative mergers, two or more commune names stitched together by bureaucratic hyphen when a fusion de communes was formalized. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, at 45 characters, is the longest: three former communes, now one, with a name that takes practice to say.

The archive underfoot

French commune names are old. Many predate the language they're written in: Eu is probably Celtic; Bû may be Gaulish for hut or dwelling; dozens of Alsatian and Lorrain names retain Germanic roots that no French speaker would recognize as French. Others are dead Latin, fossilized in the administrative record. The Revolution renamed a handful of communes for ideological reasons (Mont-de-Terreur became Mont-de-Marsan once Thermidor was over), but mostly it left the names alone. It just decided that every one of them was a commune, officially, forever.

The result is a 35,000-entry archive of medieval geography, Catholic devotion, river toponymy, and the occasional bureaucratic accident. One of those entries is just the letter Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes in the original APL file, augmented to 35,014 via the administrative boundaries file). Name-length analysis and duplicate counting are computed from the full commune roster. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census (embedded in the APL dataset). The commune Y (code 80829) is real.
