What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. One has 45 characters in its name. The naming of French communes is a thousand years of history, compressed into a national gazetteer.

2026-05-10 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, 140 kilometres north of Paris, with 89 residents, a church, and the shortest place name in France: Y. That is its entire official name — one letter, no article, no hyphen. The commune code is 80829. It appears in the IGN mapping database, the INSEE census, and on road signs. It has a mayor.

Y is an extreme, but it is not as much of an outlier as you might think. France has thirteen communes with names of two characters or fewer — places called Sy, By, Bû, Oô, Oz, Ri, Uz, Py, Ur, Gy, Eu, Ry, Us — and 154 with three. The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The distribution peaks around 7–8 characters, then trails off into a long right tail of compound names grafted from multiple villages.

The shape of a commune name

Most commune names are short. More than half — 57.7% — are a single unhyphenated word. The mode is around seven or eight characters. But France has a strong tradition of fusing villages into hyphenated compounds, which drags the distribution right. A full 38.3% of commune names contain a hyphen; 2,250 names have three or more of them.

The shortest names

The thirteen two-character communes are a curiosity of French toponymy. Many are ancient, pre-Roman place names whose original meaning has been entirely compressed by centuries of phonetic change. Eu, in Seine-Maritime, was once a fortified town — it still has a château and about 6,600 residents. Gy in Haute-Saône (1,001 residents) is a historic market town. Y has 89.

One in nine communes is a saint

France has 3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of the total. In Dordogne alone (département 24), 130 communes carry a saint's name. Gironde has 121, Isère 106, Saône-et-Loire 105. The Dordogne figure reflects the region's medieval Catholicism: it was dense with priories and pilgrim routes, and each parish claimed its patron.

The most common saint name — Sainte-Colombe — appears twelve times, spread across twelve different départements from the Alps to the Atlantic. Twelve villages, each named for the same third-century martyred saint, each convinced its is the real one. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu ten times each.

Names shared across France

Of 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have unique names — meaning 2,250 communes share their name with at least one other. The most-duplicated names are almost all saint names or generic descriptors (Beaulieu, Le Pin, Verrières). This is not confusion — French addresses include the département code, and every commune has a unique five-digit INSEE code. But it does mean that if you search for "Saint-Sauveur" in a mapping app, you need to know which one.

The longest names

At the other extreme sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, in the Marne (51), population 491. It was formed by the merger of three villages, and each village insisted its name stay in. France had a wave of commune mergers throughout the twentieth century (and again in the 2010s under the loi NOTRe), and the hyphen was the diplomatic solution: no village name was erased, they were simply joined by a dash. The result can be magnificent.

The hyphen as history

The hyphen is a French civic compromise. When two or three villages were amalgamated — administratively, after a war, under a reform — rather than one name disappearing, both were kept, joined by a dash. The result is a geography that reads like a legal contract. Roche-sur-Linotte-et-Sorans-les-Cordiers (Haute-Saône, 64 residents). Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (also Haute-Saône, 923 residents). Haute-Saône has two of the five longest names in France — possibly because it had an unusually merger-prone nineteenth century, or possibly because its mayors had strong feelings about dashes.

38.3% of French commune names contain at least one hyphen. The median number of hyphens, for hyphenated names, is two. There is one commune name with seven hyphens. There is, of course, a commune named Y with none.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the INSEE commune index embedded in the DREES APL 2023 dataset, cross-referenced with the administrative boundaries file from data.gouv.fr. Coverage is 35,014 communes including overseas territories. The data represents the 2023–2025 vintage; names and codes reflect recent mergers.
