What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes, and their names tell the whole history — Catholic saints on every hilltop, rivers in a thousand hyphens, and one village that just calls itself Y.

Culture 5 min read May 2026
Y
commune · Somme (80) · population 89

That's it. That's the name. One letter. In the Somme, in northern France, there is a village called Y, and it has 89 residents, and their address genuinely ends in Y, 80190. It is officially the shortest commune name in France, and almost certainly in the world.

France has 35,014 communes. Their names run from a single letter to 45 characters, from the ancient to the absurd, from the piously Catholic to the geographically blunt. Pull on any name and you find a little history — of which saint the village priest admired, which river ran past, which two villages merged under some administrative decree in 1971 and had to keep both names hyphenated forever.

The tiny names

Thirteen communes manage two letters. One of them, Oô (Haute-Garonne), has two Os and a circumflex accent and 103 people in the Pyrenees. Another, Eu (Seine-Maritime), is the only French commune to share its name with a European Union preposition. Us (Val-d'Oise, 1,338 residents) and Oz (Isère, 213 residents) round out the set of genuinely puzzling candidates.

Where these short names come from is mostly lost. Y is thought to derive from a pre-Roman root. The Somme river gets its name from the same place. The village has been Y since at least the 13th century — nearly 800 years of one-letter mail.

The longest name

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 45 characters · population 491

At the other extreme, 491 people live in a commune that takes 45 characters to name. This is not a record — it's a symptom. French administrative history is littered with fusions, mergers of two or three villages into one commune, each merger obliged to carry all the old names hyphenated together. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is three former villages, now one commune, now one very long entry in the national registry.

The second-longest, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône. Three places. One postal code. Four proper nouns.

Two kinds of commune names

Name length distribution — all 35,014 communes

Count of communes by number of characters in the official name. The two humps reflect two naming traditions: short, medieval place names (peaks around 6–8 characters) and the long hyphenated Saint-X-sur-Y-et-Z fusions (the long tail).

The distribution is almost bimodal. Short names — three to eight characters — are old, worn smooth by centuries of use: Gap, Gex, Brest, Troyes, Rouen. The long names are administrative artifacts. When two villages merged, the new commune inherited both names, plus a hyphen, plus sometimes a preposition. The record is 45 characters. There are 55 communes with names longer than 28 characters.

One in nine communes is a saint

France is a secular republic, but you'd never guess it from the commune registry. 3,898 communes — one in nine — carry the name of a Catholic saint. Saint-Martin leads with 202 occurrences: there are 202 Saint-Martins scattered across France, each one named after the fourth-century bishop of Tours. Saint-Jean comes second with 155, Saint-Pierre third with 144.

The saints tend not to stay pure. Most Saint-Martin communes are actually Saint-Martin-something — Saint-Martin-de-Belleville, Saint-Martin-le-Gaillard, Saint-Martin-d'Hères. The saint's name is the prefix; the geography is the suffix. This structure — saint plus river, saint plus topography, saint plus cardinal direction — accounts for a vast portion of France's hyphenated name inventory.

The same name, in 12 different departments

The most duplicated commune name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times in 12 different departments — from Hautes-Alpes to Seine-et-Marne. They are unrelated, share no common origin (there are several Saint Colombas in Catholic hagiography), and have been independently named for centuries. If you post a letter to Sainte-Colombe with no department code, the post office has 12 equally valid answers.

Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times. All told, 4,832 names are shared by at least two communes somewhere in France — roughly 14% of all commune names.

The other patterns

Beyond saints, French commune names have a few other persistent structures. 1,991 communes have -sur- in their name — placed upon a river, a stream, a brook, a pond. There is a Saint-André-sur-Orne and a Saint-André-sur-Vieux-Jonc, a Château-sur-Epte and a Château-sur-Cher. The river names the place as much as the place names itself.

38% of all communes have at least one hyphen. In the Normandy and Pays de la Loire regions, that rises above 50% — a legacy of the dense network of small parishes, each one preserving its identity in a compound name rather than vanishing into a neighbour. The hyphen is France's administrative scar tissue.

Then there are the outliers that resist categorisation: Ô doesn't exist (that's Oô, two letters). % isn't a commune, just a bar in Paris that filed a very creative SIRENE registration. But Y is real. Eighty-nine people wake up every morning in a village with one letter on the sign, send emails with one letter in the address, and live — quite happily, one assumes — in the shortest place name in the French republic.
