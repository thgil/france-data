What's in a name?

35,014 communes. One called Y. Twelve called Sainte-Colombe. And a single name that takes 7 hyphens to spell out.

28 May 2026 5 min read Culture

Every French commune has a name. Obvious enough. But when you pull all 35,014 of them into a single list and sort by length, by frequency, by first word, the picture gets strange fast.

You find a commune that is literally named Y. You find twelve separate villages all named Sainte-Colombe. You find a town with a name long enough to fill a tweet on its own. And you find that more than one in nine French communes is named after a saint — a proportion that would surprise even the most devout parish priest.

This is a story about what France's map is made of, one name at a time.

One letter

Y
Commune of Y, Somme (80) — population 89

Y (pronounced "ee") sits in the Somme, south of Amiens, with 89 residents and what is almost certainly the shortest place name in France. It is not an abbreviation; it is the full, official name.

Below Y are 13 two-letter communes — from Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) to Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, pop. 36). Many cluster in Normandy and the Pyrénées, where old linguistic roots compressed syllables that longer French names elsewhere expanded.

At the other extreme, the longest commune name in France runs to 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 7 hyphens · 45 characters · population 253

That name — and many of the other long ones — is the result of administrative mergers. Two villages fused into one commune, and rather than pick a winner, bureaucrats joined the names together with a hyphen. The practice peaked in the 1970s under the Marcellin Law, which encouraged voluntary consolidations. The results are still on every road sign.

One in nine communes is a saint

The most common word in French commune names is not a preposition. It is not a geographical feature. It is saint — appearing in 4,090 of 35,014 commune names, far ahead of sur (2,007), les (799), and lès (546).

The word saint comes from the Latin sanctus — and in medieval France, villages were routinely placed under the protection of a local patron. The pattern is strongest in the old Catholic heartland of the Massif Central and the Atlantic coast. Ardèche (dept 07) leads all départements, with 97 of its 335 communes — 29% — carrying a saintly name. Creuse (28.2%), Loire (27.8%), and Haute-Vienne (27.2%) follow close behind.

No single name dominates

France has no "Springfield" — no single name that stands alone as the default. The top spot goes to Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 communes across 11 different départements. Saint-Sauveur follows at 11, and a cluster of saint names ties at 10.

What's striking is how thin the distribution is at the top: the most common name appears only 12 times in 35,014. English-speaking countries — with their New Yorks and New Londons — tend to replicate a small set of old-world names at scale. France recycled saints instead, giving each commune a distinct if formulaic identity.

But 3,700 communes share a name with someone else

Despite the variety at the top, 1,450 distinct names appear more than once — touching 3,700 communes in total. One in ten communes shares its name with at least one other commune somewhere in France.

The consequences are practical: French postal codes do a lot of disambiguation work. A letter addressed only to "Saint-Aubin" could arrive at any of 10 different villages in 9 different départements. The post office earns its keep.

The shape of all 35,014

Zoom out and the pattern is clear: most French commune names fall in the 6–15 character range, with a peak at 8 characters. Very short names (under 4 characters) are rare — Y is the only one-letter commune. Very long names (over 35 characters) are almost exclusively administrative mergers.

The letter frequency has its own texture: saint is the skeleton, sur and les are the connective tissue, and the actual geographical nouns — bois, pierre, chapelle, mont, mer — sketch a physical portrait of rural France. Mountains, rivers, chapels, and woods. The landscape is written into the addresses.
