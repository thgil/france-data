What's in a Name?

35,014 communes. One called Y. 3,898 saints. And one village with a 45-letter name.

2026-05-31 4 min read

France is carved into 35,014 communes — the country's smallest official administrative units. Each has a proper name registered with the INSEE, a postal code, a mayor, and a sign at each road entering the village. Most names are stately and descriptive: Saint-Laurent-de-la-Salle, Montigny-sur-Loing, La Chapelle-des-Fougeretz. A few are extraordinary. One is just a single letter.

Y

In the département of the Somme, north of Amiens, sits a commune of 89 inhabitants. It has a church, a mairie, and an official INSEE code (80829). Its name is a single character: Y. By most measures, it's the shortest place name in France and one of the shortest in the world.

Y is not the only short one. A further 13 communes have two-character names — Eu, Oz, Gy, By, Sy, Bû — most of them small villages with names that predate French as a language, rooted in Gaulish, Latin, or Germanic forms that survived centuries of administrative standardisation. Eu, with its 6,591 residents and a château that once hosted Queen Victoria, is the largest. Uz, in the Hautes-Pyrénées, is the smallest at 36 people.

The country of saints

France's communes weren't named by committee. They were named over centuries: by monks founding chapels, by lords asserting domains, by administrators standardising what already existed on the ground. The heaviest thumb on the scale was the Church. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,898 — one in nine — start with Saint or Sainte. No other country has this density of sainted place names.

The most common saint-name? Sainte-Colombe, which appears in 12 separate départements — from the Haute-Garonne to the Yonne, from the Seine-Maritime to the Gironde. If you ask someone in France where Sainte-Colombe is, they'd need to ask which one.

Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each) round out the top four. The pattern reveals something about medieval naming conventions: saints were picked for their local patronage, not for uniqueness. Two parishes on opposite sides of France could independently invoke the same saint centuries apart and end up with identical names. INSEE distinguishes them by their five-digit code. Signs at the village edge do not.

The long end of the tail

If Y is at one extreme, the other end belongs to a commune in the Marne. In full, it reads:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters · Marne (51) · population: 540

This is a commune nouvelle — created by a 1972 merger of previously separate villages. The name is the merger embodied: take all the old names, hyphenate them together, declare the problem solved. The result is the longest official commune name in France. Its second-place runner-up is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), followed by Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados).

A name is not enough to locate you

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,700 share their name with at least one other. That's more than one in ten. 1,450 distinct names are used more than once. If you're trying to navigate to Le Pin, you'll need the département too — nine different communes go by that name, scattered from Brittany to Gascony.

The five-digit INSEE code solves the problem cleanly: each commune has a unique one, and it encodes the département in its first two digits. But the codes were designed for data systems. The sign at the village boundary still just says Sainte-Colombe.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories.
