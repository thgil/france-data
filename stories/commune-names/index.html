The names of France

35,014 communes. Only 32,764 distinct names. One of them is just "Y." One is 45 characters long. And 202 are called Saint-Martin — so you'll need to specify.

2026-05-18 5 min read

Every French commune has a name. But names aren't unique. France's 35,014 communes share only 32,764 distinct ones — meaning 3,700 communes share a name with at least one other place. If you say "Sainte-Colombe" without specifying where, you could be talking about any of twelve different villages in twelve different départements.

This is not an accident. It's a product of how France was named: medieval parishes, Carolingian saints, natural features, administrative mergers. The names are a geographical fossil record of a thousand years of Christianity, feudalism, and rural settlement patterns.

The most-duplicated names

Sainte-Colombe wins the duplication race: twelve communes carry that name across as many départements — from Hautes-Alpes to Yonne. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear ten times. These are not obscure names. They're the common vocabulary of the landscape.

Most shared commune names

The pattern is telling. "Beaulieu" — beautiful place — appears ten times, in ten corners of France, because medieval people across the country independently found the same word for a valley they liked. "Beaumont" — beautiful hill — appears eight times for the same reason. The names describe the landscape, not the history; they recur because landscapes recur.

The saint problem

4,443 communes — 12.7% of all of France — have the word saint or sainte in their name. The country runs on saints. But which saints? Saint-Martin dominates: 202 communes are named after the 4th-century bishop of Tours who is the patron saint of France. Say "Saint-Martin" on a French map and you'll need to be more specific.

Most common saints in commune names

Saint-Jean (155), Saint-Pierre (144), Saint-Germain (108) — the roster reads like the first chapter of a medieval hagiography. The saints aren't distributed randomly: Saint-Martin and Saint-Jean are spread nationwide; Saint-Gilles clusters in the south; Saint-Brieuc, Saint-Pol, Saint-Tugdual belong to Brittany's Breton-Christian tradition. The saints map the history of conversion and colonisation, village by village.

Two-letter clubs

Eu, Gy, Uz, By, Oz, Sy…

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the largest; Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, pop. 36) the smallest. Several are Norse in origin — the -y and -ou suffixes trace Viking settlement routes inland from Normandy.

A fossil record

The naming of communes is a stratigraphic record. At the bottom: pre-Roman names, Gaulish or Ibero-Basque or Breton, surviving on the periphery — Uz, Eu, Gy. Above that: the Gallo-Roman layer — -ac and -anum suffixes in the south, -y in the north. Then the Christian layer — 4,443 saints' names. Then the feudal layer — Beaumont, Villeneuve, Montfort. Finally: the merger layer, still being written today, concatenating old names into administrative hyphens.

The total: 35,014 data points, each one a name, each name a political choice about whose history to honour. Twelve villages called Sainte-Colombe, 202 called Saint-Martin, one village called Y. All France, named.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 34,886 communes with APL scores. A small number of additional communes (128) without APL data are included from the same source file. Total: 35,014 features. Centroids are approximated as the mean coordinate of each commune polygon's exterior ring — suitable for display but not for precise centroid calculations.
