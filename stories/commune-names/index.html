What's in a French commune name?

35,014 communes. One called Y. 3,891 saints. And a hamlet in Marne with a 45-character postal fate.

2026-05-22 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Most of them have perfectly ordinary names — Beaumont, La Chapelle, Villeneuve. But the edges of the distribution are something else. One commune is named Y. Another has a name long enough to fill a line of verse. And more than one in ten communes is, officially, a saint.

One letter is enough

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter: Y. It sits in the Somme département (80), population 89. The name comes from a Gaulish word meaning water or river, filtered through centuries of scribal economy. In the age of postcodes and GPS, Y, Somme is perfectly unambiguous — but imagine explaining it over the phone.

A country of saints

France has 3,891 communes whose names begin with Saint or Sainte — exactly 11.1% of the total. That's one in nine. The phenomenon is ancient: Christianisation ran deep into the landscape, and every local chapel, every martyr, every obscure confessor became a place name that subsequent reforms never quite managed to erase. The Revolution tried: for a few years in the 1790s, communes were required to drop their saints' names. Most changed back within a decade.

The saints are not evenly distributed. Normandie, Bretagne, and the old dioceses of the south-west produce the densest saint-clusters. The Île-de-France basin is comparatively secular. You can read the old diocesan boundaries in the naming patterns, if you look.

The name clash problem

With 35,014 communes and only 32,764 distinct names, 1,450 names are shared by more than one commune. The most duplicated name in France is Sainte-Colombe, used by twelve separate communes in twelve different départements. If you say you're from Sainte-Colombe, you need to add the département. Always.

The runner-up is Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), followed by Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each). The pattern is overwhelmingly saints and landscape terms — beauty (Beaulieu, Beaumont), trees (Le Pin), and a dozen flavours of blessed protector.

The long game

At the other extreme is a commune in Marne that sits at 45 characters and clearly had a very productive municipal merger:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 45 characters · formed by the merger of three communes

French commune mergers — fusions de communes — create these baroque hyphenated constructions. The logic is civic dignity: each constituent village insists its name survive. The result is a postal address that challenges every form field ever designed. The second longest, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), sounds like the name of a law firm.

The shape of a name

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The median is 10. Most names cluster between 6 and 18 characters, with a long tail on the right — the merged municipalities dragging the distribution toward baroque lengths. The histogram below shows the full spread.

The sharp peak around 8–12 characters covers the canonical French commune name: a landscape term, a colour, a saint in a manageable hyphenation. From there the drop-off is steep. Fewer than 200 communes exceed 30 characters. Fewer than 30 exceed 40.

France's communes are a living archive of the country's linguistic and ecclesiastical history, written out across the map in 35,014 entries. Most names are dignified and forgettable. A handful are memorable for all the wrong reasons. And one — in the Somme, population 89 — is just a single letter, standing alone, entirely sufficient.
