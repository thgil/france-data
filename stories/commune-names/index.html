What's in a name?

35,014 communes, from a single letter to a 45-character behemoth. And 3,898 of them start with Saint.

2026-05-23 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — villages, towns, arrondissements, and a handful of administrative curiosities. They range from microscopic to sprawling, from ancient to merged-last-Tuesday. But the most striking fact about them isn't their size or age. It's their names.

One of them is named Y.

Y A commune in Somme (dept. 80), pop. 89. The shortest commune name in France — and, arguably, the world.

Y sits in the Somme département, northeast of Paris. Population 89. It appears on maps as a single capital letter, which causes obvious problems for search engines, postal systems, and anyone trying to explain where they live at a dinner party. Its name comes from the Old French word for a place near water — ye, from a root shared with the English place-name suffix -ey (as in Putney, Bermondsey). The letter just stuck.

The two-letter club

Y isn't alone in brevity. Thirteen other communes manage two letters — a small, scattered club of minimalist municipalities, mostly in the north and southwest.

The two-letter names cluster in two geographic zones: the Norman and Picard plains (Eu, Ry, By, Bû, Ri) and the Pyrenean foothills (Py, Ur, Uz, Oô). The spelling is often archaic — Bû carries a circumflex that signals a dropped letter, historically Busc or Bus. Oô, in Haute-Garonne, contains a ligature rarely seen outside Tolkien. And Eu — a port town of 6,591 people in Seine-Maritime — has an airport code (XEU) that looks like it was generated by a drunk algorithm.

The shape of a commune name

Most French commune names fall between 6 and 20 characters. The distribution is roughly bell-shaped with a long right tail — a handful of names stretch so far they begin to resemble sentences.

The longest name in France belongs to a commune in Marne (dept. 51): a 2016 merger that combined two villages and their histories into a single official name.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (51) · pop. 491 · formed 2016 by the merger of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont and Saint-Genest-et-Isson

It's not uniquely cursed. Four other communes exceed 40 characters, all formed in the wave of post-2015 municipal mergers encouraged by the Loi NOTRe. When two villages merge but neither wants to disappear, the solution is sometimes just to concatenate. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados) testify to this particular brand of democratic stubbornness.

The army of saints

The most pervasive pattern in French commune names isn't a letter — it's a prefix. Saint- and Sainte- appear 3,898 times across the 35,014 communes. That's 11.1% of France, dedicated to one saint or another.

The saints aren't concentrated — they're distributed across every département in France. Saint-Pierre alone appears nine times. Saint-Michel too. The champion is Sainte-Colombe: twelve communes across twelve different départements share that name, from Charentes-Maritime to the Rhône, from Eure-et-Loir to the Yonne. If you say you're from Sainte-Colombe, the follow-up question is always which one?

Which one?

The French Republic has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names. The remaining 2,250 slots are filled by repetition: 1,450 names are shared by two or more communes, accounting for 3,700 communes in total. One in ten French communes shares its name with at least one other.

This isn't purely a saint problem. Beaulieu (beautiful place) appears 10 times. Le Pin (the pine) nine times. Beaumont eight. Verrières eight. These are the generic, optimistic, geographically generic names that medieval villages converged on independently: a beautiful view, a hilltop, a nearby pine tree. Each one thought it was the only Beaumont. It wasn't.

A commune's name is its compressed history: a saint venerated during the Carolingians, a landform that predates Latin, a merger negotiated in 2016 over a conference table in a sous-préfecture. Taken together, 35,014 of them form a kind of compressed atlas — every habitation pattern, every linguistic stratum, every bureaucratic compromise France has ever made, expressed as a word. Or, in one exceptional case, as a letter.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the APL (accessibilité potentielle localisée) 2023 dataset published by DREES, which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Population figures use the 2021 INSEE census base. The full communes index (name, code, département, population, centroid) is available as communes-index.json (3 MB).
