The name of the place

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. One has 45 characters and two saints in it.

2026-05-14 5 min read

Every one of France's 35,014 communes has an official name. Most are pleasantly forgettable — Vieille-Chapelle, Fontaine-le-Comte, Sainte-Foy-de-Longas. And then there is Y.

Y is a commune in the Somme, département 80, population 89. Its name is one letter. It has a post office (code: 80700), a mairie, and presumably a sign at the roadside that tourists photograph. The administrative machinery of the French Republic treats it identically to everywhere else: it has an INSEE code (80829), an APL score (1.33 — a medical desert), and a full polygon on every government mapping service in the country.

Y is not alone at the short end. France has 13 communes with two-letter names — a small, strange club spread across nine départements:

Most are in mountainous or heavily rural territory where Occitan, Breton, or Basque roots produce short, consonant-heavy syllables. Eu, with 6,591 residents, is the outlier — a real town on the Normandy coast with a medieval château and a train station. The others run between 36 and 2,022 people. Oô, up in the Pyrenees, has 103.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

At the other end of the frequency table sits Sainte-Colombe. Twelve separate communes share that exact name, scattered across the country — in Ardèche, Charente, Côtes-d'Armor, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Lot, Rhône, Saône-et-Loire, Seine-Maritime, Somme, Vosges, and Yonne. They have nothing in common except a third-century martyr named Columba of Sens, venerated across Frankish Gaul, whose cult seeded parishes as Christianity spread. Each parish became a commune after the Revolution. The Revolution standardised the administrative grid; the saints stayed.

The pattern holds across the top of the list. Of the 15 most common commune names in France, 11 begin with Saint- or Sainte-. The two outliers that sneak in — Beaulieu and Beaumont — are geographic descriptions so generic they landed independently in dozens of valleys. Beau view, beau hill. France had plenty of both.

The saint factory

France has 3,882 communes whose names begin with Saint- or Sainte- — that's 11.1% of all communes. They account for the single most common word in the entire corpus of commune names: "saint" appears 4,090 times across 35,014 names. The runner-up is "sur" at 2,007 — reflecting the second great naming convention, geography.

The full saint picture is odd in a way the raw count doesn't capture. France has a Saint-Martin in the Antilles (a collectivité), and nine communes called Saint-Martin scattered across metropolitan France alone. It has a Saint-Rémy-de-Provence (population ~10,000, a significant tourist draw), and eight other communes just called Saint-Rémy with no further qualifier. Many saints are not saints at all in the ecclesiastical sense — "Saint-Clair" in some villages derives from the Latin clarus (clear water), not from any martyr. The names accreted over centuries, and the Revolution's project to rename them all Liberté-sur-Mer and Montagne-de-la-Fraternité failed comprehensively. Most communes reverted within a generation.

Sitting on things

1,991 communes are named after their position on a body of water — they are something-sur-something. What they sit on follows the French river system almost perfectly. The Loire and Seine are tied at 70 communes each. The Marne has 55, the Meuse 43, the Saône 34. Then, well ahead of any single river, comes the sea itself: 99 communes are called something-sur-Mer. The Atlantic coast, the Channel, the Mediterranean — all of them named by communities who wanted to tell you, simply, that they were on the water.

The full table of things communes sit on is effectively a hydraulic map of France — which rivers were large enough, navigable enough, or dangerous enough to define a settlement. The Moselle has 17, the Garonne 16, the Oise 16. The Rhône, one of France's most powerful rivers, has fewer because Rhône-side communes often adopted the region's name (Rhône, Ardèche) rather than the river. Naming conventions have their own logic.

45 characters, two saints, one village

At the far end of the length spectrum sits the longest commune name in France's administrative record:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

Marne · département 51 · 45 characters · population approx. 160

The name is the product of a commune merger — two old parishes folded into one administrative unit, each insisting on keeping its identity in the name. France has hundreds of these hyphenated compounds created during the fusion de communes programme, but most stop at one or two additions. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson kept going. The et-Isson at the end is the third piece: a hamlet that also asked to be named. The result is a name you need to say twice to remember and three times to pronounce correctly on the first attempt.

Runner-up at 43 characters is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône — another merger, another list of villages that refused to disappear quietly into bureaucratic anonymity.

The shape of a French name

Across all 35,014 communes, the average name is 11.8 characters long. The most common lengths are 7 and 8 characters — short, single-word names like Morteau, Lorient, Salins, Auriol. But the distribution has an unusually long tail: the compound saint names and merger constructions drag it well past what you'd see in most countries. The long tail isn't just a curiosity — it's evidence of the French state's unusual tolerance for administrative specificity. France never imposed a maximum length on commune names. Germany caps Gemeinde names informally by convention; anglophone countries tend to abbreviate on maps. France just lets the names be what they are. The postman, presumably, adapts.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 metropolitan and overseas French communes with their INSEE codes, names, populations (2021 base), and APL scores. The APL data was used in Story 2 (medical deserts); the commune names are extracted here for the first time. Name length counts are character-counts on the official communeName field as supplied by DREES/INSEE.
