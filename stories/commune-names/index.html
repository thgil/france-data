What's in a French name?

France has 35,014 commune names. One is a single letter. One is 45 letters long. And one in nine starts with "Saint."

May 2026 4 min read 35,014 communes

Every place has a name, but France takes the naming of places very seriously. Across 35,014 communes — the basic administrative unit of the Republic — you find a lexicon that spans ancient Latin saints, medieval geography, Breton roots, Occitan hills, and at least one town that gave up entirely and called itself Y.

The commune name dataset, derived from the INSEE commune registry, holds the full inventory: 35,014 names, ranging in length from one character to forty-five, and ranging in ambition from "Uz" to "Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson." Here is what that inventory looks like.

The shortest name in France — and probably the world

There is a commune in the Somme, 110 km north of Paris, whose full official name fits in a single keystroke.

Y

Commune de Y, Somme (80)
Population: 89 inhabitants
Postal code: 80440
Postal address: "Y, 80440 Y"

Y (pronounced like the French letter, roughly "ee-grek") has a population of 89 and sits on the Noye river. Its one-letter name is almost certainly the shortest official commune name in the world — the Oxford Handbook of Place-Name Studies includes it as an outlier. The INSEE code is 80829. The postman writes three letters.

Thirteen communes have two-letter names: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Their populations range from 36 (Uz) to 6,591 (Eu).

Saint and its 3,898 followers

The most common opening word in a French commune name is not La, not Le, and not anything geographical. It is Saint — by a distance that is almost comical.

The word "saint" (in both Saint- and Sainte- prefixes) appears in roughly 4,090 commune names — more than twice the next runner-up. Martin is the most popular saint's name: 255 communes carry it. Then come Pierre (180), Jean (170), Germain (120), and Julien (88).

In Ardèche (département 07), 29% of all communes start with Saint or Sainte — nearly one in three. The Creuse (23), Loire (42), and Haute-Vienne (87) all exceed 27%. These are the old heartlands of rural Roman Catholicism, where medieval parish geography froze into administrative geography and stayed.

The most replicated exact name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears twelve times. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Saint-Marcel each appear ten. Each instance is a distinct commune, with its own mayor and INSEE code, in a different part of France — though if you ask your GPS for "Saint-Aubin," you will need to specify which one.

France and its hyphen obsession

Of the 35,014 commune names, 13,410 — or 38.3% — contain at least one hyphen. Hyphens are how French commune names absorb mergers, double geography ("Saint-Martin-de-Ré"), and the habit of specifying the hill or river that makes your Saint-Martin different from the other nine nearby ones.

At one end of the hyphen spectrum: the shortest names, like Y. At the other end:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (51) · population 491

This commune in the Marne contains two saints, one river name (Bouzemont), one village (Genest), and one hamlet (Isson), fused together after a 1972 merger. It is the longest commune name in France at 45 characters. The postcode is 51290. Writing a return address is a commitment.

The next longest, at 43 characters: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, pop. 779). Then Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 chars, Calvados) and Saint-Laurent-les-Bains-Laval-d'Aurelle (39 chars, Ardèche). All are merger products — France consolidated thousands of tiny communes in the 1970s under the Marcellin law, and many chose to preserve every component name rather than pick a winner.

The name length landscape

The modal commune name is 7 or 8 characters long — that's where the distribution peaks, at 3,695 communes each. But the distribution has a long right tail: 500+ communes have names over 24 characters.

The most common names

Out of 35,014 communes, only 32,764 distinct names exist — 2,250 names are shared by two or more communes. That means 1,450 names are duplicated at least once. The twelve Saint-Colombes are not an anomaly; they are the extreme of a general pattern of naming convergence.

A lexicon of France

Taken together, the 35,014 commune names form an accidental dictionary of French geography, religion, and history. Bois (217 communes) and Mer (109) mark the old landscape. Chapelle (178) and Martin (255) mark the church. Villeneuve (85) marks medieval town-founding. Val (130) marks the river valleys. And somewhere in the Somme, contributing its single letter to all of this, sits Y — population 89, asking nothing and giving everything away in one keystroke.
