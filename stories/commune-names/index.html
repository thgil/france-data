What's in a commune name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Two hundred are called Saint-Martin. The rest tell their own stories.

The shortest name in France is a single letter

There is a commune in the Somme called Y. Just Y. Population 89. It sits in a flat northern plain, unremarkable in every way except its name — the shortest in France. It has appeared in French atlases for centuries, and it still confounds postal software and puzzles address-autocomplete forms today.

Y isn't alone in its economy of letters. Thirteen other communes manage two characters. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the largest of them — it was once home to a royal château. Oz (Isère) is a ski resort. Oô (Haute-Garonne) sounds like an exclamation. Gy (Haute-Saône) is a small market town. The Pyrénées-Orientales has both Py and Ur, which sound like they belong in a linguistics textbook.

One commune in nine is a saint

France has 3,882 communes whose name begins with Saint- or Sainte- — 11.1% of the total. This is not coincidence. It's eight centuries of the Catholic Church naming parishes after patron saints, and those names outlasting the institution that created them by hundreds of years. The Revolution tried to rename many of them (Saint-Martin became Montagne-la-Montagne for a few years in the 1790s), but the saints came back.

The most common: Saint-Martin, with 202 communes. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow. If someone tells you they're from Saint-Martin, ask which one — there are 202 of them, spread across every corner of France, from the Pyrénées to Normandy to Guyane.

When the same name appears twelve times

The most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, appearing in 12 different communes across 10 départements. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times. In total, 1,450 distinct names are shared by 3,700 communes. This is why French addresses always include the département — the name alone doesn't disambiguate.

The rivers are written into the addresses

France has 2,248 communes that locate themselves by a river or elevation using -sur- (on the) or -sous- (below the). Bar-sur-Aube, Nogent-sur-Seine, Villeneuve-sur-Lot — these names are geographic facts embedded in the postal system. The most common last word across all commune names is bois (wood, 178 communes), followed by mer (sea, 108), seine (77), and loire (74). France's two great rivers run through 151 commune names combined.

Of 35,014 commune names, 13,410 contain at least one hyphen — 38% of all communes. The average hyphenated name has 1.87 hyphens. The record holder has seven.

Seven hyphens, 45 characters

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

Marne — 45 characters, 7 hyphens, population 491

The longest commune name in France is a document of merger politics. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (Marne) was created when three villages fused into one administrative unit — and rather than pick a winner, they kept everyone's name. At 45 characters and 7 hyphens, it's the record holder. Second place goes to Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters) — same story, same logic.

The fusion-de-communes process that created these marathon names has been accelerating since 2015, when France began offering financial incentives for mergers. There will likely be longer names in future editions of this dataset.

The commune gazetteer is a historical document masquerading as a postal list. Every saint's name is a medieval parish. Every -sur-Seine is a 12th-century description of where you'd find the village. Every hyphenated compound is a modern merger that tried to be fair. And Y — Y is just Y, a Norse or Gallic syllable that stuck.

The same administrative grid that maps France's medical deserts and bar counts also maps its vocabulary. The names are data too.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes) joined with commune boundary polygons from the data.gouv.fr administrative contours file. The resulting dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Centroids are computed as the mean coordinate of each commune's polygon ring vertices — a simple approximation suitable for reference but not for precise distance calculations. Name analysis was done in Python from the same GeoJSON used in the medical deserts story.
