What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. The average commune name is 11.8 characters long — and one municipality in the Marne is called something that takes a full breath to say.

2026-05-15 5 min read

The French commune is an old institution — older than the Revolution that codified it, rooted in parishes and market towns that go back to the Middle Ages. France has been adding, splitting, and merging communes ever since, and each one has kept its name, or acquired a new one, or sometimes been hyphenated into something that no longer fits on a sign. The names are a fossil record: saints, landscapes, rivers, kings, hamlets absorbed by their neighbours and reborn as half of a hyphen.

The DREES APL dataset covers 35,014 of France's communes, each one with a full official name, an INSEE code, and a département. Running the names through a basic frequency and length analysis surfaces a few things worth knowing.

Y

Y
The shortest commune name in France — and reportedly in the world. Somme (80). Population: 89.

Y is a village in the Somme, département 80, with 89 residents and an APL score of 2.78 — barely above the medical-desert threshold. Its single-letter name has been certified by the Guinness Book of Records as the shortest official place name in the world. The name almost certainly derives from a Gaulish word for water or a stream, filtered through centuries of phonetic compression until only the vowel remained.

Y has no particular claim to fame beyond its name, which turns out to be enough. Every French administrative database has to accommodate it. Every lookup table that assumes a minimum name length is wrong.

The two-letter club

Beyond Y, France has exactly 13 communes with two-character names. They are spread across a dozen départements and share a common quality: they sound like a single exhalation. Most end in a vowel or a soft consonant. Many are in mountainous or rural regions where Gaulish, Basque, Occitan, and Alsatian roots survived the standardisation of French.

Eu (Seine-Maritime, 6,591 residents) is the largest — a medieval port town on the Bresle river that gave its name to the Hundred Years' War treaty signed there in 1475. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, 36 residents) is the smallest, a mountain hamlet whose name is almost certainly Basque. Oô (Haute-Garonne, 103 residents) is the one that always stops people: two vowels, one of them accented, a name that looks like a typo and isn't.

The Saint epidemic

3,882 of France's 35,014 communes — 11.1% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. This is not evenly distributed: Brittany, Normandy, Burgundy, and the Massif Central are saturated with saints. The Île-de-France is comparatively secular. The naming pattern reflects Christianisation, which reached different regions at different rates and left a denser topographical imprint where the Church's grip persisted longest.

The most common saint name, repeated across 12 separate communes, is Sainte-Colombe. There is no single famous Sainte Colombe — several saints bore the name — which may be why 12 different villages reached for it independently. The runner-up, Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), is more theologically specific: it means "Holy Saviour," and it clusters near pilgrimage routes.

    Of the 3,882 saint-prefixed commune names, 3,376 are unique — meaning the vast majority of saints in the French communal map appear only once. France has given itself 3,376 distinct saintly postal addresses.

    Name length: a long tail

    The average French commune name is 11.8 characters. The distribution peaks at 7–8 characters, then develops a long right tail as hyphenated names — often the result of two or more communes merging and keeping both identities — pile up past 20, 30, even 40 characters.

    The peak at 7–8 characters is the "normal" French commune: a single word derived from a Roman settlement name, a landscape feature, or a saint stripped of their prefix. Everything to the right of 15 characters is almost always a hyphenated compound — and the further right you go, the more likely it is that you're looking at a commune formed by administrative merger, where both original names were preserved and joined with a dash.

    The monsters

    The longest commune name in France, at 45 characters, is:

    Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (51) · 306 residents · Formed by the merger of three villages in 1972

    This is what happens when three communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — are consolidated into one and nobody agrees to shorten anything. The result is a hyphen-linked triple-barrel name that no GPS voice pronounces convincingly. The runner-up, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône: four villages, one sign.

    The name everyone chose

    Outside the Saint/Sainte prefix, certain base names also repeat. Beaulieu (beautiful place) appears 10 times. Le Pin (the pine) appears 9 times. Beaumont (beautiful hill) 8 times. These are names that describe a landscape so common across France — a pine tree, a hilltop, a beautiful view — that dozens of unrelated villages reached for them independently, centuries apart, with no way to know the name was already taken elsewhere. There was no central register of commune names until the Revolution standardised the Code Officiel Géographique. By then it was too late.

    The result is that French addresses, uniquely among European countries, require the INSEE commune code (a 5-digit number) to be unambiguous. The name alone will not do. There are 12 Sainte-Colombes, 10 Beaulieus, and somewhere, a village called Y that fits in a single character and is unique precisely because it is too short for anyone else to have wanted it.

    A note on the data. Name analysis is based on the 35,014 commune records in the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all metropolitan communes and overseas territories. A small number of merged communes or special-status territories may be absent. The communes-index.json file published with this story provides a compact lookup table — name, INSEE code, département, centroid — for use in future stories.
