What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. And the longest name fits seven hyphens into 45 characters.

May 2026 · 5 min read · Data: INSEE communes 2025
Y Y — commune in the Somme, Hauts-de-France · population 89 · INSEE code 80829

There is a village in the Somme called Y. Not a placeholder. Not an abbreviation. Just Y — one letter, valid since the Middle Ages, now home to 89 people and an improbable amount of administrative paperwork. It is the shortest commune name in France.

France's 35,014 communes were named over a thousand years of history: Latin saints, Frankish lords, river crossings, oak trees, and the occasional administrative merger that left someone typing "Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson" into a form. Analysing all of them at once reveals a country of strange patterns and stranger choices.

The République is named after saints

France is a secular republic since 1905. Its map disagrees. Exactly 3,891 communes — one in nine — open with Saint or Sainte. Add villages that contain a saint reference mid-name (Villeneuve-Saint-Georges, Aulnay-sous-Bois) and the devotion runs deeper still.

The most common name in France — shared by twelve communes — is Sainte-Colombe. There is a Sainte-Colombe in the Ain, the Ardèche, the Charente-Maritime, the Gironde, the Haute-Garonne, the Ille-et-Vilaine, the Marne, the Rhône, the Saône-et-Loire, and two more. If someone says they're from Sainte-Colombe, ask which one.

The length of a name

The median commune name is ten characters long. The mean is 11.8 — pulled right by a long tail of baroque hyphenated constructions. A single character handles one commune. Forty-five characters handle one other.

Name length distribution

Each bar = number of communes with that character count. Red bar = median (10 characters). Most names cluster between 6 and 15 characters.

The spike at 7–8 characters is where most single-word names live: Morlaix, Verdun, Antibes, Besançon. The long tail beyond 25 characters is almost entirely merger products — communes that were joined administratively in the 1970s under the Marcellin law, and retained both names as a hyphenated compound, sometimes forever.

From one letter to forty-five

Shortest · 1 character
Y
Somme (80) · Hauts-de-France · population 89 · INSEE 80829

Y has been Y since at least the 12th century, derived from the Latin Ica or possibly a Celtic water-root. The village sits on the Somme marshes, roughly halfway between Amiens and Saint-Quentin. Its post code is 80190 and its administrative existence requires a surprising number of workarounds in modern database systems that won't accept single-character fields.

Thirteen communes manage two-character names: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Many cluster in the Pyrenees — short names from Basque or Gascon roots that never expanded.

Longest · 45 characters · 7 hyphens
Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · Grand Est · population 491 · created by merger 1973

This is what happens when a 1973 merger joins three communes and nobody wins the naming argument. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, 7 hyphens, 6 words, 491 residents — is the longest commune name in France. It sits in the Marne, south of the Lac du Der reservoir. Its mayor signs every document with the full name.

The runners-up are equally architectural: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 chars, 923 people), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41 chars, 499 people), and Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne (Côte-d'Or, 40 chars, 381 people). The 1970s mergers left a durable mark.

The name-clash problem

6.4% of communes — 2,250 of them — share their name with at least one other commune. France has a built-in disambiguation layer (département numbers and INSEE codes), but maps, search bars, and GPS systems regularly get confused. Which Beaulieu? Saint-Marcel where?

The INSEE commune code exists precisely because names alone can't identify a place. The two-digit département prefix turns "Saint-Aubin" into ten unambiguous addresses. Without it, French administrative geography would simply not function.

A nation of hyphens

38.3% of France's commune names contain at least one hyphen — 13,410 places. The hyphen serves three purposes: it connects a saint's name to a geography (Saint-Valery-sur-Somme), it captures a historical possession (Villeneuve-lès-Avignon, meaning "next to Avignon"), or it records a merger, like the seven-hyphen champion above.

The hyphen is doing a lot of work. It is the typographic equivalent of French bureaucracy: precise, insistent, and endless.

There is no commune called "Y-sur-Quelque-Chose." Y stands alone.
