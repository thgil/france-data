What's in a French commune name?

35,014 commune names — one letter long to 45. Twelve villages called Sainte-Colombe. Nearly 2,000 ending in -sur- some river. The map of France is a vocabulary lesson.

2026-05-22 5 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — the smallest unit of French administrative life. Each has a name. Most of those names are long, hyphenated, and contain at least one saint. But one commune, in the Somme, is called Y. Just that. One letter. Population: 89.

Y

Commune de Y · Somme (80) · pop. 89 · APL 2.78

Y has been on the official maps since the 13th century. Its brevity is a bureaucratic accident that became a kind of fame — it holds the record as the shortest place name in France, and possibly in Europe. The commune next door is called Esmery-Hallon, which is 13 characters and a different universe.

One in nine communes is a saint

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,898 — just over 11%, nearly one in nine — begin with "Saint-" or "Sainte-". This is a country whose administrative map was drawn partly by the Catholic Church, and the names haven't changed. The diagonale du vide might shift over centuries, but the saints hold.

The most repeated name in all of France is Sainte-Colombe — 12 different communes across 12 different départements share it exactly. None of them particularly famous; all of them perfectly real, with their own mayors, post codes, and APL scores. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin, 10 times.

Most duplicated commune names (top 18)

Beyond the saints, 93 names appear five or more times. Beaulieu (10 communes), Le Pin (9), Beaumont (8), Verrières (8). France has been naming villages after pretty views, pine trees, and beautiful hills for long enough that the supply of original names ran low centuries ago.

From one letter to 45

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. The median is 12. But the distribution has a long right tail: 79 communes have names of 30 characters or more.

The longest is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne — 45 characters, two saints, one village of 491 people. It was created by merging three communes, and the name was simply concatenated rather than invented. French administrative logic at its most literal.

The 2-character club has 13 members: Sy, By, Bû, Oô, Oz, Ri, Uz, Py, Ur, Gy, Eu, Ry, Us. Eu, in Seine-Maritime, is the largest with 6,591 residents and a château where William the Conqueror is said to have met Harold before 1066. The name is from the Germanic ow, meaning water.

A geography lesson in prepositions

French commune names are not just labels — they're geographic descriptions. The most common locative connector is -sur- ("on" or "above"): 1,991 communes use it, usually placing themselves sur a river, a hill, or a sea. Then come -de- and -en-, and the quieter -lès- (an archaic "near"), -du-, and -sous- ("under").

Locative connectors in commune names

Each preposition implies a relationship with landscape. -sur-Seine, -sur-Loire, -sur-Mer — the rivers write their names across the map through these compound forms. -sous- typically means a village that grew up in the shadow of a larger settlement, a castle, or a hill. -lès- is especially archaic, surviving mainly in the north and northeast.

What France ends with

Split every commune name at spaces and hyphens and count the final word. The most common terminal word is bois — 178 communes end there. Then mer (108), and the rivers: seine (77), loire (74). France's geography saturates its own place names.

The hyphen as load-bearing infrastructure

13,410 communes — 38% of the total — have a hyphen in their name. The hyphen does most of the work: it joins the saint to the place (Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite), the landscape to the river (Villeneuve-sur-Loire), the old commune to the new one after a merger (Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne). Without the hyphen, the French commune name collapses into noise.

When INSEE records a commune, it records the hyphen too. The data in this story comes from the national administrative register: 35,014 entries, each with a name, a code, a département, and a population count. Analysed as a corpus, they form something between a census and a poem — a list of 35,000 small settlements, each one named after the landscape it sits in, the saint it was founded under, or the fact that it's on a river. France is a very old country. The names show it.
