What's in a name?

35,014 communes, 32,764 unique names — and one that's just the letter Y.

2026-06-04 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Each one has a name. With 35,000 slots to fill, you might expect 35,000 distinct names — but 31,314 communes have a name entirely their own. The other 3,700 share theirs with at least one sibling somewhere in the country. The most shared name in France — Sainte-Colombe — appears twelve times, in twelve different départements, with no disambiguation and no apology.

And then there is Y.

Y
Y (Somme, dept. 80) is France's shortest commune name — and one of the shortest official place names anywhere on earth. Population: 89. It sits in a farming valley 15 km south-east of Amiens. Its brevity is not a nickname or a postal abbreviation. It is simply the name. The commune of Y has been called Y since at least the 12th century, possibly deriving from a Germanic root meaning water. The local weekly newspaper covering the area is called Le Courrier picard. Y has no hyphen, no accent, no article. Just Y.

The two-letter club

Y is not alone in its brevity. Thirteen French communes make do with exactly two letters. They are scattered across the country — from Pyrénées-Orientales in the south to Ardennes in the north — and most carry names that are pre-Roman in origin, often Gaulish or Germanic roots that meant something specific a thousand years ago and now survive as charming accidents of administrative continuity.

The two-letter communes

Longest commune names

At the other extreme: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne, at 45 characters the longest commune name in France. It acquired this magnificent title through the French government's policy of encouraging communes to merge — formally called communes nouvelles — which sometimes means hyphenating two or three existing names into one. The result reads less like a place name and more like a legal contract.

The Sainte-Colombe problem

There are twelve communes called Sainte-Colombe. They are in twelve different départements: Ain, Ardèche, Côte-d'Or, Gers, Gironde, Haute-Garonne, Haute-Loire, Jura, Rhône, Savoie, Seine-Maritime, and Yonne. Sending a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France" is not useful. This is a general problem: 1,450 names are shared by at least two communes.

The pattern is clear: saint names dominate. Sauveur, Aubin, Marcel, Michel, Pierre, Rémy, Clément, Hilaire — these are the saints France ran out of communes to name. Beaulieu (10 instances) and Beaumont (8) break the religious pattern, but only barely: beau- was a default compliment for a new settlement that nobody ever retired.

One in eleven is a saint

3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of all communes

That is more saint-named communes than Germany has communes in total in several of its states. The church was not merely a spiritual institution in medieval France — it was the only administrative infrastructure that reached every village. A saint's name was a deed of ownership, a statement of who built the chapel and who the village owed its existence to. The saints themselves had little say in the matter.

Add in the communes starting with Le, La, or Les — another 2,220 — and roughly one commune in six announces its nature in the first syllable of its name.

The shape of French commune names

The median commune name is 10 characters long; the mean is 11.8. The distribution is right-skewed — most names cluster between 6 and 18 characters, with a long tail of hyphenated monsters. The most common length is characters.

Commune names are a living archive. They record which saint the medieval peasants were most afraid of, which rivers flooded which valleys, which Norman duke claimed which hill. They record the mergers of the 1970s loi Marcellin and the reluctant hyphenations of the 2010s. They record the stubbornness of a village in the Somme that has been called Y for a thousand years and sees no reason to change now.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to INSEE administrative boundaries — 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name lengths and duplicate counts are computed from the communeName field as it appears in the INSEE administrative files. Some merged communes (communes nouvelles) carry hyphenated names reflecting their constituent villages.
