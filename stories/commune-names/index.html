What's in a name?

35,014 French communes, from Y (one letter, 89 people) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters, 7 hyphens).

2026-06-01 5 min read

France has a commune called Y. Not a village that sounds like the letter Y. Not an abbreviation. The actual letter Y — one character, officially recognised since the 12th century, still on the maps today. It sits in the Somme, a few kilometres east of Amiens, with a population of 89. It has a mayor. It has a church. And in the French toponymic tradition, it counts.

Y The shortest commune name in France — 1 letter, Somme (80), population 89

Y derives from the Picard word aie, meaning a hedge or water. The commune's name predates modern orthographic standardisation by centuries; no one has ever felt the need to lengthen it. That makes it, officially, the shortest place name in France — and one of the shortest on earth.

One letter to forty-five

At the other extreme, the longest commune name in France runs to 45 characters and 7 hyphens: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne (population 491). That name is not a mistake — it's the administrative fusion of three older villages, all hyphenated together in 1972 during a wave of communes fusionnées reforms. The result is a name so long it regularly breaks web forms.

The distribution between them follows an expected bell curve — most communes cluster at 7–8 characters — but the long tail is surprisingly populated. There are 3 communes with names of 40+ characters, 7 with 35+, and 55 with 28+. Each one of those long names is typically a fused commune: two or three old villages merged administratively, their original names stitched together with hyphens.

The saint problem

11.1% of France's communes — 3,898 in total — start with Saint or Sainte. This is not a rounding artefact. France is, in toponymic terms, a deeply Catholic country. The medieval parish system named settlements after their patron saints, and those names crystallised into official commune names as administrative structures hardened after the Revolution. They were never cleaned up.

Dordogne (dept 24) leads the count with 130 Saint communes — roughly one in every three of its 505 communes carries a saint's name. Gironde (33) follows with 121, and Isère (38) with 106. These are old territories with dense medieval settlement networks; each cluster of farms got its own patron, and the names stuck.

Send a letter to Sainte-Colombe

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times — in Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. They are completely separate villages with no administrative relationship. They all happen to have been named after the same third-century Iberian martyr.

In total, 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other — that's more than 1 in 10. The French postal system handles this via the INSEE commune code (a 5-digit identifier that prefixes the postal code), but informal addresses, navigation apps, and journalists regularly get confused. Send a letter to Saint-Sauveur, France and the post office has 11 equally valid destinations.

The hyphen explosion

38.3% of France's commune names — 13,410 of them — contain at least one hyphen. French administrative naming convention generally hyphenates compound place names where English would leave a space or use a preposition. Saint-Aubin, not St Aubin. Villers-le-Sec, not Villers le Sec. The result is a system that looks more consistent on paper but generates names that are long, complicated, and occasionally absurd.

The record holder, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, was formed in 1972 by fusing three communes. All three kept their names. The administrative town is now forced to print this on legal documents, road signs, and official correspondence. The local mairie's letterhead is, presumably, a design challenge.

The two-letter club

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. They cluster in the extreme north and south — the Ardennes, the Pyrenees, the Basque fringe, Normandy — where toponymic traditions diverge sharply from the central French pattern. Many derive from ancient Gaulish, Basque, or Germanic roots that were never expanded by medieval Latinate naming conventions.

Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591) is by far the largest of the short-name communes — a market town on the Norman coast where the Treaty of Eu was signed in 1843. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, population 36) is the smallest. Oô is the only two-letter commune name that uses a double vowel with a circumflex — pronounced roughly oh-oh in Gascon, referring to a mountain stream. Geography leaves its mark on language, even at two characters.

35,014 names, none quite alike

France has more communes than any other European country — a legacy of the Revolutionary settlement that preserved every medieval parish as an administrative unit. That decision locked in ~35,000 names that had evolved organically over centuries, in a dozen regional linguistic traditions, before orthographic standardisation existed. The result is a naming system that is historically rich, geographically specific, and administratively chaotic.

Y and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson are the same system, just at different points of the distribution. One name that never needed to grow. One that couldn't stop.
