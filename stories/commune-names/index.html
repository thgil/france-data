From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

France has 34,969 communes. One is named Y. Eleven percent begin with "Saint." Twelve of them are called Sainte-Colombe. A census of names.

2026-05-12 4 min read

France carves its territory into 34,969 communes — the smallest administrative unit, each one a name on an official register. Those names run from a single letter to a 45-character chain of hyphenated saints and rivers. Between those extremes, there is a grammar: rivers (-sur-, -sous-), forests (-en-), saints (so many saints), merger scars (two village names joined by -et-), and the occasional monumental brevity of a name that is simply, stubbornly, Y.

One letter: Y

Y is a village of 89 people in the Somme, roughly equidistant between Amiens and Saint-Quentin. It has its own mairie, its own INSEE code (80829), and the shortest official commune name in France. The name is ancient — likely from a Celtic root — and has survived every administrative reform since the Revolution. No prefect has ever successfully renamed it something more legible.

Thirteen communes manage two letters. Most end in a vowel sound that suggests deep regional roots — Breton By, Norman Ry, Béarnais Uz, Catalan Py and Ur. The exception is Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop 6,591), a Norman city whose two letters conceal a 1,000-year history and a château where Queen Victoria once stayed.

    The Christian naming reflex runs deepest in the old rural heartlands. Ardèche (département 07) leads: 29% of its communes invoke a saint. Creuse, Loire, and Haute-Vienne aren't far behind. Northern France — with its larger communes, its Flemish and Germanic naming traditions — uses saints far less. Paris has none: its arrondissements have numbers.

    Département % saint names

    The saints themselves have favourites: Martin appears in 304 commune names (the most popular patron, unsurprisingly). Pierre (180), Jean (170), Germain (120), and Julien (88) follow. The Virgin — via Marie — appears in hundreds more, often without the Saint prefix.

    Sainte-Colombe: twelve communes, one name

    France has no system for preventing duplicate commune names. Two communes can — and 1,450 names do — exist simultaneously in different départements with the exact same official name. In total, 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. Type Sainte-Colombe into a French postal system and you will be asked to pick from twelve options spread across Ain, Charente-Maritime, Gironde, Haute-Garonne, and seven other départements.

    Saint-Aubin (10 communes), Saint-Marcel (9), Saint-Michel (9), Le Pin (9). The pattern is the same: generic enough to be chosen independently by medieval parishes across the country, specific enough that nobody ever bothered to disambiguate them.

    Seven hyphens: the marathon commune

    Y 1 character · Somme · pop. 89

    Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · 7 hyphens · Marne · pop. 491

    Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is a commune in the Marne — 491 residents, born in 1972 from the merger of three medieval villages. Its name is the physical record of that merger: the hyphens are seams. The Bouzemont lake (a reservoir created by the same 1960s infrastructure project that prompted the merger) is now a leisure destination. The name, which requires seven hyphens and 45 characters to type in full, appears on every official document the 491 residents receive.

    Names are not random. They are accumulated history — religious, geographic, political — compressed into a string of letters that fits on a postage stamp and stands in for centuries. France has 34,969 of them. One is Y.

    A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the communes-apl.geojson dataset built for the medical deserts story — 35,014 features including overseas territories and arrondissement-level subdivisions. This analysis uses 34,969 non-arrondissement communes. Name-length analysis, duplicate detection, and saint-prefix counts were computed from the communeName field of that file.
