What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is named Y. Another needs 45 characters. And one name appears 12 times across the country.

2026-05-23 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, there is a village of 89 people whose name is a single letter.

Y

Commune of Y · Somme (80) · pop. 89 · APL 2.78

It has a church, a war memorial, a listing in every official database in France. Its INSEE code is 80579. If you need to find it on a map, you search for it by a character that a search engine will usually ignore. Y is, unambiguously, a place.

The two-letter club

Y is not alone in its brevity. France has 13 communes with two-letter names — each one a syllable or a sound that somehow stuck through the centuries of standardisation that gave France its 35,000 named places.

Longest commune names

At the other extreme, there is this:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-
Saint-Genest-et-Isson

45 characters · Marne (51) · the result of a 1972 commune merger

It is not a typo. It is a commune in the Marne, born from the administrative fusion of three smaller villages in 1972. France has been merging communes for decades — the number has fallen from around 38,000 in the 1960s to 35,014 today — and when villages merge, the path of least political resistance is often just to concatenate the names. The result is occasionally spectacular.

The saints, numbered

11.1% of French communes — 3,882 of them — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That is more than the entire population of a small county. They represent 3,376 distinct saint-names, which means most saints have only one commune named for them. But a few are everywhere.

Sainte Colombe was a Christian martyr, possibly of the third century, possibly in Gaul, possibly near Sens. The details are thin. What's not thin is the reach: her name appears in the Dordogne, in the Gironde, in Côtes-d'Armor, in the Haute-Garonne, in the Seine-Maritime, in Essonne, and six more départements besides. Twelve communes, one name, zero disambiguation. The French postal system handles it; addresses require commune + département together.

The gendered article problem

2,140 communes begin with a definite article — Le, La, or Les. This is a snapshot of how French geography absorbed the grammatical gender of medieval French: rivers, hills, and territories picked up articles that then became part of official place names. Le Pin appears nine times. La Chapelle- something appears dozens of times. The article is part of the name; you cannot drop it.

The average commune name in France is 11.8 characters long. The median is probably around 10. The distribution peaks at 7–8 characters — short, often a single word — but runs all the way to 45. And at the bottom of that curve, with its single character sitting in every French administrative database like a piece of conceptual art, is Y.

A note on the data. Commune names were extracted from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (data.gouv.fr), which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. A small number of arrondissement subdivisions (Paris, Lyon, Marseille) are included in the total. Name lengths, frequencies, and rankings were computed from the INSEE communeName field. Dordogne (dept 24) has 128 Saint-* communes — the most of any département, ahead of Gironde (121) and Isère (105).
