What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. Their names are a compressed history — saints, rivers, hyphenated mergers, and one place called Y.

2026-05-27 5 min read

France has more communes than Germany, Italy, Spain, and the UK combined. Every one of them has a name. Those 35,014 names form a kind of compressed national memory — saints from the early Church, rivers and hills that fixed coordinates before cartography, and centuries of mergers, revolutions, and revivals that left compound surnames no single parish could claim alone.

Almost everything worth knowing about French local identity lives somewhere in these names. Here's what the data reveals.

The shortest and the longest

France has exactly one commune with a one-letter name: Y. It sits in the Somme (département 80), 89 residents, named for a river valley that the locals simply called by the old Picard word for water. It is entirely unremarkable as a place. As a name, it wins everything.

Below Y sit thirteen communes with two-letter names — a compact set including Eu (Seine-Maritime, 6,591 residents, named for its river), Gy (Haute-Saône, 1,001 residents), and Oz (Isère, 213 residents, which is not in Kansas).

At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. 491 residents. Marne, département 51. It was created by merging three smaller communes in 1972, and the name reflects that: each fragment insisted on its own place in the record. It is currently the longest commune name in France.

Most common names

The same name, twelve times over

Of the 35,014 commune names, 32,764 are unique. The remaining 3,700 communes — home to over 5.5 million people — share a name with at least one other commune in a different département.

The most duplicated: Sainte-Colombe, used 12 times across 12 different départements stretching from Charente to Haute-Savoie. There are eleven communes called Saint-Sauveur, ten each called Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu.

This is not coincidence or bureaucratic laziness. Medieval communes were named after their local patron saint, nearest geographic feature, or founding family. When a saint was popular across a region, many nearby parishes took the same name — separated by territory but sharing a devotion. Before the Revolutionary cadastre pinned each commune to a unique INSEE code, the name alone was often enough. Today, the code is canonical. The name is cultural memory.

One in nine communes honours a saint

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. That's roughly one in nine. If you picked a French commune at random, you'd pull a saint's name more than once in ten draws.

Saint-Martin leads all patrons with 202 communes. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow. The top ten patrons together account for over 1,100 communes — a third of all the saints-named places in France.

Saint-Martin's dominance reflects his central place in early French Christianity: Tours was his seat, and his cult spread through every diocese in the kingdom. Jean and Pierre are simply the most popular saints in the medieval Catholic world. What's striking is that these ancient devotional patterns are still visible in the administrative map today — the Church named the parishes; the Republic absorbed the names.

38% of communes are compound names

13,410 commune names — 38.3% — contain a hyphen. These compound names are the French way of encoding geography into an address: the base name, a preposition, a feature. Boulogne-sur-Mer: Boulogne, on the sea. Le-Puy-en-Velay: the peak, in the Velay. Sous-la-Côte: below the hillside.

Prepositions in compound names

First letters (after articles)

The favourite preposition is -sur-, appearing in 2,007 names — almost always marking a river junction. France's ancient settlement pattern followed water: mill towns, ford crossings, fish weirs. The names record those choices. -sous- (under, below) and -lès-/-lez- (near) appear less often but are equally precise: they locate a commune in relation to a neighbour, an elevation, or a boundary that no longer shows on any map.

S is for Saint — and for 16% of all communes

Strip the articles (Le, La, Les, L') and sort by first letter: S leads with 5,678 communes, far ahead of C (4,123) and B (3,439). S's dominance is almost entirely due to Saint and Sainte — without them, C would win comfortably. The rarest first letter is Œ, with 4 communes (including Œuilly in Aisne). X appears only 21 times.

The letters tell their own story about French linguistic history. K appears only 78 times, mostly in Alsace-Lorraine and Bretagne where German and Breton naming conventions survive. W (198) follows the same pattern: the north and east, where Frankish Germanic vocabulary persists in place names. The Mediterranean south shows up in Q (131) and X (21) — Occitan and Catalan roots that kept orthographies the north abandoned centuries ago.

The names outlast everything else

France has changed its administrative map repeatedly — the Revolution, the Napoleonic prefectures, the communal mergers of the 1960s and 70s, the communes nouvelles of the last decade. Each reform reshuffled boundaries and codes. But the names persisted. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson absorbed three communes and kept all three surnames. Y survived the Revolution, two World Wars, and the slow depopulation of the Somme. Sainte-Colombe multiplied across twelve départements and never resolved into a single place.

The commune name is the one piece of French administrative identity that is genuinely hard to erase. It was there before the state, and it will likely be there after the next reform.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the APL 2023 dataset assembled for Story 2 (medical deserts), which joins DREES APL scores to INSEE commune boundaries (Contours administratifs 2025). Of the 35,014 communes in the dataset, every one has a name field derived from the INSEE commune registry. Population figures are INSEE 2021 base.
