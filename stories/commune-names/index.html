From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

France has 35,014 communes. Their names run from a single letter to 45 characters. One is just "Y". Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe.

June 2026 4 min read Culture

The French commune is one of the world's most numerous municipal units — there are roughly 35,000 of them, more than in the rest of the European Union combined. They come in every size, from Paris (over 2 million) to hamlets of a dozen souls. And they come with names. 35,014 names, to be precise. The shortest is one letter long.

Y is a real place. It has a mairie, a postal code (80190), and 89 registered inhabitants who presumably tire of spelling out their address over the phone. It sits in a flat stretch of Picardy south of Amiens. Etymologically the name comes from the Latin aqua — water — filtered through centuries of Old Picard. France's second-shortest commune names are all two letters: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz. Thirteen of them.

At the other extreme, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is what happens when several villages merge and each one insists on keeping its name in the combined entity. The French fusion de communes law allows this — and the result, across rural France, is a growing catalogue of hyphenated marathons. None of these long names are ancient; they're the product of 20th-century administrative mergers, each village unwilling to disappear.

The shape of a name

Most commune names are short. The median is 10 characters; the mean is 11.8. But the distribution has a long right tail, pulled out by all those hyphenated mergers. The histogram below shows every commune by name length. The single red bar on the left is Y.

The hump between 15 and 22 characters is notable: it's not a smooth tail, but a second, smaller peak. That's the signature of the Saint-X-de-Y and Saint-X-sur-Y construction — single towns with patron-saint compound names that were never mergers at all. France's cartography of sainthood runs deep.

The 12 Sainte-Colombes

3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. The most common single commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which belongs to 12 different communes in 12 different départements.

If you address a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France" without a département code, you are in trouble. The name could point to a commune of 59 people in the Hautes-Alpes or a town of nearly 2,000 in the Rhône. Those 12 Sainte-Colombes span the country: Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-et-Marne, Seine-Maritime, and two in the far south. The post code is the only thing that keeps the mail sorted.

Mostly unique, mostly old

95.6% of France's commune names are unique — one name, one place. The 4.4% that share a name with another commune account for 3,700 communes in total. These are nearly all saint names and medieval descriptives (Beaulieu, Beaumont, Le Pin, Verrières) — the naming vocabulary of the Middle Ages was smaller than France's territory, and the same names settled independently in dozens of valleys.

The names as a whole are a compressed history of settlement. The short ones — Y, Eu, Ry, Oz — are pre-Latin, Gaulish or even older, their origins worn smooth by two millennia of use. The medium-length ones are mostly medieval: saints, natural features, lord's names. The long ones are modern: the 20th century's reluctant village mergers, each hyphen a small act of administrative grief.

Thirty-five thousand names, and the shortest of them is a single letter standing in a flat field in Picardy.
