What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another is called Sainte-Colombe — twelve times over.

2026-05-13 4 min read

Every commune in France has an official name. Most names are sensible — geographical features, patron saints, historical settlements. And then there is Y.

Y

A commune of 89 residents in the Somme (département 80). The shortest official place name in France. Not an abbreviation. Not a postal shorthand. Just Y.

Y is not alone. France has 13 communes whose names are two letters or fewer — including Gy (Haute-Saône), Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591), Ri (Orne), Oz (Isère), Ur and Py (both Pyrénées-Orientales), and Bû (Eure-et-Loir, the only one with an accent). Each is a real municipality. Each has a mayor, a budget, a flag, and the world's most confusing mailing address.

The most common name in France appears 12 times

At the other extreme of identity, France has a chronic duplication problem. The single most common commune name is Sainte-Colombe — which occurs in 12 different départements simultaneously, from the Alps to the Atlantic. There are 11 communes called Saint-Sauveur, 10 each named Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu. The top 15 most-duplicated names account for 128 separate communes, all with identical names.

The duplication is a postal and cartographic headache. France's postal service disambiguates them via département code — Sainte-Colombe (05), Sainte-Colombe (17), Sainte-Colombe (25) — the way we distinguish Saint-Paul de Vence from Saint-Paul-lès-Dax. Twelve places, one name, twelve separate mayors.

France has 3,882 saint-named communes

The duplication pattern is driven by one overwhelming cause: the patron-saint naming convention that has governed French commune names for a thousand years. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,882 — that's 11.1% — carry a saint's name as their first word. Saint-Martin alone accounts for 202 communes. Saint-Jean for 155. Saint-Pierre for 144.

The Ardèche (département 07) leads the nation: 29% of its communes carry a saint's name. The Gironde and Dordogne follow close behind. In some rural pockets of the Massif Central and the south-west, every road sign for 30 kilometres features a saint. The Enlightenment de-sanctified French politics; it never got around to the road network.

From one letter to forty-five

Between Y (1 character) and the longest commune name in France (45 characters) is roughly a continent of naming ambition. The winner is a commune in the Marne, formed by a merger of several villages — each of which insisted on keeping its name in the combined title. The result is hyphenated into existence:

Longest commune name — 45 characters
Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, Marne (51)
Shortest commune name — 1 character
Y, Somme (80) — 89 residents

89% of communes have a name no one else is using

Despite all the saints and the hyphens and the Sainte-Colombes, France's commune names are remarkably individual. Of the 32,764 distinct names in use, 31,314 — fully 89.4% of all communes — have a name that appears nowhere else in France. No twin, no triple. Just one commune, one name.

2,250 communes share their exact name with at least one other. 128 of those are involved in chains of three or more identical names. The remaining 32,886 communes stand alone — named for a forgotten lord, a bend in a river, a tree that blew down centuries ago. Their names are the last material evidence that the place ever existed as a distinct community before the Revolution redrew everything.

Y was always going to be an outlier.

A note on the data. Commune names and boundaries come from data.gouv.fr's 2025 administrative contours file (simplified at 1 km resolution), the same dataset used in the medical deserts story. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census as embedded in the DREES APL 2023 dataset. 35,014 communes are represented, including Paris, Lyon, and Marseille arrondissements. Overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte) are included. New Caledonia communes (département 98) have zero population in the APL data; their names are included but population-dependent stats exclude them.
