The naming of France

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called "Y". Another needs 45 characters just to say its name. And 3,882 of them are saints.

2026-05-11 5 min read

In the Somme, there is a village of 89 people called Y. That's the whole name. One letter. Post office included. Officially, it is the shortest commune name in France — and, as far as anyone can tell, one of the shortest place names in the world used as an official administrative designation.

Y sits in the department of the Somme (80), has been a commune since the Revolution, and appears in INSEE's national commune registry under code 80829. It does not find this remarkable. Residents of Y have been living there for centuries, and they see no reason to apologise for the brevity.

The saint factory

11.1% of all French communes — 3,882 of them — carry a saint's name. The Catholic Church shaped French administrative geography long before INSEE arrived to count it. Saint- appears in 3,587 commune names; Sainte- in another 295. Combined, they outnumber the entire population of many small départements.

But the saints are not evenly distributed. The most-duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in 12 different départements simultaneously — from Hautes-Alpes (05) to Yonne (89). This is not confusion; it's ecclesiastical geography. Each Sainte-Colombe was named for the same 3rd-century martyr, in different centuries, by different bishops, and none of them thought to check if the name was taken. INSEE issued each one a unique code. The post office manages.

The twenty most common names

Of France's 35,014 communes, 31,314 have a name shared by no other commune. The remaining 3,700 share theirs with at least one neighbour — in a different département, with a different INSEE code, but the same name. The top 20 most duplicated names are almost entirely saints and generic landscape terms.

How long is a French commune name?

The average commune name runs to 11.8 characters — long enough to say Beaulieu (8) or Saint-Aubin (10), short enough to tweet. The distribution peaks around 7–8 characters: names like Brignon, Floirac, Castres. The long right tail is driven by merger artifacts — when two or more communes join, they often hyphenate all their names together rather than choose, producing the 40-and-above monsters at the far end.

The short end

Y is alone at one character. Thirteen communes manage two. Their names read like the random syllables a password generator might produce — By, Eu, Gy, Oô, Oz, Ur, Us — but each one is ancient, each one is a settlement that predates the bureaucracy that named it. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the largest, a medieval town where Henri IV was married. Bû (Eure-et-Loir, pop. 2,022) is a market town 70km from Paris. Sy (Ardennes, pop. 54) is the smallest: 54 people in a valley, two letters to their name.

The French commune system is a product of the Revolution: 1789 swept away the parishes and lordships and replaced them with a uniform administrative grid. The names, though, weren't uniform — they were inherited. Every Saint-Martin, every Beaulieu, every Y already existed; the revolutionaries just stamped them with codes and let them be. Two centuries later, INSEE keeps the list, mergers hyphenate the names, and Y remains Y. The post office manages.

A note on the data. Commune names and counts derive from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to the 2025 INSEE commune boundary file, both already in the repository from the medical deserts story. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name-length analysis uses the full official commune name as stored in the INSEE code field.
