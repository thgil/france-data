What France calls its villages

35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. One has a 45-character name. A brief tour of France's most extreme place names.

2026-05-26 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Their names run from the ancient to the bureaucratic, from Latin saints to Norse syllables that survived a thousand years of administration. Most are six to twelve characters long. And then there is one commune, in the département of Somme, whose entire official name is a single letter.

Shortest commune name in France
Y
Somme (dept. 80) · population 89 · commune code 80829

Y is a real village, with a real mayor, a real post office, and a real entry in the INSEE commune register. Its name derives from an old Picard root word for water or stream. It has been called Y since at least the medieval period — long before anyone worried about mail sorting. Today, the postal code (80190) does most of the disambiguation. The name Y is not a quirk or a typo. It is simply the oldest local word that stuck.

The short end of the alphabet

Y is alone at one character. But thirteen more communes manage with just two. Most are names inherited from languages that predate French — old Norse, Occitan, Basque, or pre-Roman Celtic — compressed down to a sound that the village has carried for longer than France has been a unified state.

Y By Bû Eu Gy Oz Oô Py Ri Ry Sy Ur Us Uz

Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is by far the largest — a market town in upper Normandy with a château that Louis-Philippe used as a summer residence. Oz is a ski village in the Isère Alps. Ur and Py are Catalan villages in the Pyrénées-Orientales. Uz, in the Hautes-Pyrénées, has a population of just 36. The single-syllable names cluster at the edges of France — in the mountain borders and the Norman north — where pre-French linguistic roots held longest against Parisian standardisation.

One saint for every eight communes

France has 4,195 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — roughly one in eight. Christianity shaped the medieval parish network, and the parish name became the commune name. The Revolution tried to rename them — Montagne-le-Bon-Dieu became Montagne tout court — but most of the changes didn't last. The saints came back.

Saint-Martin leads the count: over 150 communes carry that name in some form (Saint-Martin, Saint-Martin-de-Xxx, Saint-Martin-sur-Xxx). Martin of Tours is the patron saint of France and apparently of bureaucratic imagination. The runner-up saints — Jean, Pierre, Georges, Michel — appear in hundreds more hyphenated variants.

Twelve villages, one name

When each commune named itself after its local saint, nobody coordinated. The result: 2,250 communes today share their exact name with at least one other commune. The worst collision in the dataset is Sainte-Colombe, which appears twelve times across twelve différent départements.

Sainte-Colombe in the Rhône (pop. 1,953) and Sainte-Colombe in Seine-et-Marne (pop. 1,793) are the largest. The smallest is the Hautes-Alpes version: 59 residents, one name, no disambiguation needed within the village — only outside it. Saint-Sauveur follows at eleven occurrences, Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu at ten each. In 1,450 collision groups across France, a name alone tells you almost nothing about which commune is which.

The other extreme

If Y is France's shortest commune name, the longest comes in at 45 characters — and it contains two saints, four villages, and three connecting prepositions, all hyphenated into a single administrative unit.

Longest commune name in France — 45 characters
Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (dept. 51) · population 571

This commune in the Marne formed through the merger of several villages, a process that began in earnest after the loi Marcellin of 1971, which incentivised municipalities to fuse. Most mergers produced sensible hyphenated names. A few did not. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 characters) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41 characters) both suggest that the committee in charge of naming preferred inclusion over brevity. The hyphen is free; the argument to drop a village name apparently is not.

What the names preserve

The 35,014 commune names are a compressed history of France — the Latin saints, the Norse monosyllables, the Basque root words, the revolutionary renames that mostly failed, and the bureaucratic mergers that produced 45-character hyphenated hybrids. Most communes were named between the 9th and 13th centuries, when whatever the locals called the place got written down by a priest or a tax collector and hardened into official record. The shorter the name, the older it usually is. The longer the name, the more recent the administrative intervention. Y is ancient. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is a committee.

A note on the data. All analysis is based on the 35,014 communes present in the DREES APL 2023 dataset, joined to commune polygon boundaries from Etalab's 2025 contours file. The communeName field reflects official INSEE commune names at the 2025 vintage. Municipal mergers (communes nouvelles) created since then are not reflected. The communes-index.json file in this story's folder is a lightweight derivative (name + centroid + population) generated from the communes-apl.geojson source.
