What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. Their names encode a millennium of history — Christian saints, river crossings, and at least one commune that goes by a single letter.

2026-06-05 5 min read

Every commune in France has a name. Some are ancient — Celtic place names worn smooth by a thousand years of French phonology. Some are ecclesiastical — every village that once sheltered a chapel became a "Saint-Something." Some are the bureaucratic inventions of the 2010s, when neighboring villages merged and hyphenated their identities together into something no one can pronounce quickly.

There are 35,014 of them. At an average of 11.8 characters, they range from one letter to forty-five. Here's what the distribution tells you.

Y

The shortest commune name in France is Y. One letter. Population 89. Department of the Somme. It sits on the marshy plain east of Amiens, a speck of habitation surrounded by beet fields and sky. The name is thought to derive from a Gaulish word for water — a cognate of the Latin aqua — that contracted over centuries until only the vowel remained.

France also has 13 two-letter communes. They cluster in the south (the Pyrenees account for four: Oô, Py, Ur, Uz), in Normandy (Eu, Ry), and scattered across the eastern uplands. Eu in Seine-Maritime is the largest of the group, with 6,591 residents. Oô in the Hautes-Pyrénées preserves a circumflex on its second vowel — a medieval diacritic that was once an s and now just marks a long syllable in a name almost no one has occasion to spell.

A bell curve with a very long tail

Strip away the extremes and commune names follow an almost normal distribution, peaking at 7–8 characters. Most communes have names you can say in one breath: Metz, Reims, Rouen, Nantes, Angers. The tail stretches right, fed by a long tradition of descriptive compounding — Saint-X-de-Y-sur-Z — that gets longer every time two villages merge and neither wants to disappear.

The winner is a 2016 merger in the Marne — Champagne country — that brought three ancient parishes under one administrative umbrella and one truly unwieldy name. Second place goes to Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), another fusion commune that sounds like a law firm. France has more than 150 communes whose names exceed 30 characters. The wave of municipal fusions begun under the 2015 Loi NOTRe added dozens of them.

    One in nine

    3,898 communes — 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. Put another way: if you pick a French commune at random, you have a one-in-nine chance of picking a saint. This is not uniformly distributed. The saintly names cluster in the regions that Christianity reached earliest and held tightest — Bretagne, the Massif Central, the Loire valley — and thin out in the Alps and Alsace, where Germanic naming traditions left a different mark.

    The most common saint: 36 communes named Saint-Marie or Sainte-Marie, one for each of France's historical provinces and then some. Behind her: 24 Saint/Sainte-Colombe, 20 Saint/Sainte-Croix, 14 Saint/Sainte-Foy. The pattern is a map of early Christian martyrology, still legible in the postal system.

    Twelve Sainte-Colombes

    The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them — in Ille-et-Vilaine, Haute-Saône, Rhône, Seine-et-Marne, and eight other départements — all named for the same 3rd-century martyred virgin who reportedly carried her own severed head to a spring near Sens. France's communes don't just share names; they share patron saints across centuries of geography and devotion.

    Most duplicated commune names

    The commune name is the smallest unit of French administrative identity. It predates the republic, the Ancien Régime, and in some cases recorded history. Y has been called Y since before anyone thought to write it down. Sainte-Colombe has been Sainte-Colombe in twelve places simultaneously for longer than France has been France. The names are not just labels — they are a sediment record of what happened here, compressed into a few syllables and a spot in the postal code.

    A note on the data. Commune names, population estimates, and geographic centroids are derived from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset joined to the Etalab administrative contours (2025 vintage, 1000m simplification). This gives 35,014 communes — the full metropolitan and overseas France territory. Population figures are INSEE 2021 estimates embedded in the APL file. The full commune name and centroid index is available as communes-index.json (3.0 MB).
