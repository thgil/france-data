What's in a French name?

From Y (one letter, 89 people) to a 45-character marvel in Marne — the hidden patterns in 35,014 commune names.

2026-06-04 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme called Y. One letter. 89 residents. It sits on flat agricultural land 25 kilometres south of Amiens, and has been continuously inhabited since at least the medieval period. The name is attested in 13th-century documents as Aye, which contracted over centuries to its present single glyph. It is, on the official record, the shortest commune name in France — and probably in Europe.

Y is not alone in its brevity. Thirteen communes have two-letter names: Sy (Ardennes), By (Doubs), Eu (Seine-Maritime), and ten others — mostly in mountainous or border regions where pre-Latin place names survived the Latinisation intact. Basque Ur (water), Occitan Uz, Breton syllables weathered down to consonant and vowel. The short names are a map of linguistic survival.

Forty-five characters

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (dept. 51) · 491 residents · 45 characters

The name exists because France has, over the past two centuries, repeatedly encouraged small communes to merge while allowing them to keep their identities hyphenated on the way down. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in Marne is three former villages stitched together — none wanted to disappear, so all three are still legible in the name. A law from 1971 formalised this "fusion-association" regime; dozens of communes took the option and ended up with names long enough to test a postal worker's patience.

The runners-up are equally elaborate: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 chars), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41 chars). Most of the longest names are in the Haute-Saône and Calvados — two départements that apparently embraced their fusions with particular enthusiasm.

Two peaks, one history

The histogram above has a shape you don't find in most datasets: two distinct humps. The first, at 7–8 characters, covers simple village names — Ambroise, Chablis, Mézille, Valcourt. Single words from Gallo-Roman roots, Frankish estates, Celtic river names. The second hump, centred around 15–18 characters, is almost entirely Saint names with geographic qualifiers: Saint-André-de-Bâgé, Saint-Julien-en-Vercors, Saint-Laurent-du-Var. Two different eras of naming, both still alive in the same address book.

The Saint army: 3,898 communes

Eleven percent of French communes — 3,898 of 35,014 — begins with "Saint." That is more than one in nine. They are not a rounding error; they are the sediment of a millennium of Carolingian and post-Carolingian Christianity, in which naming a village after its patron saint was the default act of civic piety.

Saint-Martin leads the table with 203 communes. Tours, his cult centre, is the hinge of French religious geography: the veneration of Martin spread north, south, and east from the Loire, and every village that built a church in his name gave itself that address. Jean (155), Pierre (144), Germain (108) follow — the apostles, the popes, the Frankish bishops whose cult was local. By contrast, modern saints barely appear. The calendar of communes is the calendar of the early Middle Ages.

Sainte-Colombe: winner of the coincidence trophy

Among exact name matches — not prefixes, but the full name — Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times, spread across 12 different départements. Rhône (pop. 1,953), Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Seine-et-Marne, and eight others all claim a Sainte-Colombe. The saint herself — a 3rd-century virgin martyr from Sens — had a cult that was genuinely national in medieval France, generating shrines and dedications from Burgundy to the Atlantic coast. The communes did not coordinate. They were each independently devoted to the same obscure holy woman.

Saint-Sauveur follows with 11 occurrences, Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each at 10. The most repeated non-Saint name is Beaulieu ("beautiful place") at 10, which suggests that the founders of those villages were either unusually optimistic or had very similar taste in real estate.

But 89% are one of a kind

For all the Saints and Beaulieus, 31,314 of France's 35,014 communes — 89.4% — have a name that appears exactly once. Montmarault. Frayssinhes. Mézilhac. Rocquemont. Escueillens. Each exists as a word nowhere else on France's administrative map. They are the linguistic residue of 2,000 years of local history: a feudal lord's name Latinised by a notary, a river ford described in Occitan, a Breton hillfort that outlasted the language that named it.

France is sometimes imagined as a country relentlessly standardised from the centre — one language, one republic, one set of administrative departments. But the commune names resist that reading. They are too old, too stubborn, too local. Y exists. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson exists. And somewhere in the Deux-Sèvres, there is a village called Brain — 1,500 people, one name, untranslatable — which has been itself since before the word "France" meant anything at all.

A note on the data. Commune names, INSEE codes, and populations come from the medical-deserts story's APL dataset (DREES 2023), which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name lengths are measured in characters including hyphens, spaces, and accented letters. The Saint-X prefix counts treat "Saint" and "Sainte" as the same root, and split on the first hyphen or space. The communes-index.json file in this story's folder contains code, name, département, population, and centroid coordinates for all 35,014 communes.
