35,014 names

France's communes span from a single letter — Y — to a 45-character hyphen catastrophe in the Marne. In between: 11% named after saints, 1,991 rivers, and 12 places all called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-06-05 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Each one has a name. Most of those names are sensible enough — a saint, a river, a hill. But the distribution is stranger than it looks, and the extremes are genuinely remarkable.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter. Y is a village in the Somme (département 80) with 89 residents. Its full address fits on a Post-it note: "Y, 80190, Somme." It's not an abbreviation. It's not a typo. The name derives from Old French water-names, possibly from a Celtic root. It is simply Y.

At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 491 residents and the patience of a notary. The name is 45 characters long. It has 7 hyphens. It came about through a series of municipal mergers in the 19th century — and unlike many merged communes that eventually simplify their names, this one never did. The post office presumably has a rubber stamp.

The saint republic

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — have a name starting with Saint or Sainte. France is not unusual in this: the Catholic parish system shaped place-naming across the whole country, and many of those parishes were dedicated to saints whose names then became the commune name after the Revolution. What is unusual is the sheer diversity: there are hundreds of different saints represented, from the common (Michel, Pierre, Jean) to the genuinely obscure.

    The second-most-common name is Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), followed by Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each). The postal service solved this long ago — the five-digit postcode is unambiguous — but the ambiguity is a charming artefact of how a country this size settled its territory over a millennium.

    A country of rivers

    1,991 communes — 5.7% — have -sur- in their name: positioned on a river, a sea, a canal. The pattern reflects which waterways were historically important enough to organise a territory around. The Loire and the Seine are tied at 70 each. The Mer (sea) edges them out with 99, concentrated on the Normandy and Breton coasts.

    The hyphen question

    38.3% of commune names contain at least one hyphen. That's 13,410 communes. Hyphens do two jobs in French commune names: they signal a compound that was always compound (Saint-Denis, Boulogne-sur-Mer), and they signal two formerly separate communes that merged. The merger cases tend to pile up: the champion is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson with 7. The runner-up is Roche-sur-Linotte-et-Sorans-les-Cordiers (6 hyphens). Both names make exactly as much geographic sense as their history requires and no more.

    For comparison: 21,604 communes — 61.7% — have zero hyphens. These are mostly the old monosyllabic or disyllabic place-names that survived the Revolution and the merger waves intact: Eu, Uz, Ry, Sy, By. Short, dense, unambiguous. The registry gives them equal status with the 45-character behemoth. Which seems right.
