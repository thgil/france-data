What's in a name?

35,014 communes, 32,764 distinct names, one that is a single letter. France's commune names are a map of saints, hyphens, and impossible postal addresses.

2026-05-13 4 min read

France has a commune called Y. Not Yonne or Yvelines — just Y. It sits in the Somme, 89 residents, an adequate APL score of 2.78. It has been called Y since at least the 13th century, derived from the Picard word for water (éau). The post office handles it fine. The world does not.

This is the story of France's 35,014 commune names — where they come from, what patterns they make, and the extremes they reach. The data comes entirely from the national commune registry; no sampling, no filtering.

The short end

Thirteen communes have two-letter names. Sy (Ardennes), By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Oô (Haute-Garonne, and yes, both the letters are O), Oz (Isère). They are small — most have fewer than 500 residents — and many trace their names to pre-Roman or early medieval roots that simply never expanded into longer forms as language evolved around them.

The long end

At the other extreme sits a commune in the Marne with 491 residents and a name that requires a deep breath:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (51) · pop. 491

It was created by a 1972 municipal merger — two villages stitched into one administrative unit, both names preserved in full, connected with a hyphen and an et. France has four communes longer than 40 characters, all products of 20th-century mergers that chose to keep every syllable. The longest names in Europe tend to be Welsh or Polish, but for a country of this size, France's hyphenated compound names are an outlier.

The saint economy

3,882 communes whose name begins with Saint- or Sainte- — one in nine of all French communes

The Christianisation of France left a permanent mark on its map. Of those 3,882 saint communes, Sainte-Colombe is the winner: twelve communes across twelve different départements share that exact name — Savoie, Gironde, Yonne, Hérault, and eight more. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11, Saint-Aubin and Saint-Michel with 10 and 9 respectively.

This is the source of a persistent French bureaucratic headache. A letter addressed to Sainte-Colombe without a département code is genuinely ambiguous — twelve possible destinations, scattered across the country. INSEE's solution is the commune code (the familiar five-digit prefix on every French postcode), but the name ambiguity remains for everything less formal.

The name-sharing problem

It is not just the saints. Across all 35,014 communes, only 32,764 distinct names exist — meaning 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. Beaulieu appears 10 times. Le Pin, 9 times. Beaumont and Verrières, 8 each. In total, 1,450 distinct names are duplicated somewhere in France.

The hyphens are doing real work. Of the 35,014 communes, 13,410 — nearly 40% — have at least one hyphen in their name. Hyphenation is the main mechanism for making a shared name unique: when two villages share a root, one gets a geographical qualifier appended. Saint-Aubin-du-Cormier, Saint-Aubin-lès-Elbeuf, Saint-Aubin-sur-Gaillon — the same saint, three different river valleys, three unambiguous addresses.

What the names reveal

The mean commune name is 11.8 characters. The median is 10. The modal length — the single most common — falls at 9–10 characters, the sweet spot of a landscape name long enough to be specific but short enough to survive centuries of oral use. Names shorter than 5 characters are usually pre-Gallo-Roman survivals; names longer than 25 are almost always post-1960s merger products.

Like the bar map, the bakery map, and the medical-access map, the commune name map is a record of something France accumulated over centuries and then counted. Every saint, every hyphen, every one-letter Picard town is a data point. The grid is everywhere. Y is on it.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the APL 2023 dataset published by DREES, which includes all 35,014 French communes (including overseas territories and Paris/Lyon/Marseille arrondissements). The geometry for APL scores was originally sourced from the Etalab administrative boundary file. Population figures are the INSEE 2021 base used in the APL calculation. The communes-index.json file in this story's folder is a compact centroid index derived from that source and is available for reuse by other stories in this project.
