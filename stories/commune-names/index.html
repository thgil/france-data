What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve others have two-letter names. And more than one in nine is named after a saint.

2026-05-20 4 min read

The smallest commune name in France is a single vowel. Y — pronounced like the letter — sits in the Somme, département 80, population 89. It has a church. It has a mayor. It occupies exactly one slot in the national commune registry, where it is sorted between Xures (Meurthe-et-Moselle) and Yainville (Seine-Maritime). The country has 35,014 of these units of local government, and their names span a century of medieval saints, Latin geography, and administrative hyphen-stacking. Here's what the data says.

The alphabet communes

Fourteen communes have names of three characters or fewer. Y stands alone at one letter. Thirteen others clock in at two — a mix of Pyrenean fragments, Norman abbreviations, and one Corrèze anomaly that nobody fully explains.

Most of the two-letter names are in the south: Py and Ur and Uz are Catalan or Basque word-stubs fossilised into commune names. Oô in Haute-Garonne has two vowels and an impractical circumflex. Eu in Seine-Maritime was once a significant Norman town — William the Conqueror married here. Gy in Haute-Saône is the most populous two-letter commune at 1,001 residents; Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées, at 36, is the smallest.

One republic, 3,882 saints

France is a secular republic — laïque, since 1905. Its commune names did not get the memo. Of the 35,014 communes, 3,882 (11.1%) begin with Saint- or Sainte-. Pick a random commune on the map: you have better than a one-in-nine chance it's named after a saint. The most common Catholic patrons map almost exactly onto the list of France's most common commune names overall. There are nine communes called Saint-Pierre, nine called Saint-Michel, ten called Saint-Aubin. The data has a very old memory.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

France does not require commune names to be unique. Of 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have distinct names — meaning 2,250 names are shared by two or more different communes. The most duplicated is Sainte-Colombe, which appears twelve times, spread across twelve different départements from Charente to Yonne. None of them are particularly famous. All of them have a post office.

The 45-character commune

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long — about as long as Montmartre. The median is 10. But the distribution has a very long right tail: some communes are the product of mergers, where two or three existing communes were fused but no one wanted to throw away the old name.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne · département 51 · 45 characters

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, two hyphens holding three commune names together, all in the Marne. It was created in 1972 when three communes merged. The commune retains all three original names, joined with the conjunction et, because the alternative was an argument nobody could win. It is, of course, named after saints.

A dictionary of where France has been

Y takes 13 keystrokes to post a letter to (four digits of postcode, the commune name, then the département). The longest commune name takes 45. The average is 11.8. Together the 35,014 names are a kind of dictionary: of saints and rivers, of mergers and stubbornness, of Occitan word-fragments that outlasted the language that coined them. The republic calls them all equal. The names remember otherwise.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the APL 2023 dataset published by DREES, which draws on INSEE's Code Officiel Géographique. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Population figures are from INSEE 2021.
