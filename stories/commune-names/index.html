What's in a French name?

35,014 communes. 32,764 names. One of them is just "Y".

2026-05-30 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — the most of any country in the EU. Most have perfectly ordinary names: Saint-Aubin, La Croix, Bois-le-Roi. But somewhere in the Somme, there is a village called Y.

Y Commune, Somme (80), pop. 89 — the shortest place name in France

It's not an abbreviation. There's no silent letter hiding behind it. The commune is officially, irreducibly, named Y. Population: 89. Département: Somme. It has a mayor, a church, a school that closed decades ago, and the distinction of being the only one-letter commune in France.

Eu (Seine-Maritime) was once a royal residence. Oz (Isère) sits in the Alps. Oô (Haute-Garonne) — with its double O and its accent — is the one that looks like a typo.

The shape of a French name

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The most common lengths are 7 and 8 characters — short, functional, usually a single word or Saint-X compound. The distribution has a long right tail: a handful of names run to 40+ characters, legacy of rural communes fused together in the 19th and 20th centuries and hyphenated into bureaucratic marathons.

One country, 3,898 saints

The single most defining feature of the French commune name corpus is saints. 3,898 communes — more than 1 in 9 — start with Saint or Sainte. Add the ones that include a saint's name after a hyphen, and the figure reaches 4,443. France has named more of its territory after saints than any other linguistic pattern.

The most duplicated saint name? Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times across France, from Ain to Vosges. Saint-Sauveur (11 times) and Saint-Aubin (10) follow. Each name is an independent commune — there's no central registry preventing the duplication, no system of disambiguation. You just have to know which one you mean.

The hyphen as geography

13,410 communes — 38.3% of France — have a hyphen in their name. The hyphen is the tool of municipal consolidation: two villages merged, neither willing to be erased, so their names were strung together. Pont-l'Abbé-d'Arnoult. Saint-Pierre-du-Mont. Neuville-sous-Montreuil. The hyphen marks every town that survived by compromise.

The marathon names

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (Marne, 51) holds the record. It's the product of three communes fused into one administrative unit, each insisting on its own name. The result is a place that takes longer to write than most addresses.

Behind every commune name is a settlement history — a saint, a geographic feature, a conquest, a merger, a stubborn village council. The 32,764 unique names in France's commune register are not just addresses. They are a compressed archaeology of how a territory was occupied, divided, named, and renamed over a thousand years. And somewhere at the start of the alphabet, a village called Y quietly holds the record for the most economical answer to the question of what a place should be called.

A note on the data. Commune names are drawn from the communes-apl.geojson built for the medical deserts story, which joins DREES APL 2023 commune-level data to the Etalab 2025 administrative boundaries. The 35,014 features cover metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name lengths and repetitions are computed directly from the communeName field of that dataset.
