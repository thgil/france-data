What's in a name?

35,014 French communes — and a surprising number of them share theirs. One is called Y.

2026-05-12 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in the EU. Their names are an accidental archive: medieval Christian dedications, river crossings, Gallo-Roman estates, Frankish hamlets. Most are unique. A surprising share are not.

Using the same national commune database behind the medical deserts map, here is what 35,014 commune names actually look like.

The saint factory

The single strongest pattern in French commune names is the Catholic calendar. 3,882 communes — one in nine — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. The church named France's villages the same way it named its children: after saints. And one saint ran away with it.

202 communes whose name begins with Saint-Martin or Sainte-Martin. The apostle of Gaul is everywhere.

Saint-Jean (155), Saint-Pierre (144), and Saint-Germain (108) follow at a distance. The chart below covers the fifteen most prolific saints in French topography — together, they account for over 1,400 communes.

There is no the Sainte-Colombe

Among 35,014 communes, 3,700 share their name with at least one other. That is one in ten communes with an identity crisis built into the postal address. Some of the duplication is mild — two or three villages sharing a name in different départements. But at the top of the list, the redundancy becomes almost theatrical.

Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. There are ten towns called Saint-Aubin and ten called Beaulieu. The chart below shows the 15 most-duplicated exact names — if you told someone you lived in one, they'd need to ask which one.

Y

At the other extreme: brevity. France's shortest commune name is a single letter. Y is a village of 89 people in the Somme (département 80), sitting in flat farmland between Amiens and Saint-Quentin. Its name appears in the Dictionnaire topographique de la Somme as far back as 1136. Historians believe it derives from a Picard word for water, or possibly from an early Latin land record using a single initial. No one knows for certain.

Y is not alone in its compression. Thirteen communes have just two letters in their name. Most are monosyllables that feel more like initials than addresses.

Of the 13 two-letter communes, the largest is Eu in Seine-Maritime (population 6,591) — a Norman market town with a château that Louis-Philippe restored in the 1840s. The smallest is Sy in the Ardennes, population 54. Oz in the Isère is the French one, a ski resort in the Belledonne range. There is no Yellow Brick Road.

The opposite problem

France's longest commune name is a merger of several historical parishes. At 45 characters, it is the kind of name you type once and thereafter paste:

France's longest commune name — 45 characters Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

The commune is in the Marne (département 51), in the Champagne-Ardenne region. Its population is a few hundred. Its name reflects the French habit of administrative consolidation without simplification — three historical parishes fused into a single legal entity, hyphen by hyphen, without any of the constituent names being dropped.

Runner-up: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône). Third: Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados). They are all, in their way, a complete local history compressed into one impossible street address.

A note on the data. All name analysis uses the 35,014-commune dataset from the DREES APL 2023 release, which draws commune names and codes from the IGN administrative boundaries (2025 vintage). Commune counts reflect the administrative geography as of early 2025; fusions and splits since then are not reflected. Population figures are INSEE 2021 base. The two-letter commune count (13) and single-letter count (1) are exact matches on commune name length from this dataset.
