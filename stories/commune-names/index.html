What's in a name?

France has a commune named Y. It has 35,014 names in total — and 3,882 of them are saints.

2026-06-05 5 min read

France has a commune named Y.

Not an initial. Not an abbreviation. Just the letter Y, standing alone, in the Somme, population 89. It has a mayor. It has a church. It has a road sign that drivers apparently photograph so often the local council has had to replace it multiple times. It is, by one character, the shortest place name in France.

It is not alone. There are thirteen two-letter communes. Taken together, these fourteen places — Y and its slightly wordier neighbours — form an accidental club of extreme brevity.

The names in the gallery above aren't random noise. Most come from very old settlements where the name precedes standardised spelling — sometimes by centuries. Eu in Seine-Maritime traces to a Roman-era place-name. Gy in Haute-Saône has been on maps since the twelfth century. Oz in Isère is from an Alpine dialect word for sheep pasture. They survived largely because no one ever had a reason to change them.

The median commune name is ten letters long

At the other end of the scale, the average commune name runs to 11.8 characters — but the distribution is right-skewed. Most names cluster between six and twelve letters; the histogram below shows the full spread.

The longest name in France runs to 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

45 characters — Marne (51) — population 305

The runner-up, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône, reaches 43 characters. Both names result from commune mergers in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries — when two or more villages amalgamated, the policy was often to hyphenate their names together rather than choose one. The result, in extreme cases, is a postal address that takes longer to say than the village does to walk across.

One in nine communes is named after a saint

3,882
communes named Saint- or Sainte-

That is 11.1% of all communes. The saint names aren't evenly distributed: Saint-Martin alone appears 202 times across France — roughly one every 25 kilometres on average, though in practice they cluster where the cult of Saint Martin of Tours spread most strongly in the early medieval period. Saint-Jean (155 communes) and Saint-Pierre (144) round out the top three.

The dominance of Martin, Jean, and Pierre reflects the early Christianisation of Gaul. Saint Martin of Tours (316–397 AD) built an enormous network of rural monasteries across what is now central France; a commune near a Martinian chapel was simply "Saint-Martin" — and when France formalised its commune system after the Revolution, it kept the names it found. Centuries of ecclesiastical geography are still legible in the postal codes.

12 communes share the most common name

Of France's 35,014 commune names, 32,764 are unique — but 1,450 names are shared by two or more communes. The most-duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears twelve times across ten different départements. If you addressed a letter simply to "Sainte-Colombe, France", it could legitimately be delivered to any of twelve different post offices.

Most of the duplicates are saint names — Saint-Sauveur (11), Saint-Aubin (10), Saint-Marcel (9) — which makes sense: the same patron saint could be venerated independently in dozens of unconnected villages. Beaulieu (10 occurrences) is a secular outlier — a purely descriptive name meaning "beautiful place," common enough that it was coined many times over.

38% of communes have at least one hyphen

The hyphen is France's favoured municipal punctuation. Of 35,014 communes, 13,410 (38.3%) contain at least one hyphen. A small number have accumulated hyphens like barnacles: two communes have six hyphens in their names, and one — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — manages seven. That last one is also the longest name in France. Of course it is.

The hyphenation convention is a French administrative reflex: two things that coexist without fully merging are joined with a hyphen and left to coexist indefinitely. The commune. The nation. The name.

A note on the data. All commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes (metropolitan France and overseas territories) with their INSEE commune codes. The communeName field is the official INSEE name as of 2023. Lengths are counted in characters (not bytes), so accented letters (é, ô, û, etc.) count as one character each. Analysis and aggregation were done in Python from the communes-apl.geojson file already on this site.
