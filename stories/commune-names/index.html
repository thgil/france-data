What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Another is Ur. Here's what those names reveal about saints, village mergers, and one very confusing mailing address.

2026-06-03 4 min read

France has a commune named Y. Not Y-les-quelque-chose. Not Y-sur-Somme. Just Y. It sits in the Somme département, north of Paris, with 89 residents and a postal code that has caused letterboxes untold confusion since the Middle Ages. It is, by some distance, the shortest official commune name in France — and possibly in Europe.

Y gets its name from an Old Picard word meaning "water" or "river bend," rooted in the same linguistic family as the Old French aigue. The name survived every round of administrative reform France has thrown at its map. Napoleon's prefects didn't touch it. The Third Republic didn't rename it. Y remains Y.

The short-name club

Y is not alone. Thirteen more communes have two-letter names: By in the Doubs (86 residents), Eu in Seine-Maritime (6,591), Gy in Haute-Saône (1,001), and ten others scattered from the Pyrenees to Normandy. Their brevity is rarely accidental — most are corruptions of ancient Gaulish, Basque, or Occitan roots, worn down by a millennium of written administration into a single syllable.

One name, twelve communes

At the other end of the creativity spectrum: Sainte-Colombe. It exists 12 times. There are Sainte-Colombes in the Rhône (4,250 residents), the Seine-Maritime, the Yonne, the Charente-Maritime, and eight other départements. Each municipality is entirely separate — different mayors, different budgets, different weather — united only by their devotion to the same fourth-century martyr.

4,410 French communes — one in eight — have "Saint" or "Sainte" somewhere in their name. The national naming stock didn't run out exactly, but it ran thin. The top 15 most-repeated commune names below are almost entirely saints.

The 7-character sweet spot

Strip away the outliers and a clear mode emerges. Most French communes have names that are exactly 7 or 8 characters long — 3,695 at each length. The distribution drops off sharply on the short side (only 1 commune with 1 character, 13 with 2), then spreads into a long right tail of hyphenated compound names created when villages merged and refused to give up either identity.

45 characters, four saints

At the far right of that histogram sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. 45 characters. Located in the Marne (département 51). It was assembled from several villages that merged in the 1970s under France's loi Marcellin fusion programme — a well-intentioned policy that created hundreds of hyphenated marathon names. The commune is longer, in characters, than "the United States of America" and nearly twice as long as "the United Kingdom."

The second-longest, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône — also a merger product, also emphatically refusing to abbreviate. France has at least six communes that exceed 40 characters. Envelope manufacturers presumably charge extra.

A note on the data. Commune names, populations, and boundaries come from Etalab's administrative contours dataset (2025 vintage, 1000m simplification), joined to the DREES APL 2023 commune-level file. Centroids are computed as polygon centroids from the simplified geometries. The "real commune" count used in the length distribution (34,969) excludes the Paris, Lyon, and Marseille arrondissements, which are sub-communal administrative entities. The full 35,014 figure includes them.
