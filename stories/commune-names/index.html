35,000 names — and one of them is Y

France has a commune called Y. It also has 12 communes called Sainte-Colombe, a village with a 45-letter name, and 13 more communes whose names are just two letters long.

2026-05-30 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, in the flat plains of northern France, there is a village called Y. Not the Y, not an abbreviation, not a placeholder — just Y. The commune has 89 residents, a church, and the distinction of being the shortest place name in France, and quite possibly in Europe. The INSEE code is 80829. The département is 80. The name is one letter.

Y has been Y since the ninth century. Historians trace the name to a Germanic root — possibly related to water, as the village sits near wetlands — but the single letter survived every administrative reform, every post-Revolutionary renaming, every march of bureaucracy that tried to standardise the republic's 35,000 communes.

These names are old enough that they predate the conventions of French spelling. Eu is a Norman town of 6,500 people, mentioned in the Domesday Book; Gy is a market town in Haute-Saône; Oz was farming terrain in the Isère long before a certain wizard's kingdom borrowed the spelling. Oô, in the Haute-Garonne foothills of the Pyrenees, may be the only commune whose name contains two accent-bearing vowels and nothing else.

On the other end of the spectrum

At the opposite extreme is a commune in the Marne, population 491, whose name runs to 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Département 51 (Marne) · Population 491

The length is a merger artefact. France has absorbed thousands of commune fusions since the 1970 loi Marcellin, which encouraged small communes to consolidate. Two or three villages that refused to lose their identities entirely would hyphenate their names together. The result is a roster of hyphenated marathons: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, 923 residents), Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters), La Vacquerie-et-Saint-Martin-de-Castries (40 characters). The longer the name, the more communes it once was.

One in nine communes is a saint

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,882 — just over 11% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That's the legacy of medieval Christianity's grip on place-naming: a saint for every valley, a chapel on every hill. The names fan out across the entire country, but they concentrate in the west and centre — old Brittany, Poitou, the Limousin — where the pace of rural life changed slowly and the saints stayed.

The most common single name in France, shared by the most communes, is Sainte-Colombe: twelve separate villages, spread across eleven différent départements, all named after the same third-century saint. Saint-Sauveur comes second (11 communes), then Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu at 10 each.

152 chapels, each with its own qualifier

Then there's the chapelle problem. France has 152 communes whose name starts with La Chapelle, all of which have tacked on a suffix to distinguish themselves from the others. 2,250 communes without a unique name

France's 35,014 communes share only 32,764 distinct names. That 2,250-commune gap means over 3,700 places — more than 10% of the total — share their official name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. None of this causes administrative problems: the INSEE five-digit code is what matters for official purposes, not the name. But it does mean that Beaulieu is ten different places, and if you look up Saint-Martin on the map, you will get the wrong one.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which includes all 35,014 French communes (metropolitan France and overseas territories) with their INSEE codes, names, and populations. The APL file was originally assembled for the medical deserts story but the commune names are a public INSEE register. Name counts, length distributions, and duplicate analysis were computed from this dataset. Totals may differ slightly from the definitive INSEE CODGEO register depending on commune mergers and dissolutions since the data vintage.
