What's in a commune name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve share the name Sainte-Colombe. One in eight is named after a saint.

2026-05-25 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, département 80, with 89 residents, an official INSEE code (80829), and a name that consists of a single letter: Y. Not Y-something. Not Yville or Yseux. Just Y. It is the shortest commune name in France, and quite possibly in Europe.

Y has existed since at least the Middle Ages. The name is thought to derive from a Picard word for water or a low-lying place — which, in the flat, waterlogged marshes of the Somme, makes geographic sense. Whether the locals find it funny that their address ends with "Y, France" is not recorded in the official data.

The thirteen two-letter communes

The most populous two-letter commune is Eu (Seine-Maritime), a town of 6,591 people whose name comes from the river Bresle — and which once hosted the English royal family (Edward III met the Valois court there in 1358). The least populous is Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées), a hamlet of 36. Together these 13 communes account for around 15,000 people — roughly the population of a mid-sized French town living under names that would earn maximum points in a game of Scrabble.

The distribution of lengths

Most commune names fall between 6 and 13 characters. The peak is around 7–8 characters: names like Sarlat, Roannez, Beaumont. The mean is 11.8 characters — pulled upward by the long tail of hyphenated compound names that make up more than a third of all communes.

At the other extreme: 45 characters

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne (département 51). At 45 characters, it comfortably exceeds the next longest (Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur at 43). Both names are the product of commune fusions — the process by which smaller villages merged under a shared administrative name. When three or four medieval villages combine, the new name inherits all their identities, concatenated with hyphens, creating something that barely fits on a road sign.

The 12 Sainte-Colombes

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names. That means 2,250 names are shared by more than one commune — 6.4% of all names are duplicates. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times, in departments ranging from the Hautes-Alpes to the Seine-et-Marne. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear 10 times.

This is why French addresses include the department number. When you write to Sainte-Colombe, the postal system needs more information — which of 12 identically named villages you actually mean.

One in eight is a saint

4,443 communes — 12.7% of France — have "saint" somewhere in their name

The dominance of saint names is a direct legacy of medieval Christianity mapping itself onto the landscape. Every patron saint of every village church eventually became the name of the place itself. 3,596 communes start with Saint or Sainte as a standalone prefix — another 847 contain "saint" embedded in a compound name like Moissac-Bellevue-Saint-Cézaire.

The département with the most saint-named communes is Dordogne (130), followed by Gironde (121) and Isère (106). These are old, deeply rural departments where medieval parish boundaries fossilised into today's administrative map.

The hyphen capital of Europe

France uses hyphens in commune names more than any other European country. 13,410 communes — 38.3% — have at least one hyphen. This reflects both the saint-name tradition (Saint-Pierre-de-Chartreuse needs two hyphens just to exist) and the fusion phenomenon: when medieval villages merge, the new name is formed by hyphenation rather than by choosing one name over another. No village is erased; all are preserved, strung together into something that looks like a password requirement and reads like a charter from 1247.

The result is a national registry that is simultaneously a history of medieval geography, a chronicle of administrative mergers, a saint's calendar, and — in the case of Y — a single letter standing patiently in the marshes of the Somme.
