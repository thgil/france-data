What's in a commune name?

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other EU country. Their names have been accumulating for a thousand years. Here's what they say.

2026-06-06 4 min read

After the Revolution, France reorganised itself into communes — small, autonomous administrative units. It inherited thousands of parishes, seigneuries, and villages that had been named across medieval Latin, Old French, Celtic, Germanic, Occitan, Basque, Breton, and Alsatian. Nobody renamed them. The result is the most densely-named country in Europe: 35,014 communes, each carrying its own etymology, each a capsule of the landscape and faith of whoever got there first.

One in nine communes is a saint

The most striking pattern in the dataset: 3,882 communes — one in nine — start with Saint- or Sainte-. They are spread from the Pyrénées to the Ardennes, from the Atlantic coast to the Alsatian plain. The country is, in its postal infrastructure, a hagiography.

The most common is Saint-Martin, with 202 communes bearing the name of the fourth-century Bishop of Tours. Saint-Jean (155), Saint-Pierre (144), and Saint-Germain (108) complete the top four. If you tell someone you live in Saint-Martin, they will ask you: which one?

At the other end of the alphabet and the character count: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (Marne, 51), 45 characters long. This is a commune created by merging two medieval parishes, and neither side was willing to disappear from the name. The hyphen is a monument to negotiation.

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. The median sits around 11. There's a long tail: dozens of names stretch past 30 characters, each one a merger or a legacy compound that survived every administrative reform.

The hyphenated republic

38.3% of French communes — 13,410 of them — have at least one hyphen. No other large country organises its place names this way at this scale. The pattern is almost always the same: a place name, a preposition, and a geographic anchor.

Prepositions in commune names

Most common first word

The most common preposition is -sur-: 1,991 communes are "on" something — a river, a lake, a canal, a sea. Selles-sur-Cher. Bagnères-de-Bigorre. Cluny is just Cluny, but the abbey is near a river, so its postal neighbours got Cormatin and Taizé. The -lès- preposition (570 communes) is older: a medieval French word meaning "near," used to orient small villages relative to a larger neighbour. Ivry-la-Bataille fought there. Gif-sur-Yvette has a particle accelerator. The prepositions are a geography lesson disguised as a postal address.

A thousand years of naming

The oldest name patterns — the ones ending in -ac, -ens, -os, -oc — are Gallo-Roman, often from a Latin owner's name followed by a suffix meaning "estate of." The -ville and -court endings are Frankish (Germanic settlers in northern France). The Breton names clustered in Finistère and Côtes-d'Armor are a different language family entirely. Alsatian -willer and -heim endings trace the historical reach of German settlement. The saints are mostly medieval, though Martin of Tours was already famous in the fifth century.

No one planned any of this. The names were given, absorbed, written down, and then in 1790 frozen into administrative law. Subsequent mergers — communes nouvelles, the modern consolidation program — occasionally produce the 35-character compounds. But the underlying layer, the hagiographic and geographic sediment, is unchanged. France carries its medieval naming system into the twenty-first century, hyphen and all.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (the same source used in the medical deserts story), joined to Etalab's contours administratifs 2025. That dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories.
