Y, and 35,013 other names

From a one-letter village in the Somme to a 45-character merger in the Marne — France's commune names are a compressed history of the country.

2026-05-14 5 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union — 35,014 of them, about as many as the rest of the EU combined. Each carries a name. Most are long, hyphenated, saint-prefixed affairs. One is not.

Y Commune in the Somme (département 80) · Population: 89 · France's shortest place name

Y sits in a bend of the Somme river, about 20 km southeast of Amiens. Its name — one letter, nothing more — predates French entirely. The most likely etymology is Gaulish or pre-Latin: a root meaning "yew tree" or simply "water," worn down to a single phoneme over two millennia of use. The commune survived Napoleon's administrative reforms, the 1871 and 1914–18 wars, and every subsequent rationalisation of French territory. It is still Y.

The two-letter club

Below Y, thirteen communes share the distinction of two-letter names. They are scattered across France — from Eu in Normandy (population 6,591, and yes, one of the oldest towns in France) to Oz in the Isère Alps (213 people, a ski resort) and Ur near the Spanish border in the Pyrénées-Orientales. Short names survive where names were never written down in the first place: they were sounds before they were letters, and the sounds were too short to erode further.

One in nine is a saint

3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of all communes

Medieval France was thoroughly Christianised at the village level. When settlements needed names, the local church's patron saint was the obvious choice. Over centuries those saints accumulated: Saint-Pierre, Saint-Jean, Saint-Martin, Sainte-Marie. The prefix "Saint" alone appears in 3,587 commune names; "Sainte" adds another 295. Together they account for more than one in nine French communes.

After the saints come the chapels. La Chapelle — the local chapel as a landmark — opens 147 commune names, making it the most common non-saint prefix in France. Villers (from the Frankish villare, a farmstead) appears 103 times, a trace of the early medieval settlement pattern. Villeneuve (new town) shows up 64 times, and Pont (bridge) 61 times — geography as destiny, settlements named for the feature that made them worth founding.

The most duplicated name in France

Even within the saint universe, some names cluster far more than others. The single most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by twelve separate communes across nine different départements. Saint-Sauveur (11), Saint-Aubin (10), and Beaulieu (10) follow. Of France's 35,014 communes, 32,764 have unique names — meaning 2,250 share their name with at least one other commune. Addressing a letter to "Saint-Martin" somewhere in France is, technically, sending it to one of nine places.

The 45-character record

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Commune in the Marne (département 51) · Population: 491 · 45 characters · France's longest commune name

The longest names are almost all merger artifacts. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is the product of a 1972 fusion de communes: Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont absorbed Saint-Genest-et-Isson and kept both names, joined with a hyphen and a conjunction. The "et" (and) in many long commune names is the giveaway — it marks the seam where two smaller communes were sewn together. The second-longest, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône (43 characters), is the same story: four villages, one administrative record.

France has been rationalising its commune map — slowly — since the 1970s. The 1971 Marcellin Law encouraged mergers; the 2015 NOTRe Act renewed the push. But French communes resist consolidation with impressive stubbornness. The 35,014 number has barely moved in half a century.

The shape of a French place name

Strip out the outliers and the distribution is clear: most commune names cluster between five and nine characters, with a mode around seven or eight. The average is 11.8 characters — pulled upward by the long saint-hyphen compounds. A name like Bû (2 characters, Eure-et-Loir) or Gy (2 characters, Haute-Saône) is genuinely rare. A name like Saint-Martin-de-Ré (18 characters) is perfectly ordinary.

What the histogram doesn't show is the texture. Oô (2 characters, Haute-Garonne) is a Pyrenean village whose name is a phoneme, not a word — the double-o is the Gascon word for "water." Bû is a corruption of something pre-Roman. Gex (3 characters, Ain) is Celtic. Gap (3 characters, Hautes-Alpes, pop. 40,500) is Gaulish. The short names are the oldest names: compressed to their irreducible minimum and left there.

France's 35,014 communes are a legal category, a budget line, an electoral constituency. They are also an etymology project nobody asked for — 35,014 small puzzles about where people settled, what they worshipped, which river they built next to, and whose name they were trying to honour or forget. Y, for its part, has been Y for longer than France has existed as a concept. That seems like a reasonable kind of permanence.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (commune-level general practitioner accessibility scores), which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories as of the 2021 INSEE census base.
