What's in a French commune name?

35,014 communes, one called Y. France's shortest place name is a single letter — its longest runs to 45 characters. In between: 3,882 saints, 13,410 hyphens, and 12 villages all called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-17 5 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — the most atomised local-government layer in Europe. Most have names nobody outside the département has ever heard of. But the names themselves are a census of language, religion, and landscape: the saint-worship of medieval France, the Latin of Roman roads, the Celtic roots of Brittany, the German endings of Alsace. And somewhere in the middle of all of it, in the Somme, is a village that decided one letter was enough.

Y

The commune of Y (pronounced like the French letter i grec) sits on the Somme plain, 20 kilometres from Amiens. Population 89. It is, officially, France's shortest commune name — and quite plausibly the shortest place name of any incorporated municipality in Europe. Its brevity appears to have medieval roots: the name derives from the Germanic root aue, meaning a water meadow, compressed by centuries of use into a single vowel.

The other end of the spectrum

If Y is the minimum, the maximum belongs to the Marne. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson clocks in at 45 characters — the result of multiple commune mergers that France's fusions de communes policy has been producing since the 1970s. Each merger appends the absorbed commune's name with a hyphen or an -et-. The names grow without bound. They are bureaucratic barnacles.

The shape of French place names

Most communes land between 6 and 20 characters. The distribution is right-skewed: a long tail of compound, hyphenated names pulls the average up to 11.8 characters. The modal bucket is 7–8 characters — names like Charlieu, Montbrun, Vézénobres. Short, complete, unambiguously French.

One in nine communes is a saint

France was Christianised early and thoroughly. 3,587 communes begin with Saint- and another 295 with Sainte- — together, 3,882 communes, or 11.1% of the national total, are named after a saint. That is more than 1 in 9. If you pick a random French commune, there's a better-than-even chance the name begins with S — because the S column is flooded by saints.

Sainte-Colombe, twelve times over

Of 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have unique names — meaning 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. This is a direct consequence of the saint-naming convention: once you've named something after a popular saint, you've guaranteed a naming collision somewhere else in the country. The most contested name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in 12 départements, from Manche in Normandy to Haute-Garonne in the south-west. They have never coordinated. They never needed to — the postal code and commune code do the disambiguation.

The name is the history

French commune names are a compressed atlas of the country's history. The Saint- wave reflects Carolingian and medieval Christianity. The La/Le/Les cluster marks villages named for a landscape feature — La Montagne, Le Bois, Les Rochers. The hyphenated compound names are mostly 20th-century mergers, bureaucratic sediment from a policy that tried to rationalise 36,000 communes down to something more manageable. (It didn't quite work.) The two-letter names at the short end — By, Sy, Ur, Uz — are pre-Roman survivals, syllables that outlasted the languages that coined them.

Somewhere in the Somme, 89 people live in a place called Y. They pay taxes in Y. They receive post addressed to Y. Their birth certificates read Y. The whole name fits in a single letter. France does not find this strange.

A note on the data. Commune names and counts come from the DREES APL dataset joined to administrative boundaries, already on file as stories/medical-deserts/communes-apl.geojson (35,014 features, INSEE vintage 2025). Centroids are computed as the mean of each polygon's exterior ring. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL dataset. Name analysis was done with Python 3.
