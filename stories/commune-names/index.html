35,014 communes, one obsession

France has more places named Sainte-Colombe than it does Nobel laureates. An exploration of what 35,000 place names reveal about a country.

2026-05-24 5 min read

Ask twelve communes their name. Each one, independently, will tell you: Sainte-Colombe. They are scattered across twelve different départements — Gironde, Yonne, Haute-Savoie, Normandie, Charente-Maritime — none of them neighbours, none of them the most important, all of them named after the same third-century martyr from Sens. Welcome to the French commune naming system.

France's 35,014 communes have accumulated their names over a thousand years of settlement, conquest, merger, and bureaucratic accident. The results are, by turns, repetitive, poetic, and occasionally baffling. Sainte-Colombe wins the frequency contest. But the data contains richer things than the winner.

The most duplicated names

Among 35,014 communes, 32,764 distinct names are used — which means 2,250 names are shared by at least two places. The top of the frequency list reads like a roll call from a medieval hagiography.

Beaulieu — "beautiful place" — appears ten times. Beaumont — "beautiful hill" — eight. Both are descriptive names that independent communities in different regions converged on for obvious reasons. The saints are different: their prevalence is not coincidence but policy. The Catholic Church spread its calendar across every valley, and villages that built a chapel to the same saint often adopted the same name.

The saint economy

3,898 communes — 11.1% of France — start with Saint or Sainte. That's more named communes than most European countries have communes total.

One name prefix, 3,898 uses. Saint-Martin leads with 203 communes — enough to fill the Stade de France with villagers all sharing the same postal prefix. Saint-Jean follows with 155, Saint-Pierre with 144. The top fifteen saints between them account for 1,283 communes.

Saint-Martin's dominance is not accidental. Martin of Tours was the first non-martyr to be canonised by the Catholic Church and the patron saint of France before Joan of Arc took the role. His feast day (November 11 — still Armistice Day on the calendar) and his popularity in the early medieval period meant that settlements founded between the 5th and 12th centuries reached for his name naturally. The result is 203 communes who share his name, plus hundreds more who append it to a hyphenated compound: Saint-Martin-d'Hères, Saint-Martin-de-Crau, Saint-Martin-en-Bresse, and so on.

The alphabet extremes

The shortest commune name in France — and possibly one of the shortest in Europe — is a single letter.

Shortest name

Y

Département de la Somme (80). Population: 89. Named from a Germanic root meaning "water" or "stream". Sitting along the Somme river, it has been spelled Y since at least the 12th century.

Longest name (45 characters)

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-
Saint-Genest-et-Isson

Département de la Marne (51). Population: 491. The name dates from a 1972 merger of three communes: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. Rather than choose, they kept all three.

Between Y and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson lies the full range of French place-name logic. Y's brevity is almost certainly an accident of phonetic survival — long vowel sounds that lost their consonants over a millennium of French evolution. The long names are the opposite: deliberate accumulations, communes merged by administrative decree in the 1970s who preserved their identity in the only way the law allowed — by concatenating their names with hyphens and et.

There are also 13 two-letter communes (Sy, By, Oô, Oz, Gy, Eu, Ry, and others), each a small grammatical puzzle, and 154 communes with three-letter names. The name-length distribution peaks at 7–8 characters — roughly the length of a French monosyllable with an article, or a saint name without embellishment.

A spectacularly diverse system

Here is the surprising number: 89.4% of French communes — 31,314 of them — have a name that exists nowhere else in France. One commune, one name, found in exactly that spot and nowhere else on the map.

For a country famous for administrative centralisation, this is remarkable. The Fifth Republic has standardised everything from school hours to bread weights. But commune names were effectively frozen centuries before the Republic existed, and France chose to preserve them rather than rationalise them. The result is a dataset of extraordinary local particularity: Abergement-Clémenciat, Abzac, Achicourt, each unrepeated.

The 2,250 shared names are the exception, not the rule. And within that group, the saint names dominate precisely because they were the one common cultural input — the Catholic Church's calendar — that reached every corner of medieval France at the same time. Strip out the saints and the Le/La/Les prefixes, and the underlying name diversity becomes even starker.

Twelve places called Sainte-Colombe. Thirty-one thousand places called nothing else. France is, officially, very hard to name.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures are drawn from INSEE's contours administratifs 2025 dataset, via the GeoJSON file generated for Story 2 (medical deserts). The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Names are as recorded in the INSEE administrative database at the 2025 vintage — post-merger names for communes that joined after 1970 appear in their current merged form.
