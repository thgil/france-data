What's in a name?

One commune is called Y. Another is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. France's 35,000 commune names, ranked by everything.

2026-05-25 4 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, 89 people live in a commune called Y. Not an abbreviation. Not a code. The official name, the one on street signs and postcards and INSEE records, is a single letter: Y.

It is the shortest commune name in France. It has been called Y since at least the 12th century, when the lords of Picardic marshland saw no reason to be verbose. The post office has made peace with it. The internet has not.

The bell curve of place names

Most commune names are 7 or 8 characters long. The distribution peaks there and falls off on both sides — a familiar bell shape, with Y as a single red outlier at the far left and a long tail of compound names stretching to 45.

The saint problem

3,891 communes — 11.1% of the total — have names beginning with Saint or Sainte. France evangelised its geography so thoroughly that if you pick a French commune at random, there's roughly a one-in-nine chance it's named after a saint.

This would be manageable if the saints were all different. They are not. Sainte-Colombe appears twelve times. Saint-Sauveur eleven. Saint-Aubin ten. Saint-Marcel, Saint-Michel, Saint-Pierre, and Sainte-Marie each appear nine times. When you write a letter to Saint-Martin, the postal service must first decide which of the nine Saint-Martins you mean.

In total, 1,450 distinct names are shared between two or more communes — accounting for 3,700 communes in all. More than one in ten French communes shares its name with at least one other.

The vocabulary of the map

Strip each commune name to its first word, and a geography of the French imagination emerges. Saint leads by an enormous margin — 3,596 communes. Then La (1,054), Le (758), Les (328), Sainte (295). After the articles: Villers (a Germanic root meaning "settlement"), Villeneuve ("new town"), Val, Pont, Fontaine. The landscape arrives next — hills, valleys, bridges, springs — all named once and then named again, a hundred kilometres away, by people who had never met.

The other end

At the opposite extreme from Y sits the longest commune name in France: seven hyphens, 45 characters, a small commune in the Marne:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (51) · population 491 · 7 hyphens · 45 characters

It was formed in 1972 by the merger of three previously separate communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — and has been a bureaucratic curiosity ever since. It is not alone: France has 23 communes with five or more hyphens, and 166 with four or more.

The longest names are overwhelmingly products of 20th-century municipal mergers, where no village was willing to cede its name entirely and a compromise was struck in hyphens. Y, by contrast, has never merged with anything. It is, and has always been, Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories (Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Guyane, Mayotte). Name lengths are measured in characters including hyphens, spaces, and diacritics. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base embedded in the APL dataset. The lightweight communes-index.json file (3 MB) in this story folder contains all 35,014 communes with their names, codes, département, population, and centroid coordinates — freely reusable by future stories.
