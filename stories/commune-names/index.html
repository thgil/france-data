What France calls its villages

From Y (one letter, population 89) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters). Inside 35,014 commune names.

May 2026 5 min read Culture

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in the EU, most of them very small, and all of them with a name. Those names span from a single vowel to a 45-character sentence involving two saints, a river junction, and an ampersand. The average is 11.8 characters, which is already longer than you'd expect, because the French have a particular affection for hyphens.

Y

The shortest commune name in France is, simply, Y. One letter. It sits in the Somme, département 80, between Roye and Saint-Quentin. Population: 89. The village has been named Y since the Middle Ages — the etymology is disputed, but the leading theory is that it derives from a word meaning "water" in a pre-Latin Picard dialect, cognate with words like eau. The name has the singular distinction of being both the entire village and a complete sentence in French internet slang.

Below Y sit thirteen communes with two-letter names. Hover any name to see where it sits.

The two-letter club is geographically scattered, but there's a notable cluster of short names in the Pyrenees — Py, Ur, and Uz all sit in Pyrénées-Orientales and Hautes-Pyrénées, where Occitan and Catalan roots produce very different consonant clusters than standard French. Oô (Haute-Garonne) has the rare distinction of an umlaut.

One in nine is a saint

France's great naming tradition is sainthood. Exactly 3,898 commune names start with Saint or Sainte — that's 11.1 percent of the entire country. Add the saints buried inside compound names and the total climbs higher. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times, spread from Hautes-Alpes to Charente-Maritime. Saint-Sauveur follows at 11; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu at 10 each.

Most common commune names — top 15

The dominance of Saint-names isn't just historical — it reflects the fact that most French villages were formally named (or renamed) during the medieval period, when the local church was the primary administrative unit. When the Revolution briefly stripped "Saint" from many place names, most reverted after 1815.

Sur

After saints, the most common word in French commune names is sur — "on" or "on the" — which appears 1,991 times, always followed by a river, a feature, or an adjacent town. Communes define themselves by what they sit on: Conflans-Sainte-Honorine is on the Seine, Châlons-sur-Saône is on the Saône, Neuilly-sur-Marne is on the Marne. A further 1,007 commune names use lès (an archaic preposition meaning "near"), which is distinct from the near-homophone les but indicates proximity rather than adjacency. In practice, this means some communes have names that look like typos.

The long ones

At the far end of the length distribution sits a commune in the Marne that seems to have run out of space on its own sign.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (dept. 51) · 45 characters · population ~300 · two saints, one commune

The name is the result of a commune merger: three villages — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — were combined into one administrative unit and all three kept their names, joined with hyphens and an et. The runner-up is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône): 43 characters, four place-names, one commune, zero short forms in daily use.

Long names are not random: almost all of them are merger artifacts. France has been consolidating communes since the 1970s under various laws, but the merged units often insist on keeping every predecessor's name rather than choosing one. The result is a growing tail on the length distribution.

The shape of 35,014 names

Commune name length distribution — number of communes by character count

The distribution peaks at 7 and 8 characters — short, single-word names, often the names of rivers, hills, or forests that the first settlers found nearby. The long right tail (names above 25 characters) is entirely composed of merged communes with hyphenated compound names. The median is 10 characters; the average is 11.8, pulled right by those outliers.

Starting letters tell a similar story: S leads with 5,598 communes (16%), almost entirely because of the Saint family. L is second at 4,279 — Le, La, Les. C is third at 3,696, followed by B and M. Z and W barely register; X has 29 communes, mostly in Occitan and Basque country.

Notes

Population figures are INSEE 2021 estimates as reported in the APL dataset. Some very small communes report zero; the village of Y (code 80829) reports 89. Length calculations use character count of the official name string, including accents and diacritics (which count as single characters in Unicode). The 13 two-letter communes listed include all communes whose official communeName field contains exactly two Unicode characters.
