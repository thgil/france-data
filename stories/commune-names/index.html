What's in a Name?

France has 35,014 communes. Their names encode a thousand years of history — saints, rivers, hilltops, and one hamlet with a single-letter name.

2026-06-01 5 min read
Y

This is a real French commune. It's in the Somme, department 80. Its INSEE code is 80829. Its population is 89. It has a name that is, by some measures, perfect: one letter, no ambiguity, nothing to misspell.

Y is the shortest commune name in France — and the only one-letter place name in the entire country. The post office delivers mail to it. French administrative databases file it. If you look it up on a map, it's there: a small cluster of farms in the Santerre plateau, between Amiens and Saint-Quentin, quietly existing under a name that is just a vowel.

The hidden alphabet

Y is the outlier, but it's not alone. France has 13 communes with two-letter names — survivals from the deep substrate of Gaulish, Frankish, and Basque place-names that the centuries could not quite smooth away. Most are unassuming villages; Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591) is a rare exception, a medieval walled town.

Step up to three letters and there are 154 more: Gap (Hautes-Alpes, pop. 40,500), Dax (Landes, pop. 21,347), Pau (Pyrénées-Atlantiques, pop. 77,066). Short names cluster in the south and west — the Basque Country, the Pyrénées, Corsica — where pre-Romance languages left the deepest marks on the map.

One in nine is a saint

3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. Add every commune that contains "saint" anywhere in its name (Saint-Jean-de-Luz, Villandraut-Saint-Hilaire) and you reach 4,443. That's 12.7% of France named, at least partly, after a saint. Roughly six million people live in a commune beginning with Saint or Sainte.

11.1% of French communes start with Saint or Sainte — more than one in nine

The saintliest département is Ardèche (dept 07), where 97 of 335 communes — 29% — carry a saint's name. Haute-Vienne (87) and Loire (42) follow at 27–28%. These are the old heartlands of French Christianity, evangelised from Lyon along the Rhône corridor in the early centuries CE. The names still show it.

The most repeated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which exists in 12 different departments — from Haute-Garonne in the south to Côte-d'Or in the north. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each 10 times. Of the 35,014 communes, 2,250 (6.4%) share their name with at least one other commune in France. Every letter addressed to "Sainte-Colombe" requires a department code to arrive.

The merger monuments

At the other extreme: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. Marne département. Population 491. Three hamlets were merged by prefectoral decree and each insisted its name come along. The result is the longest commune name in France, and a small monument to rural administrative stubbornness.

These hyphenated giants are a 20th-century phenomenon. The 1971 Marcellin law encouraged rural communes to merge to save costs. Many did — but they negotiated their names as a condition. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône, 43 chars) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (Calvados, 41 chars) tell the same story: the villages merged, the identities didn't.

The first word tells the story

The most common first word of a commune name isn't an article — it's Saint, at 3,596 instances. Then come the articles: La (1,054), Le (758), Les (328), Sainte (295). After the saints and articles, the landscape appears: Villers (105 — from the Latin villare, a hamlet), Villeneuve (70 — "new town"), Val (67 — a valley), Pont (63 — a bridge), Fontaine (58 — a spring).

The name is a compressed portrait of medieval France. Villages were named for what was nearest and most memorable: the water source (Fontaine, Pont, Rivière), the hill (Mont, Val), the settlement pattern (Villers, Villeneuve, Bourg), and the local saint. Christianity did the work of standardisation across dozens of dialects and centuries, leaving its mark on 11% of the map.

Y, meanwhile, remains gloriously unnamed.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the communes-apl.geojson file built for Story 2 (medical deserts), which joins DREES APL 2023 commune scores to the Etalab administrative-boundary polygons (2025 vintage, 35,014 communes including overseas territories). Name analysis was done entirely from this in-repo dataset. The communes-index.json file in this story folder is a 3 MB lightweight index of all 35,014 communes with name, code, department, population, and centroid coordinates — available for reuse by future stories.
