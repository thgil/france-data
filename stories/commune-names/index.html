35,014 names

Every commune in France has a name. Most names belong to just one commune. But 3,700 don't — and the winner might surprise you.

2026-05-14 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — every village, town, borough, and arrondissement in the republic, each one a named piece of administrative geography. Most of those names are unique: 31,314 communes (89.4%) have a name no other commune in France shares.

But 3,700 do share their name. Across 1,450 repeated names, France has doubles, triples, and in one case, a name used by twelve different communes, scattered across seven different regions. That name is not Saint-Martin. It is not Saint-Pierre. It is Sainte-Colombe.

The top 20 most repeated names

Twelve separate communes share the name Sainte-Colombe. If you mail a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France" without a postal code, La Poste has a problem. Saint-Sauveur runs second with eleven. The saints dominate — but Beaulieu (10) and Beaumont (8) show that the pattern goes beyond the Catholic calendar.

One in nine communes is a saint

3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of France

France has 3,392 distinct saint names in use as commune names — a staggering number, reflecting the medieval boom in parish dedications that shaped the map of rural France for a thousand years. Some saints appear once. Others appear nine times: Saint-Michel, Saint-Marcel, Saint-Pierre. The Catholic naming convention created a coordination problem that the French postal system has had to live with ever since.

Even within the saints, the non-saint repeaters are notable. Beaulieu — "beautiful place" — appears ten times across Ardèche, Aveyron, Corrèze, Drôme, Haute-Loire, Haute-Vienne, Hérault, Loire-et-Cher, and Seine-et-Marne. Beautiful places, apparently, were common enough that ten different villages reached for the same name.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont

At the short end of the spectrum, one commune wins outright: Y, in the Somme (department 80), population 109. One letter. Not an abbreviation — the full official name. It is the shortest commune name in France, and possibly in the world. At the other extreme, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne stretches to 45 characters, a hyphenated merger of several villages whose combined name outlasted the administrative logic that produced it.

Between those extremes, twelve communes manage their name in two letters — Eu (Seine-Maritime), Oz (Isère), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), and others. None of them are abbreviations. They are old place-names, often of Celtic or pre-Latin origin, that simply never grew longer. The French sense of administrative seriousness has preserved them faithfully — Y has a website, a mayor, and a full INSEE commune code (80579).

The uniqueness paradox

Despite all the repetition, the overwhelming pattern is uniqueness. 89.4% of French communes have a name no other commune shares. France is not a country of cookie-cutter place names — it is a country of L'Abergement-Clémenciat, Quinsac, Mézin, and Île de Clipperton. The 31,314 unique names are a linguistic record of regional languages, local geography, historical ownership, and the slow accretion of administrative decisions over twelve centuries.

The 3,700 communes with shared names are the exceptions that prove the rule. And in a country with 35,014 communes, even a dozen Sainte-Colombes is a surprisingly small number — the map of France is not as repetitive as the saints might make it seem.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes are drawn from the DREES APL 2023 extract, which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories (including Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, and Mayotte). The population field is from INSEE 2021. The same underlying data powers the medical deserts story. Commune name counts are exact matches (case-sensitive, accent-sensitive) — variants like Saint-Martin and Saint-Martin-des-Champs are counted separately.
