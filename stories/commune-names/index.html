What's in a Name?

35,014 French communes — one is called Y, twelve are Sainte-Colombe, and more than one in ten are named after a saint.

2026-05-23 5 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. One letter. Legally, administratively, permanently: Y. It sits in the Somme, in the Hauts-de-France region, with 89 residents, a church, and what must be a truly exhausting experience when answering any form that asks for your town of birth. The phone company does not believe Y is a real place. The GPS software asks you to try again.

Y is not a fluke or a nickname. It is the official name on the French commune register — INSEE code 80829 — and it has been so since well before the republic. It is also the shortest commune name in France. On the other end of the scale, in the Marne, there is a commune whose full official name is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. That is 45 characters, 7 hyphens, and presumably a postman who has developed opinions about urban planning.

Most names are 7 or 8 characters long

The length distribution peaks at 7–8 characters — names like Bordeaux, Limoges, Chartres, Cabanès. Half of all communes have names between 5 and 10 characters. The long tail stretches out to 45 characters for a handful of merged communes that accumulated their names through history, two towns stitched together, sometimes three, with et-Isson tacked on the end as a formality.

The post office's nightmare

10.6% of communes — 3,700 in total — share their name with at least one other commune. The most duplicated: Sainte-Colombe, which exists twelve times across twelve different départements. Each is its own village, its own mayor, its own school, its own fête nationale. They are not related. They all happened to be named after the same saint.

Below Sainte-Colombe: Saint-Sauveur (11 times), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each), Saint-Marcel, Saint-Michel, Le Pin, Saint-Pierre, and Sainte-Marie (9 each). The duplicates are overwhelmingly saint names — saints were the naming default for a millennium, and saints are not unique to one region.

Most common commune names

One commune in nine is a saint

3,882 French communes — 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. That is more than one in eleven. Saint-Martin alone accounts for 202 village names, making France's patron saint the most popular commune name-root in the country. Saint-Jean follows with 155. Together, the top five saint suffixes — Martin, Jean, Pierre, Germain, Julien — cover over 600 communes between them.

The saint density is highest in the southwest. The Dordogne (département 24) leads with 128 saint-named communes — about a third of all its communes. The Gironde (33) follows with 121, then the Isère (38) and the Saône-et-Loire (71) with 105 each. These are old regions with deep Catholic administrative traditions: the church named villages for a thousand years before the republic renamed much of anything.

89% of communes are one of a kind

Despite all the saints, 89.4% of French communes — 31,314 of them — have a name that appears nowhere else in France. Béziers is only one place. So is Mortagne-sur-Gironde. So is Oô (two letters, 103 residents, département 31, contains a character that breaks German spell-checkers). The name survey of France is really a survey of the continent's historical imagination: Roman place-names, Frankish tribal markers, Gaulish river-crossings, church dedications, obscure saints, and then a long tail of Le Pin (9 of them) and Beaulieu (10) where medieval settlers looked at a hill and reached for the most obvious word available.

Y was probably not obvious. It was probably a ford, or a bend, or something in a language that has been gone for a long time. The 89 people who live there today will tell you it's fine.

A note on the data. Commune names, population figures, and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset — specifically the communes-apl.geojson file used in Story 2 (medical deserts), which includes all 35,014 French communes. Name analysis was performed in Python. Commune counts include overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte). A small number of records with null or missing names were excluded.
