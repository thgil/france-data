What's in a name?

35,014 communes. One is called Y. 3,898 start with Saint. The longest name needs seven hyphens to fit.

2026-06-06 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes, and their names stretch from a single capital letter to a 45-character hyphenated clause that takes a breath to read aloud. The shortest is Y — one letter, population 89, tucked into the Somme in northern France. It has a mayor. It has a road sign. The road sign says Y.

Thirteen communes manage exactly two letters: By (Doubs), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées), Ri (Orne), and Bû (Eure-et-Loir). The Pyrénées clearly like short names.

A bell curve that skews right

Most commune names cluster around 7–8 characters — the sweet spot for something pronounceable, distinctive, and printable on a road sign. But the distribution has a long right tail: hundreds of communes carry names longer than 20 characters, and 50 push past 30. There is also a curious secondary hump in the 16–20 character range — an artifact of France's most productive naming template: Saint-Xxx-de-Yyy and Xxx-sur-Yyy, which reliably land in that band.

One in nine communes is a saint

3,898 communes — 11.1% of France — start their name with Saint or Sainte. No other country in Europe has so many places named after Christian saints; it is the accumulated weight of twelve centuries of church politics, parish boundaries, and local piety cast into administrative concrete.

Saint-Martin leads with 202 variations scattered across France. It has been the country's most popular saint since the 5th century, when the bishop of Tours turned his cloak-sharing story into an institutional franchise. Saint-Jean (155), Saint-Pierre (144), and Saint-Germain (108) follow. Add them up and the top-10 saints alone account for 928 communes — more places than most French départements have in total.

On the river, by the sea

French commune names carry a compressed geography. 1,991 names include -sur- — built on, or at least adjacent to, something worth mentioning. The Loire and Seine each claim 70 communes in their name. The Marne, 55. The Meuse, 43.

But the sea wins. 99 communes are called something-sur-Mer — places where the ocean was important enough to advertise in the name. The preposition changes — -sur-, -les-, -en-, -lès- — but the logic is always the same: tell a stranger where you are relative to something they might know. Another 545 communes use -lès- (meaning "near"), the medieval preposition that survives in French place names long after it vanished from the spoken language.

Seven hyphens, one commune

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. It is 45 characters long, has seven hyphens, and sits in the Marne with a population of 491. It was created in 1972 by the merger of three separate communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — whose collective pride was sufficient to insist that all three names appear in the result.

This is the French administrative logic in full flower: every merger preserves its components, every component gets a hyphen, and nobody goes home unhappy except the cartographers. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 chars, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 chars, Calvados) round out the podium.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

Sainte-Colombe exists 12 times, scattered across 12 different départements from the Hautes-Alpes to Val-d'Oise. Each one has its own INSEE code, its own mayor, its own road sign. Each one is, administratively, a completely different place. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu, 10 times each.

Most duplicated commune names

In all, 4,272 commune names are shared by at least two different communes. France has 35,014 communes and roughly 30,742 distinct names — meaning around one name in eight belongs to more than one place. You can mail a letter to Sainte-Colombe and be confident only that it will reach one of twelve possible villages, any of which will want to tell you it's the real one.

Every commune name is a layer of sediment: Old Occitan, Old Frankish, Latin ecclesiastics, revolutionary renaming, mergers, splits, and the occasional bureaucratic whim. The names aren't labels. They're the shortest possible record of how France became France — 35,014 times, one hyphen at a time.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL dataset joined to IGN administrative contours — the same source used in Story 2 (medical deserts). The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories, in their official INSEE COG form. Population figures are 2021 INSEE census estimates. Name-length statistics, saint counts, preposition tallies, and duplicate counts were computed from this dataset; no external source was used for commune names.
