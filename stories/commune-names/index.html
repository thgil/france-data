What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Twelve of them are called Sainte-Colombe. And the longest name — 45 characters — contains two saints, four hyphens, and a et.

2026-05-22 5 min read

Every French commune has a name registered with the Institut national de la statistique et des études économiques (INSEE). Those names range from a single letter to a 45-character sprawl that reads like a legal merger. Between those extremes: 35,014 communes, 32,764 distinct names, and a surprisingly revealing portrait of how France thinks about places.

Y is not an abbreviation. It is the name. The commune's address is straightforward: Y, 80190. There are 13 two-letter communes — a small club of geographic minimalists:

Oô (Haute-Garonne) is a favourite — the two-letter name that makes spellcheckers panic. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées) has 36 residents. Sy (Ardennes) has 54. They exist mostly as curiosities: old village names that survived standardisation, too stubborn or too small to ever acquire a suffix.

The shape of French commune names

Most commune names are 7 or 8 characters long. That's the sweet spot: long enough to carry a place, short enough to fit on a sign. The distribution is roughly bell-shaped, then develops a long right tail — because France likes hyphenated saints.

The saint economy

3,882 French communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. That's not counting the many communes where a saint appears mid-name, or where the local saint is written as San (in Corsica or the Basque Country). The phenomenon has a simple explanation: most French villages grew up around a church, and the church was dedicated to a local saint. The village inherited the name. Nobody changed it during the Revolution, despite trying.

After the saints, the pattern continues with geography and land use. La Chapelle (chapel) appears in 151 commune names. Villers — an old Germanic word for hamlet — 105. Villeneuve (new village) 70. Pont (bridge) 62. France's commune names are a stratigraphic record: Latin saints, Gallo-Roman suffixes, Frankish roots, Revolutionary renamings, and the occasional oddity that survived everything.

The Sainte-Colombe problem

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, with 12 communes in 12 different départements. They share nothing except a 3rd-century martyr, a popular saint across early Gaul. There's no coordination. No legal dispute over which Sainte-Colombe is the real one. They simply all exist, separately, in the same country, under the same name.

The runner-up is Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), followed by Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each). Saint-Marcel, Saint-Michel, Saint-Pierre, Sainte-Marie — all at 9. The name collision is so routine that INSEE identifies every commune by a 5-digit code (code commune INSEE), not by name. In official data, France doesn't have 12 Sainte-Colombes. It has one 05135, one 17319, one 25515, and so on.

The long end

At the far end of the length distribution sits a commune in the Marne (département 51) with a 45-character name and a population of 491:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (51) · pop. 491

The name is the result of a 20th-century merger between three separate communes — Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — which declined to lose any part of their identity. Rather than choose a new name, they simply concatenated all three. The hyphen is load-bearing.

The second-longest is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône), also a merger. The pattern is consistent: the longest names in France are almost always the result of communes that merged and refused to forget. Every hyphen is a small act of municipal stubbornness.

How unique is a name?

31,314 communes have a name shared by no other commune in France — 89.4% of the total. The remaining 3,700 share their name with at least one other. Among the duplicated names, most appear twice or three times; the Sainte-Colombe count of 12 is genuinely unusual.

Names in France thus walk a line between uniqueness and common heritage. Most villages are their own — Vauréal is Vauréal, Montauban is Montauban. But the sainted ones exist in multiples, tied to the same calendar entry, sometimes hundreds of kilometres apart, with no knowledge of each other's existence and no wish to be confused.

89% of communes have a name that belongs only to them. The other 11% share a saint.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures come from DREES's APL 2023 commune-level dataset, joined to administrative geometry from Etalab's 2025 contours file — the same data underlying the medical deserts story. Name lengths are computed from the communeName field as stored in INSEE's directory; names with accent characters (Oô, Bû) count the accented character as one. The dataset covers all 35,014 communes including overseas territories.
