What's in a name?

35,014 communes, 32,764 unique names — and one that's just one letter.

2026-06-01 5 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union. 35,014 of them, ranging from Paris (2.1 million people) down to villages of a handful of residents. They each have an official name, registered with the INSEE directory, which is searchable, sortable — and quietly delightful.

Of those 35,014 names, only 32,764 are unique. That leaves 1,450 names shared across multiple communes — a name collision problem that French postal workers have navigated for centuries. But before we get to the duplicates, there's the question of length.

One letter

The shortest commune name in France is Y. Not an abbreviation. Not a registration quirk. The commune is called Y, it is in the Somme department, it has 89 residents, and its postcode is 80134. Postal workers have presumably adapted.

The two-letter club has 13 members: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, and Uz. At the other end, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in Marne clocks in at 45 characters — a commune formed by merging three villages, which kept all three names and hyphenated the result. It sits in the champagne country east of Vitry-le-François and has 491 residents who presumably have very short addresses on file.

The shape of French commune names

The typical commune name runs 7–8 characters. The distribution peaks there before a long, flat tail extends rightward — driven almost entirely by saint names with long geographic suffixes. Short names under 4 characters are genuinely rare: just 168 communes out of 35,014.

The saint problem

3,898
communes whose name starts with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of all French communes

France's commune names are a stratigraphic record of Catholic settlement patterns. 3,898 communes — more than one in nine — have names beginning with Saint or Sainte. The saints canonised most often are the ones with the most local churches, the most medieval dedications, the most hamlets that grew up around a chapel and took its name.

The most duplicated commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 separate communes across 11 departments — from Charente-Maritime in the west to Yonne in the east, spread across a belt of old agricultural France. Twelve separate villages, each with its own postcode, mayor, and tax base, all named after the same third-century martyr from Sens.

The collision problem

1,450 names are shared by two or more communes — accounting for 3,700 communes in total. That's more than 10% of France's commune roster existing in duplicate, triplicate, or greater. The collision problem isn't academic: mail addressed to "Sainte-Colombe, France" could plausibly arrive at a dozen different destinations.

The French solution, dating back to the revolutionary reorganisation of 1790, was the département-commune system: every commune gets a five-digit INSEE code (the first two digits are the département) which uniquely identifies it regardless of name. Postal codes are a separate layer on top. The names, with all their collisions, remain — because names carry history that codes don't.

France has more communes than Germany, Italy, Spain, and Poland combined. It has kept them — through mergers proposed and refused, through rural depopulation, through two centuries of administrative reform — because the commune remains the basic unit of democratic self-government in French political culture. 35,014 names, 35,014 mayors, 35,014 municipal councils. Some of them with 89 residents. One of them called Y.
