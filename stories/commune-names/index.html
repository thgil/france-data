Y is a place

France has 35,014 communes and 32,764 distinct names — and one of them is just the letter Y.

2026-05-26 4 min read

In the Somme — département 80, half an hour south-east of Amiens — 89 people live in a commune whose official name on every French administrative document is a single capital letter: Y. It has a church, a mairie, a bus stop, and the shortest postal address in France.

Y is not a typo. It is not an abbreviation. It predates the French Republic — the name comes from a Gaulish root meaning "yew tree," the same etymology as the English town of Eu in Essex. France just never bothered to rename it.

The two-letter club

Y has company. Across France there are 13 communes with two-letter names, plus Y itself. Between them they cover six letters of the alphabet, both Norman river towns and Pyrenean villages. The largest — Eu, in Seine-Maritime — has 6,591 residents and a royal château where Queen Victoria once visited.

A bell curve with a strange left tail

Plot all 35,014 commune names by character count and you get something close to a normal distribution — but with a tail that shouldn't exist. French names cluster around 7–9 characters. Y sits alone at 1. At the other extreme, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in Marne, runs to 45 characters — a hyphenated merger of three villages that history refused to simplify.

One commune in nine is a saint

France was evangelised methodically. The evidence survives in the gazetteer: 3,882 commune names — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That's one in nine. Every département has them. Every region has dozens.

The most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — twelve separate communes in twelve separate départements, from Hautes-Alpes to Yonne, all named after the same martyred Roman-era virgin. They are not twins. They don't share a history beyond the hagiographic one. They just all happened to have a church dedicated to the same saint, and the name stuck.

The most repeated names

Below Sainte-Colombe, the repetition pattern is overwhelmingly saintly. Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin (10), Saint-Marcel (9), Saint-Michel (9), Saint-Pierre (9). The only non-saint in the top five by count is Beaulieu — ten places that someone, at some point, simply thought looked nice.

What the names are

Read the full list of 32,764 unique names and France's administrative history becomes legible. Villeneuve (new town) appears as a first word 70 times — every one a medieval foundation signalling growth. Pont (bridge) appears 63 times. Fontaine (spring) 58 times. Mont 52 times. The landscape wrote the names and the names survived the communes, the cantons, the révolutions, and the fusions of the last century.

Y survived too. Its 89 residents receive post addressed to the shortest commune in France, fill in administrative forms with a single capital letter, and answer — if asked — that yes, it is a real place. The yew trees are long gone. The name remains.
