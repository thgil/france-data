What's in a name?

35,014 communes. One is called Y. A dozen share the same name. And one runs to 45 letters.

2026-05-09 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes, and their names span a range that feels almost deliberate. At one end sits a commune in the Somme département called, simply, Y. At the other end, somewhere in the Marne, is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, five place-names fused into one in the 1970s when France was merging its smallest villages under the loi Marcellin. Between them lies the full vocabulary of French geography: saints, forests, rivers, hilltops, valleys, and a remarkable amount of ambiguity about who exactly "Saint-Martin" refers to.

The shape of French place names

Most commune names are 7 to 12 characters long — the median is 10. The histogram below shows the full distribution. The peak is real: there's a characteristic rhythm to a French commune name, something like Saint-Martin (12) or Beaulieu (8). The long tail to the right consists mostly of merged communes — places that combined their names with hyphens rather than choosing a new one.

The saint epidemic

32,764 unique names

One in nine French communes starts with Saint or Sainte. That's 3,891 of them — more than all the communes in Bretagne. They are so numerous that even the most common name in France, Sainte-Colombe, appears only 12 times — once in each of 12 different départements. There is a Sainte-Colombe in the Gironde and another in the Yonne, one in the Finistère and one in the Oise. They don't disambiguate by region; they share the name as a kind of medieval franchise, each honouring the same third-century martyr from Sens.

The runner-up, Saint-Sauveur, appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu follow with 10 each. Below is the full top-20 — the names so common they had to be told apart by département, river, or hilltop suffix.

The other end of the alphabet

The 14 shortest commune names — one with a single letter, 13 with two — are not random. They cluster in three zones: the Pyrénées (Ur, Uz, Py, Oz), Normandie (Eu, Ry, By), and scattered across the north-east (Gy, Sy, Ri). These are pre-Latin survivals. Basque, Celtic, and Germanic toponyms that the Roman and Frankish layers of renaming never fully replaced. Eu (pop 6,591) was a major town under William the Conqueror. Y in the Somme appears in records as early as the 12th century — its name probably derives from the Latin aqua (water), referring to a river bend, compressed over centuries into a single letter.

History hyphenated

The longest names are a different kind of document. They're almost all the product of administrative mergers — the loi Marcellin of 1971 offered subsidies to communes that merged, and many did, by simply concatenating their names with hyphens. The champion, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, wraps three village names into one 45-character string. It is, technically, one commune. It is, in practice, three villages that agreed to pool their town hall but not their identities.

35,014 answers to the same question

Every commune name is an answer to the same question: how do we tell this place apart from everywhere else? The saints gave a ready-made vocabulary, which is why they dominate — but France ran out of saints long before it ran out of communes, which is why there are 12 Sainte-Colombes and 11 Saint-Sauveurs. The Pyrénéan one-syllable names are a refusal to translate, a pre-modern holdout. The hyphenated 45-character beasts are a compromise: we merged, but we didn't forget. Each name is a small argument about identity, mostly settled, occasionally still contested.
