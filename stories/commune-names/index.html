What's in a French name?

France has 35,014 communes. The shortest name is one letter. The longest takes 45. And one in nine is a saint.

2026-05-30 5 min read

Every French commune has a name. That name is its identity, its postal address, its place on the map, and — more often than you'd expect — a direct line back to the medieval church. Of France's 35,014 communes, those names range from a single letter to a 45-character hyphen-linked epic. In between: 3,882 saints, 1,450 duplicate names, and a two-letter club with exactly 13 members.

Y

The shortest commune name in France — and almost certainly the shortest in any major country — is Y. One letter. Département Somme, code 80829. Population 89. It sits in the flat agricultural country north of Paris, unremarkable to pass through, extraordinary on paper.

The name most likely derives from a Picard dialect word for water — the same root as the Latin aqua, clipped down through centuries of local speech until only the vowel survived. Y has been a commune since the French Revolution fixed the map. It has had this name much longer.

The two-letter club

France has 13 communes with two-letter names. Most are tiny. Two — Eu (Seine-Maritime) and Bû (Eure-et-Loir) — have real towns behind them, with over a thousand people each. The rest are villages of under a few hundred.

Their origins are similarly compressed: old Frankish personal names, Latin roots worn smooth, Norman dialect words for water or stream. The short name is never random. It's always a survivor.

The saint explosion

One in nine French communes is named after a saint. Exactly 3,882 of 35,014 — 11.1% — carry a Saint- or Sainte- prefix. This isn't coincidence. Medieval parishes dedicated their churches to a patron saint; that dedication became the village's identity; the Revolution kept the names even as it abolished the Church's power. The result is that France's administrative map is also, involuntarily, a hagiography.

The most common saint in France is Saint-Martin, patron of soldiers and beggars, with 202 communes bearing his name — more than any other. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow. Together, the top five saints account for more than 700 communes.

The tail goes deep. France has at least one commune dedicated to each of: Saint-Hippolyte, Saint-Saturnin, Saint-Sulpice, Saint-Christophe, Sainte-Gemme. Every obscure martyr and minor bishop has at least one village keeping the memory alive, embedded in the postal code.

The same name, everywhere

France's 35,014 communes share only 32,764 distinct names. That means 1,450 names appear more than once — sometimes many times more. Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times, each in a different département. These are not the same village seen through different eyes: they are 12 distinct places, with separate histories, administrations, and residents, united only by the same medieval dedication to the same saint.

Most repeated commune names

The duplicates are almost entirely saint dedications and topographic generics: Beaulieu (beautiful place), Le Pin (the pine), Beaumont (beautiful hill). France's landscape produced the same name in many places, independently. No coordination was needed — the same terrain, the same Church, the same Old French vocabulary.

The shape of a name

Most French communes have names between 5 and 10 characters long — a comfortable Latin or Old French word, maybe with an article. The distribution peaks sharply around 7–8 characters, then trails off into the long compound names at the right.

45 characters

At the other end of the spectrum is the longest commune name in France, which requires 45 characters to write in full:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (Dept 51) · 45 characters · population 525

This kind of name is almost never original — it's a merger's souvenir. Three villages once existed separately: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. At some point in the twentieth century, an administrative reform merged them into one commune. Rather than pick a winner, the new entity kept every name, hyphenated. The result reads less like a place name and more like a legal document.

A thousand years of sediment

France's communes were fixed largely by the Revolution and have barely changed since — around 34,000 in 1789, about 35,000 today. The names are older still: Gaulish topography, Latin settlements, Frankish personal names, medieval saint dedications, Norman dialect. Y got its name before French was recognizable as a language. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson got its final hyphen in the late twentieth century. Between them: a republic of villages, each named once, many named the same.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Names reflect the official INSEE commune registry as of the 2023 vintage. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base. Name-length statistics and saint-name counts were computed from the full commune list.
