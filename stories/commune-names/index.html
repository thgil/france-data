What France is called

35,014 communes, 32,764 unique names — from a single-letter village in the Somme to a 45-character marvel in the Marne.

2026-05-15 5 min read

France has a commune called Y. That's the whole name. One letter. The commune sits in the Somme, département 80, with 89 registered residents and a valid INSEE code (80829). It has a mayor. It holds municipal elections. It appears in the legal gazette. It is, officially and without irony, called Y.

Y

The shortest commune name in France. Population 89. Département de la Somme. INSEE code 80829. Y was already recorded as a place name in medieval documents — its origin is Gaulish, probably from a root meaning water or watercourse. The letter is not an abbreviation. It is the name.

This is not an anomaly. France's commune naming system is the accumulated sediment of two millennia of Gaulish, Latin, Frankish, Occitan, Breton, Basque, Alsatian, and Revolutionary-era bureaucratic invention. The results are extraordinary: 35,014 communes, only 32,764 unique names among them, with lengths ranging from one letter to 45.

The shape of a French place name

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. Most names land between 8 and 16 characters — a comfortable Saint-Quelque-Chose-sur-Fleuve length. But the distribution has a long right tail, pulled by the small number of communes that have merged, hyphenated, and accumulated names over centuries.

Number of communes by name length (characters)

The modal length is around 10–11 characters. Names under 5 characters are genuinely rare — only 14 communes have two-letter names, and only one has a single letter. Names over 30 characters represent deliberate administrative mergers: two or more formerly independent communes fused into one hyphenated entity, each half insisting on keeping its old identity.

Longest names

The longest name — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters, 491 residents in the Marne — is the product of a 1972 municipal merger. The commune absorbed two neighbours and stitched their names together. This is common practice: rather than choose one name and erase the others, French communes often carry all of them, separated by hyphens, -et-, and patience.

The most duplicated name in France

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,700 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. The most common name — Sainte-Colombe — appears 12 times, spread across 12 different départements from Calvados to the Var. There are 12 different mayors of Sainte-Colombe, 12 different budgets, 12 different municipal councils, none of them related.

The Saint machine

11.1% of France's communes — 3,898 of them — start with Saint or Sainte. This is not a coincidence of language: it is a map of Christianisation. Every valley, hilltop, and crossroads that was named or renamed during the medieval centuries received a patron saint. Those names have survived the Revolution (which briefly renamed hundreds of Saint- communes to Republican alternatives like Libre or Roche-de-la-Liberté), the Restoration, and every administrative reform since.

Add in the 2,220 communes whose name begins with a definite article — La, Le, Les, L' — and more than 17% of France's communes start with either a saint's name or a gendered article. The grammar of French place names reflects medieval Latin syntax, where every settlement was defined by its relationship to the divine, the geographical, or the feudal lord.

11.1% of French communes begin with Saint- or Sainte- — 3,898 out of 35,014.

The most common Saint-prefixed names tell you which saints the medieval Church promoted hardest: Sainte-Colombe (12 communes), Saint-Sauveur (11), Saint-Aubin (10), Saint-Marcel (9), Saint-Michel (9), Saint-Pierre (9). These are the saints of regional patronage, local miracles, and cathedral dedications — not the Roman martyrs of the capital, but the local intercessors of the countryside.

France's communes are often described as an administrative anachronism — 35,000 units where Germany has 11,000, where mergers are perpetually proposed and perpetually resisted. The names are part of that resistance. To rename a commune is to erase a Gaulish watercourse, a medieval saint, a revolutionary aspiration, a 1972 merger that two village councils agreed to with gritted teeth. The name is the history. The history, it turns out, is hard to compress.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes in the original source) joined to administrative boundaries from Etalab Contours 2025. The merged GeoJSON contains 35,014 features with the communeName field populated. Name analysis is performed on this corpus; a small number of very recent municipal mergers may not be reflected.
