35,000 names for 35,000 places

France has more communes than any other country in the EU. They can't all have different names.

2026-05-31 5 min read

France has 34,969 communes — one for roughly every five minutes of the year, or one for every 1,900 residents. It's an extraordinary administrative inheritance from the Revolution: in 1790, the new Republic converted almost every parish into a commune and left most of them standing.

The names didn't all get updated either. Of the 34,969 communes, only 32,719 have genuinely unique names. The remaining 2,250 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in France. Two places with the same name, in different departments, occasionally in adjacent ones — a geography that has confused couriers, tax collectors, and sat-navs for two centuries.

The saint problem

France is deeply Catholic, and the commune names reflect it. 3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — have a name beginning with Saint- or Sainte-. More than one in ten French communes is named after a saint.

But the saints themselves are not evenly distributed. Saint-Martin generates the most variants: over 200 communes somewhere have Saint-Martin in their name. Saint-Jean comes second with 155, Saint-Pierre third with 144. The list below shows the ten most popular patron saints, by count of communes named for them.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most duplicated name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in exactly 12 departments: Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. Each one is the real Sainte-Colombe. None of them are near each other.

The one-letter republic

At one extreme: Y, in the Somme. One letter. Population 89. It is probably the world's shortest official municipal name. The commune's APL score is 2.78 — above the medical-desert threshold. The post office delivers there without complaint.

Y is not alone in its brevity. Thirteen communes have two-letter names. They are concentrated in the north and in the mountain borderlands — old Frankish and Basque place names worn down to near-nothing by a thousand years of usage.

How long is a French commune name?

The median name length is 10 characters. Most names cluster between 7 and 15 characters — enough room for a saint, a geographic qualifier, and a hyphen. The distribution has a long right tail: a small number of communes have names over 30 characters, almost all of them products of recent municipal mergers.

The merger monsters

The longest commune name in France — at 45 characters — is:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (dept. 51) · population 491 · created by merger

It was formed in 2016 when three communes merged: Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. Rather than pick a winner, they hyphenated all three. The resulting commune name takes longer to type than most French sentences.

Seven of the eight longest commune names contain at least two saint names. All of them are merger products. France has been consolidating its communes since the 1971 Loi Marcellin, but the mergers have been slow and voluntary — France still has about twice as many communes as Germany, with a third of the population. The long names are a record of that reluctance: you can merge the administration, but you can't make anyone choose.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the communes-apl.geojson used in the medical deserts story, which joins DREES APL 2023 scores to the 2025 Etalab administrative boundaries. The dataset covers 35,014 entries including Paris, Lyon, and Marseille arrondissements; those 45 entries are excluded from name-frequency and length analysis, leaving 34,969 true communes. Name-deduplication is exact-match only — Saint-Martin-de-Bréhal and Saint-Martin-des-Champs are counted as different names.
