What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. Their names run from a single letter to 45 characters — and one saint alone appears in 250 of them.

2026-06-02 5 min read

France has a commune called Y. Not Yonne. Not Yvelines. Just Y. It sits in the Somme — département 80, commune code 80829 — with 89 inhabitants and a name that has caused problems for postal workers since the Merovingians. Your autocomplete has never suffered as much as the Y post office does every day.

Y is the shortest commune name in France. But it is not alone in its brevity. There are 13 communes with two-letter names — each a small act of naming restraint in a country that also produced Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson.

These aren't abbreviations or mistakes. They're historical names, often derived from old Gaulish or Latin roots worn smooth by a thousand years of usage. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) traces back to the Latin Augia; Oz is a hamlet-turned-ski-resort in the Isère. Oô — two vowels and no consonants — sits in the Haute-Garonne at the foot of the Pyrenees.

The shape of French names

Most commune names are 6–10 characters long. The distribution peaks around 7–8 letters, then has a second, flatter hump in the 15–22 range — the merged communes, hyphenated saints, and river-qualified doubles that France accumulates over centuries of administrative reform. The median is 10 characters; the average is 11.8.

At the far right: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters, Marne), a 1972 merger of three villages that were apparently content to keep everyone's name in the title. France has three communes above 40 characters, all merger products of the loi Marcellin era, when the government tried — and largely failed — to consolidate rural France's 37,000 communes into something more manageable.

The most popular names

If you need to find a town called Sainte-Colombe, you'll need to specify which one — there are 12 of them, spread across 12 different départements from Gironde to Yonne. France gave the same saintly name to a dozen places and never made them sort it out.

Longest names

Saint-Sauveur runs a close second with 11 communes. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu tie at 10 each. The pattern is consistent: the most common names are either saints (interchangeable, abundant, blessed by the Church) or generic geographic descriptors (Beaulieu = beautiful place; Beaumont = beautiful hill; Le Pin = the pine tree) that hundreds of medieval villages independently decided described their home perfectly.

The Saint empire

4,437
communes contain "Saint" in their name — 12.7% of all French communes

France is saintly in its place-naming. 4,437 communes contain the word Saint — either as a standalone prefix, embedded in a hyphenated compound, or fused into older forms. The most common saint-derived first element is Saint-Martin, which appears in 250 commune names, from Autevielle-Saint-Martin-Bideren in the Pyrénées-Atlantiques to Bussy-Saint-Martin in Seine-et-Marne.

Martin of Tours, the soldier-turned-bishop who famously cut his cloak in two to share with a beggar, became the patron saint of France and thus the patron saint of an enormous number of villages. Saint-Jean follows with 155 appearances; Saint-Pierre with 144. Together the top-ten saint prefixes account for over 1,000 communes — roughly one in thirty.

La, Le, Les: the definite republic

2,140 communes begin with a definite article: La (1,054), Le (758), Les (328). Every one of these is, grammatically, confident. La Rochelle. Le Havre. Les Andelys. France doesn't deal in indefinite articles when naming places — these are THE rock, THE harbour, THE rapids. No hedging.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes (metropolitan and overseas territories) with their INSEE commune codes, department codes, and 2021 population estimates. Names are the official INSEE communeName field and reflect post-2023 mergers where communes déléguées are counted as one unit.
