What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. And 38% have a hyphen.

2026-06-02 4 min read

France is the country of administrative precision. Every commune has a 5-digit INSEE code, a canton, an arrondissement, a département, a région. The system is meticulous. And then, somewhere in the Somme, there is a commune whose official name is a single letter.

Y Somme · Département 80 · Population 89

Y is not an acronym. It is not an abbreviation. It is, simply, the name of the place — and has been since at least the 9th century, derived from an old Picard word for water. It is the shortest commune name in France, possibly in the world. Its postcode is 80190. Its residents are called Ypsiloniens.

A spectrum from Y to Bouzemont

Commune names range from one character to 45. The typical name is 11 characters long. Most fall between 8 and 15 — long enough to carry a saint, a geographic descriptor, and a hyphen or two, but short enough to fit on a road sign without heroic typography.

The longest name in France belongs to a commune in the Marne:

45 characters · 7 hyphens · Marne (51) Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

This is a commune nouvelle — an administrative merger of several formerly independent villages. France has been quietly consolidating communes for decades, and when two or three towns merge, the convention is to concatenate the names with hyphens. The result is a name that functions less as a place name and more as a legal document. In second place: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône).

The two-letter club

Below Y, there are 13 communes with two-letter names. They are scattered across France, from the Ardennes to the Pyrénées, from Normandy to the Val-d'Oise. They have nothing in common except brevity.

Most are vestiges of ancient, pre-Latin place names — Celtic, Gaulish, even Basque roots compressed over millennia into pure phoneme. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the largest of the group and was once a royal residence of the Dukes of Normandy. Oz (Isère, pop. 213) is a ski village in the Belledonne mountains. Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales, pop. 348) is in Catalan country, near the Spanish border.

The saint explosion

One in nine French communes starts with Saint or Sainte. The Catholicism baked into French place names is not subtle. Saint alone accounts for 3,601 communes; Sainte adds another 297. If you include names that contain a saint further into the compound (like Montoire-sur-le-Loir-de-Saint-Gilles), the count rises further. French geography is a hagiography.

3,700 communes that aren't alone

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 unique names. That means 3,700 communes share their exact name with at least one other commune, somewhere else in France. You cannot tell them apart by name — only by their INSEE code, their département, or the quiet context of knowing which one you mean.

Most duplicated names

The short-name table

Sainte-Colombe is the most common commune name in France — twelve communes share it, spread across twelve différent départements from the Hautes-Alpes to the Yonne. They range in size from 59 residents (dept. 05) to 1,953 (dept. 69, just south of Lyon). None of them are particularly famous. All of them share a patron saint and a name, and nothing else.

The 38% who hyphenate

France is the world's most enthusiastic hyphenator of place names. Of the 35,014 communes, 13,410 — more than one in three — contain at least one hyphen. The hyphen is the connective tissue of French administrative geography: it joins saint to town, town to river, river to mountain. Some names use it once; the communes nouvelles use it six or seven times.

For context: the United Kingdom has almost no hyphenated town names. Germany has fewer than 200. France has 13,410. It is a stylistic commitment so total that it barely registers as unusual — until you notice it.

The naming of French communes is a sedimentation of history: Roman saints layered over Gaulish phonemes, Norman syllables fused to Occitan roots, administrative mergers concatenated with modern hyphens. The system has 35,014 entries and no coherent logic. It is, in other words, exactly what you'd expect from a country that's been naming places for two thousand years.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes are drawn from the APL dataset used in the medical deserts story, which carries the full official commune name as registered by INSEE. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Centroids are computed as the simple arithmetic mean of polygon vertex coordinates — adequate for story purposes, not for navigation.
