What's in a Name?

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Here's what the rest reveal about how a country names itself.

2026-06-04 5 min read

Y is a commune in the Somme. Population 89. Located at 49.79°N, 3.01°E, about 130 km north of Paris. Its name is not an abbreviation. It is not a placeholder. The commune is called Y, and it has been called that for centuries — the name likely derives from a Picard word for water. The INSEE code is 80829. That's it. One letter.

Y holds the undisputed title of France's shortest commune name. But it is not alone in its brevity. Thirteen other communes make do with just two letters.

Each of these is a real administrative unit, with a mayor, a budget, and a spot in the national census. Eu (Seine-Maritime) is the site of a medieval castle where Henry II of England and Louis VII of France once negotiated. Oz (Isère) is a ski resort. Gy (Haute-Saône) has a market going back to the 13th century. Oô — that accent on the final letter — is a Pyrenean hamlet so small (103 residents) it barely registers as a data point.

One in nine communes is a saint

France has 35,014 communes and 3,891 of them — 11.1% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. The Catholic Church left a deep imprint on French administrative geography: every parish saint became a village name, and most of those village names survived the Revolutionary period, the Napoleonic reform, and a hundred subsequent waves of municipal mergers.

The word Saint appears 4,090 times across commune names — making it the single most common word in the French administrative toponymy, ahead of sur (2,007 times), Martin (255), and Pierre (180). France is, in its place names, extremely religious.

Twelve places, one name

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have distinct names. The remaining 2,250 share a name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. The most duplicated: Sainte-Colombe, which appears in 12 different départements — from Gironde in the south-west to Seine-et-Marne east of Paris, Yonne in Burgundy, and Manche in Normandy. Twelve places named for the same fourth-century Gaulish martyr.

Below that: Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each), and a long tail of Saint-Michels, Saint-Marcels, and Beaumonts. The naming pattern is not random — it reflects the patchwork of pre-Revolutionary dioceses, each of which had its own favoured saints, and the tendency of neighbouring villages to adopt identical dedications from the same local bishop.

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long

Across 35,014 communes, the mean name length is 11.8 characters — about the length of Saint-Aubin. The distribution peaks around 7–8 characters, with a long right tail driven by the merger names that dominate the upper end. 38.3% of all commune names include at least one hyphen, a characteristic French feature absent from most European country's toponymies.

The merger names

France's longest commune name reaches 45 characters — a six-word construction from Marne that is, fittingly, also the most hyphenated: seven hyphens, two saints, one river, and the name of a hamlet that had been separately governed before being absorbed into the commune.

These baroque compound names are the administrative fossils of France's fusion de communes programme, which began in earnest under the 1971 Marcellin Law. Smaller communes were offered financial incentives to merge, but many insisted on preserving their historic names rather than losing them entirely. The result is a cohort of municipalities whose names read like legal contracts — every former commune's identity appended with a hyphen and an et.

Names are not arbitrary. Every Saint-Martin-de-Quelquechose tells you something about who held the diocese, which way the river ran, and where medieval roads forked. Every two-letter name — Y, Eu, Oz — is a survival: something short enough that no bureaucrat ever found a convincing reason to change it. And somewhere in Somme, in a commune of 89 people, the name is simply Y. It goes in the census, on the road signs, and in the database. One letter. Enough.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes are drawn from the APL 2023 dataset published by DREES, covering 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name analysis is based on the communeName field as it appears in that dataset; spelling reflects official INSEE nomenclature as of 2023. Two communes appear under a single-character name in this dataset: Y (Somme). The 13 two-letter communes listed are all those with names of exactly two characters in the same source.
