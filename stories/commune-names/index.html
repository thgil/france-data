35,014 names

One of them is "Y". One is 45 characters long. Twelve are "Sainte-Colombe". France's commune names are a peculiar national archive.

2026-05-19 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — towns, villages, and hamlets that each carry an official name registered with the state. Those names span a single letter to forty-five. They include the generic (Beaumont appears eight times), the specific (L'Abergement-Clémenciat), and the genuinely unclassifiable (Y).

These aren't just addresses. They're administrative artifacts — some medieval in origin, some reflecting centuries of hyphenated mergers, some simply commemorating a saint who may or may not have visited. The full list of 35,014 names is a compressed history of how France named itself.

One in nine communes is a saint

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. It is the single most productive naming convention in the country. Among these, Saint-Martin leads with 203 occurrences, followed by Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144). France has more communes named Saint-Martin than it has communes in several entire départements.

The saints aren't evenly distributed. Saint-Martin concentrates in Saône-et-Loire (12), Seine-Maritime (10), Calvados (7), and Manche (7) — the old Norman and Burgundian heartlands of the cult of St Martin of Tours. Saint-Jean and Saint-Pierre spread more evenly across the hexagon. The aggregate effect is visible in the first-letter breakdown: S towers over every other letter.

The most common name: Sainte-Colombe

1,450 names appear more than once — shared by 3,700 communes in total. That means 10.6% of communes share their exact name with at least one other commune. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, held by twelve communes across twelve different départements: in Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. They have nothing in common except a shared devotion to the obscure 3rd-century martyr Saint Columba.

Y is a commune of 89 people in the Somme, département 80. Its name is the entire French alphabet's rarest initial letter — only one commune starts with Y, and that commune is Y. It sits on a plateau near Montdidier and has a church dating to the 12th century. It is adequately served by doctors (APL 2.78, just above the medical-desert threshold of 2.5).

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, at 45 characters, is three villages stitched together in the Marne. The hyphen count alone — eight — gives it away as a fusion. These long compound names are a paper record of the fusions de communes that the state has encouraged over the centuries and continues to encourage today: merge your village into a neighbouring one, keep both names, run them together with hyphens until the post office gives up.

Between the two extremes, 14 other communes manage with just two letters. The largest two-letter commune is Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591 — and yes, the name predates the European Union).

The shape of a commune name

The median commune name is 10 characters long. The distribution peaks sharply at 9–11 characters, then has a long right tail of hyphenated compounds. Names shorter than 4 characters are genuinely rare — there are only 15 communes with names of 3 characters or fewer, and one of them is a single vowel.

The 25 most common names

Below the saint-heavy top tier, the common names reflect the old French rural vocabulary: Beaulieu (beautiful place), Le Pin (the pine), Beaumont (beautiful hill), Verrières (glasshouses). These are landscape descriptions that hundreds of independent villages, with no knowledge of each other, hit upon at the same moment in medieval history. The result is that France has eight communes called Beaumont, nine called Le Pin, and ten called Beaulieu.

The 31,314 unique names — the ones that belong to exactly one commune — are the real plurality: the specific names that record a landform, a founder, a forgotten miracle, a tree that no longer exists. Each one is a piece of French administrative DNA that has survived every reform, every merger, every Republic. Y will almost certainly outlast us all.

A note on the data. Commune names and counts are taken from the INSEE administrative boundaries file for 2025 (contours-administratifs, 1000m simplification), which underpins the medical-deserts story on this site. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Centroids were computed from polygon geometry. Name length counts include spaces, hyphens, and accented characters.
