What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. Over 3,800 begin with Saint.

May 2026 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, département 80, postal code 80114, with 89 residents and an official INSEE code of 80829. Its name, in full, as it appears on every administrative document, every road sign, and every electoral roll in the Republic, is: Y.

Not a placeholder. Not an abbreviation. The commune's name is the twenty-fifth letter of the alphabet, alone, a single glyph, and it has been that way for longer than the French state has existed. The name comes from Old French, likely derived from the Latin aqua (water), reflecting a now-vanished pond. But the etymology doesn't make the street address any easier to write. Imagine telling someone where you live.

Y is an outlier — the only single-letter commune in France — but it points at something real about the French naming system: a tangle of medieval saints, pre-Romance place names, duplicates, mergers, and names so long they barely fit on a sign. France's 35,014 communes are, collectively, a complete survey of how a country accumulated names over a thousand years without much coordination.

The short end

Y's nearest companions in brevity are thirteen communes with two-letter names. They cluster in the east and the Pyrenees, in regions where pre-Romance naming survived — Occitan in the south, old Germanic in the east, older Celtic or Gaulish strata in between. Sy (Ardennes, 54 residents). Py (Pyrénées-Orientales, 79 residents). Ur (also Pyrénées-Orientales, 348 residents). By (Doubs, 86 residents). Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, 36 residents — the least-populated 2-letter commune). Then the Norman ones: Eu (Seine-Maritime, 6,591 residents, the largest), Ry (Seine-Maritime, 775 residents). And Bû, with a circumflex that presumably made the post office feel better, in Eure-et-Loir.

None of these were mistakes. They are all that remains of names that once had more syllables, worn down by centuries of phonetic attrition. A hamlet became a village, the village kept the consonantal core, the rest fell away.

The duplicate problem

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names. The other 2,250 names are shared — 3,700 communes answer to a name that at least one other commune also answers to. Before the five-digit postal code was introduced in 1972, receiving a letter addressed to Sainte-Colombe, France was a genuine crisis: there are twelve communes called Sainte-Colombe, scattered across twelve different départements from Hautes-Alpes (pop. 59) to Seine-et-Marne (pop. 1,793). The post would have had to guess.

The duplicates are overwhelmingly saint names — medieval parishes founded under the same patronage independently, in different regions, centuries apart. Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin ten times. Beaulieu (literally "beautiful place," not a saint, but equally universal) also ten times. The top-twenty most-common names are almost entirely saint names or generic descriptors: Beaumont, Verrières, Brion — pretty places, common hills, unremarkable rivers, named without anyone checking if the name was taken.

The saint problem

The duplicate issue is a symptom of the deeper pattern: France is, by name at least, a very saintly country. Of its 35,014 communes, 3,882 — 11.1% — begin with "Saint" or "Sainte." The leading saint for place-naming is Martin: 202 communes begin with Saint-Martin, more than one for every two French départements. Saint-Jean follows with 155. Saint-Pierre with 144.

This matters beyond toponymy. The French Revolution, which abolished the calendar of saints and renamed hundreds of streets and towns, barely dented the commune-name layer. Revolutionary administrators rechristened Saint-Malo as Port-Malo and Saint-Germain-en-Laye as Montagne-du-Bon-Air, but most of those renames lasted a few years at most and were quietly reversed. The commune layer is pre-Revolutionary and has mostly stayed that way. If you want to find the depth of the ancien régime in French geography, look at the map and count the saints.

The long end

At the other end of the distribution sits a 45-character marvel: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne, département 51, population 491. It's not an accident. It's a product of the loi Marcellin of 1971, which encouraged neighbouring communes to merge by creating a legal mechanism for voluntary fusion. The law offered financial incentives; the catch was that merged communes were usually named by concatenating the old names with a hyphen. Saint-Remy, Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson merged in the early 1970s and produced the longest commune name in France as a direct consequence of the policy.

The merger programme generated a cluster of long names: runner-up Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, in Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, in Calvados) both date from the same era. The loi Marcellin ultimately merged only about 1,000 communes — far fewer than the government hoped — before enthusiasm faded. But it left behind a permanent signature in the postal atlas: names that look like the result of a typographical accident.

The distribution

Between the single letter and the 45-character mega-commune, France's name lengths form a roughly normal distribution centred around 7–8 characters, with a long right tail from the merger-era hyphenated names. Most communes have names of 6–10 characters — long enough to be descriptive, short enough to be pronounceable. The outliers at both ends are historical accidents: extreme erosion on the left, extreme policy-driven agglomeration on the right.

Names as map

Commune names are not arbitrary. They encode topography (Mont-, Val-, Fontaine-), religious geography (the saint layer), ethnicity and language (the Breton -ker, the Alsatian -heim, the Basque -hau, the Occitan -an), conquest (dozens of -les-Bains and -la-Forêt added to distinguish merger neighbours), and occasionally just a body of water that has since disappeared. The name Y remembers a pond that existed in the Middle Ages and is now a drained field. The name Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson remembers the moment a 1970s government ministry offered villages money to cooperate.

That both of these outcomes — one letter and forty-five letters — exist simultaneously in the same legal registry, on the same administrative map, is not an oversight. It's what happens when you let names accumulate for a thousand years and then try very hard not to delete any of them.
