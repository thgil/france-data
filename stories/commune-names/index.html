Y, and 35,013 others

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. The most popular name is shared by 12. The longest runs to 45 characters.

2026-05-18 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes, each with an official name recorded in the Code Officiel Géographique. Most names are one of a kind: 32,764 — 93.6% — appear exactly once. The other 3,700 communes share their name with at least one neighbour somewhere in the country. Those duplicates pile up: the single most common name appears twelve times.

One letter

The shortest commune name in France is Y — a single letter. It sits in the Somme département (Hauts-de-France), near Amiens, with a population of 89. Pronounced "ee," its name derives from a medieval form of the Latin aqua (water), marking an old pond near the village. Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 communes — one each in Ain, Ardèche, Calvados, Côtes-d'Armor, Gironde, Haute-Garonne, Saône-et-Loire, Seine-Maritime, Vosges, Yonne, and two others. If you need to mail a letter to Sainte-Colombe, you will need the postcode. Saint-Sauveur follows at 11, then Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu at 10 each.

One name in nine starts with a saint

Step back from exact matches and a larger pattern appears. — communes — one in nine — have a name beginning with Saint or Sainte. The Christianisation of Gaul left a denser imprint on French geography than on almost any other country. Saint-Martin is the most pervasive figure: names beginning with Saint-Martin appear in 203 different communes — everything from Saint-Martin-d'Ablois (Marne) to Saint-Martin-de-Ré (Charente-Maritime) to Saint-Martin-de-Belleville (Savoie). That one bishop of Tours, who died in 397, generated more French commune names than any other saint.

The marathon names

At the far end of the distribution sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, one hyphen for every three letters, population 491, in the Marne. These marathon names are almost always the trace of an administrative fusion: two or three small medieval villages merged into one commune during the 19th or 20th centuries, each village insisting on keeping its name in the new hyphenated whole. The result is a place name that outlasted the reasoning behind it.

The names encode a thousand years of French history — Romanisation, Christianisation, feudal geography, revolutionary renaming, and post-war mergers. Most of that history is invisible in daily use. Y is just Y. Sainte-Colombe is just Sainte-Colombe. All twelve of them.
