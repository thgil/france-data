What's in a name?

35,014 communes, one called Y, and 3,891 saints — a data portrait of French place names.

2026-05-21 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is named Y.

Not an abbreviation. Not a placeholder. Y is a real village in the Somme, population 89, sitting quietly in the flat farmland of northern Picardy about 90 km north of Paris. It has been called Y — or something phonetically close to it — since the Middle Ages, the name likely derived from a local toponym. It holds one French record with certainty: the shortest commune name in the country.

Y
Commune in the Somme (dept. 80) — population 89 — the only single-letter commune in France.

Y is alone in the single-letter category. But the two-letter club has 13 members. They are a study in compressed French phonetics: Sy (Ardennes, pop. 54), By (Doubs, pop. 86), Oô (Haute-Garonne, pop. 103). Most of the short names cluster in northern and eastern France, relics of Old French or Frankish roots that resisted the standardising impulse of later centuries.

One nation, many saints

3,891 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. That's more than one in nine. France did not become a secular republic until 1905, and the saints got there first: the church had been naming settlements for the better part of a millennium by then. The saint names survived.

Saint-Martin leads by a comfortable margin, with 203 communes bearing the name in some form — Saint-Martin-de-Belleville, Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-sur-Oust, and 200 others. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow. Twelve Sainte-Colombes

Among exact name matches — not just prefix patterns — Sainte-Colombe wins. There are twelve of them, scattered across Normandy, Burgundy, the Landes, Gironde, and Île-de-France. They share a name and nothing else: the largest, Sainte-Colombe in the Rhône (pop. 1,953), sits on the banks of the Rhône opposite Vienne; the smallest, in the Hautes-Alpes, has 59 residents.

This is the repetition problem. France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names — meaning at least 2,250 names are shared by more than one commune. When a letter goes to "Saint-Jean," France has ten candidates. When you ask a Frenchman which Saint-Georges he means, he will look slightly weary and reach for the département.

The long tail of uniqueness

Despite the repetitions, the overwhelming story is distinctiveness. 31,314 communes — 89.4% — have a name that belongs to no other commune in France. Moustiers-Sainte-Marie, Lacapelle-Marival, Fleury-Mérogis, Châteauneuf-du-Faou: each is genuinely one of a kind. The median commune name is 12 characters. The mode — the most frequent length — is 7 or 8 characters, where French compound nouns and hyphenated village names cluster.

At the far end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters, Marne). It holds this record because three separate villages merged in 1972 rather than disappear, and each insisted on keeping its name. The result is a commune that requires two lines on a standard envelope.

Y sends 89 people off to school, work, and the weekly market. It mails letters with a two-character return address. It has a mayor, a budget, a road sign. The republic that produced elaborate bureaucratic standardisation also left room — somehow — for a commune whose entire name fits in a single keystroke. That feels very French.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes (metropolitan France and overseas territories).
