Y, and 35,013 other communes

France has a commune called Y. It has 89 residents, sits in the Somme, and shares a country with 35,013 neighbours — a country absolutely obsessed with saints, hyphens, and the letter combination that sounds like "ooh".

2026-05-21 5 min read

The commune of Y sits in the Somme, 12 kilometres north of Montdidier, at coordinates 49.8°N, 2.99°E. Its population is 89. Its name is a single letter. It is, so far as any registry confirms, the shortest place name in France — quite possibly in all of Europe.

Nobody is entirely sure how Y acquired its name. One theory involves a fork in the Roman road: a junction that looked, from above, like the letter. Another points to a corruption of the Picard word yeu, meaning water. The commune's own website leans into the mystery with a certain local pride. Whatever the origin, the French state has been perfectly comfortable, for several centuries, addressing official mail to "Y, Somme."

France has 35,014 communes in total — a number that puts it in a category of its own. Germany, with twice the population, has about 11,000. Spain has around 8,000. France's extreme communal fragmentation is a legacy of the Revolution: the National Constituent Assembly created a commune for nearly every parish in 1789, and most of them have survived every attempt at rationalisation since. The result is a country where roughly a third of all communes have fewer than 500 residents, and where a village of 89 people in Picardy still maintains its own mayor, its own budget, and its own single-letter name.

One in nine communes is a saint

The most visible pattern in the French commune naming system is religious. 3,891 communes — just over 11% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. France's patron saint coverage is so comprehensive that virtually every Christian saint of note has at least one commune named after them, and many have dozens.

The most duplicated name in all of France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in twelve different départements simultaneously — from Hautes-Alpes to Ille-et-Vilaine, from the Manche to Seine-et-Marne. Sainte Colombe of Sens, a 3rd-century martyr, clearly made a lasting impression. Twelve communes share her name with perfect administrative independence, each with its own INSEE code, its own mayor, and its own Sainte-Colombe town sign.

The runner-up, Saint-Sauveur, appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each score ten. Of the twenty most common commune names, seventeen start with Saint or Sainte. Non-saint placeholders — Beaulieu ("beautiful place"), Beaumont ("beautiful hill"), Brion, Le Pin — are the exceptions. The saint names win, overwhelmingly and in every direction.

The hyphen as administrative duct tape

France's second great naming convention is the hyphen. 13,410 communes — 38.3% of the total — carry at least one hyphen in their name. The hyphen is the tool of distinction: when two communes share a saint, you add the local river, valley, or hilltop. Saint-Pierre becomes Saint-Pierre-sur-Avesnette or Saint-Pierre-d'Oléron. When two villages merge, you concatenate. When that wasn't enough, you concatenate again.

The logical conclusion of this process is the longest commune name in France:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

45 characters. Located in the Marne (département 51), population 491. The name encodes the history of three former communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — fused into one administrative unit over the course of the 20th century. The commune embraces its unwieldy identity; it even appears on road signs.

The ultra-short communes

At the opposite extreme from Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson are France's thirteen two-letter communes. Y is alone in the one-letter category, but these thirteen are its nearest rivals:

Y Eu Us Gy Ri By Ry Sy Oz Py Ur Uz Oô Bû

Eu (Seine-Maritime, population ~7,500) is the most recognisable — a Norman port town with a long royal history, where William the Conqueror is said to have met Harold. Us is in Val-d'Oise. Gy is in Haute-Saône. Oô — with its circumflex — is a tiny Pyrenean commune in Haute-Garonne, notable mainly for having the only double-o-circumflex in the French postal system.

The name-length distribution

Across all 35,014 communes, the average name length is 11.8 characters. The most common lengths are 7 and 8 characters — names like Brioude, Morlaix, Libourne, Pontarlier. But the distribution has a long right tail, stretched by the hyphenated compound names. Roughly 5,300 communes have names longer than 20 characters; 100 exceed 27.

The French commune system is sometimes described as a political anachronism — too many units, too small, too expensive to maintain. Successive governments have pushed mergers. The 2010 reform created the commune nouvelle framework to incentivise consolidation. And yet the count barely moves. Communes persist. Names accumulate hyphens. Y, Somme, remains Y.

France has 35,014 reasons to think a place deserves its own name. That's probably about right.

A note on the data. Commune names and counts come from the DREES APL 2023 database (34,886 communes with APL scores), supplemented by the Etalab administrative boundaries file (2025 vintage). The total of 35,014 reflects the GeoJSON as used in the medical deserts story, which includes overseas territories. Name analysis was done in Python against the communeName field in communes-apl.geojson.
