What's in a name?

35,014 French communes. 32,764 distinct names. One commune is called Y.

2026-06-03 5 min read

France is carved into 35,014 communes — a number that famously exceeds the rest of the European Union combined. Most have unremarkable names: Saint-Something, Le Something, a handful of letters inherited from a village that predates the Republic. But pull the full list and some patterns snap into focus immediately. And some names just make you stop.

Y is a place

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter: Y. Population 89. Located in the Somme (département 80), it sits between Amiens and Saint-Quentin and has been called Y since at least the 13th century — the name is believed to derive from a Gaulish or Germanic root meaning "water" or "island." It has a mayor. It has a church. It has a postcode. It is, in every administrative sense, a commune.

Thirteen other communes make do with two letters. The two-letter club includes Sy (Ardennes, pop. 54), By (Doubs, pop. 86), Bû (Eure-et-Loir, pop. 2,022), Oô (Haute-Garonne, pop. 103 — and yes, the circumflex is intentional), and Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) — the largest two-letter commune in France, and arguably the most useful to spell.

Longest names

At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters, population 491, in the Marne. It holds two saints and three place-names hyphenated together — the mark of a commune fusion, where two or more villages merged under a single administrative umbrella and couldn't agree on whose name went first. The next longest, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône (pop. 923) — four villages, one commune, zero brevity.

The saint hegemony

4,424 communes — one in eight — carry saint or sainte somewhere in their name. That's 12.6% of all communes, home to roughly 11% of the French population. The Christianisation of place names started in the Merovingian period and never really stopped; every martyred bishop, every obscure desert father, every local miracle-worker left a commune bearing their name.

Which saint appears most often? Saint-Martin, with 249 communes — nearly one per département. Saint-Martin was the bishop of Tours who famously shared his cloak with a beggar; he became the patron saint of France, and the landscape reflects it. Saint-Jean (168) and Saint-Pierre (163) follow. Together, the top three saints appear in over 580 communes.

Most common saint names (communes)

The most saintly département is Dordogne (24), with 145 saint communes out of its roughly 500 total — nearly one in three. Gironde (33), Seine-Maritime (76), and Saône-et-Loire (71) follow. These are old agricultural landscapes with small, stable parishes. The Bassin Parisien and the Alps have fewer saints per square kilometre; their communes tend toward geographic descriptions — rivers, hills, forests.

How French communes begin

3,596 commune names begin with Saint — one in ten. Nothing else comes close. After that: La (1,054), Le (758), Les (328). The article-first naming pattern — defining a place by what's prominent in it, with the definite article leading — is distinctly French. La Roche. Le Moulin. Les Eaux. Then Sainte (295), and a long tail of recurrent roots: Villers (105 communes beginning with it), Villeneuve (70), Val (67).

There are twelve Sainte-Colombes

3,700 communes — more than 10% — share their exact name with at least one other commune in France. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times across 12 different départements: from Charente-Maritime to the Yonne, from Isère to Manche. There is no one Sainte-Colombe; there are a dozen, each with its own mayor, its own church, its own postcode starting with a different two digits.

This creates genuine administrative confusion. Mail goes astray. GPS systems hesitate. And when a news story mentions "Sainte-Colombe," the reader in nine out of twelve of them assumes it must be about somewhere else. The post office handles it via the département code. Everyone else just guesses.

38% with a hyphen

13,410 commune names — 38.3% — contain at least one hyphen. The hyphen is the administrative suture: it joins merged villages, it links a saint to a suffix, it stitches a geographic descriptor to a proper noun. Every hyphen in a commune name is a small piece of local history: a merger in the 1970s, a distinction from a neighbouring commune of similar name, a bureaucratic compromise between two village councils who both wanted to be named first.

France's commune names are not merely addresses. They are a compressed archive of settlement patterns, religious history, linguistic geography, and administrative politics — all squeezed into an average of twelve characters and catalogued in a national register that the Republic has been updating, continuously, since 1790.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the communeName field in the DREES/Etalab APL dataset (34,886 communes), joined to the 2025 administrative boundaries GeoJSON.
