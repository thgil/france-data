What's in a name?

35,014 commune names — one letter to 45, twelve Sainte-Colombes, and a village that breaks autocomplete.

2026-05-12 5 min read

France has 35,014 administrative communes. Each one has a name — registered, official, printed on road signs and tax forms and INSEE census reports. The names range from monosyllabic to sprawling. They carry centuries of saint veneration, royal geography, and village mergers that fused two names into one with a hyphen. Aggregated across all 35,014 of them, a statistical portrait emerges.

Y

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter. Y is a commune of 89 residents in the Somme, département 80, about 110 kilometres north of Paris. Its INSEE code is 80829. The local post office has presumably developed coping strategies.

There are also 13 two-letter communes: Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591), Gy (Haute-Saône, 1,001), Oz (Isère, 213), Bû (Eure-et-Loir, 2,022), and nine others. Most are in northern and eastern France, where Norse, Germanic, and Gaulish name roots were short to begin with.

At the other end of the spectrum sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 506 residents and a 45-character name that is the result of a 1972 merger that nobody rationalised. It is followed closely by Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados). The average is 11.8 characters.

The shape of French place names

Most commune names cluster between 6 and 10 characters, peaking around 7–8. The distribution has a long right tail pulled by hyphenated compound names — the kind that resulted from the 1972 Marcellin Law, which encouraged communes to merge while retaining both original names, joined by hyphens.

One in nine communes is a saint

The most striking pattern in the data is the dominance of saints. 3,596 communes begin with Saint-; another 302 begin with Sainte-. Together that is 3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total, or roughly one in nine. The French church, working over twelve centuries of Christianisation, named the map.

A further 1,054 begin with La, 758 with Le, and 328 with Les — the definite article as toponym, pointing at a geographical feature that the village grew around. Then come the recurring stems: Villers (105 communes), Villeneuve (70), Val (67), Pont (63), Fontaine (58).

Which Sainte-Colombe?

6.4% of commune names are shared by more than one commune — 3,700 communes that are not uniquely named. Postal systems handle this via the département code; GPS does the same. But the data reveals just how deep the duplication runs.

Sainte-Colombe wins: 12 communes across 12 different départements, ranging from a hamlet of 59 in Hautes-Alpes to a suburb of Lyon (Sainte-Colombe in Rhône, pop. 1,953) that sits on the opposite bank of the Rhône from Vienne. None of them are the same place. The full top tier is all saints: Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each), Saint-Marcel, Saint-Michel, Le Pin, and Saint-Pierre (9 each).

A linguistic register

These names are not just cultural residue. They are administrative infrastructure. Every one of the 35,014 is tied to a code postal, an INSEE code, a tax jurisdiction, a boundary on a GeoJSON file. The single letter Y processes payroll and elections and vehicle registrations for 89 people who find it perfectly normal. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson does the same for 506.

The 1972 law that produced those hyphenated giants was trying to save small communes from extinction by merging them administratively while preserving both identities in the name. Some of those names are still waiting for a second merger — or a braver préfet.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which bundles INSEE commune codes, names, populations, and administrative boundaries. The 35,014 figure is the count of distinct commune features in that dataset (metropolitan France and overseas territories, 2023/2025 vintage). Commune names match the official INSEE nomenclature. Paris, Marseille, and Lyon arrondissements are counted as separate communes here, consistent with how they appear in the INSEE data.
