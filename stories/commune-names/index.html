A republic of Saints — and one lonely Y

35,014 communes. 32,764 unique names. And one commune that answers to a single letter.

2026-05-17 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme — département 80, between Amiens and Saint-Quentin — there is a commune called Y. Just Y. Population: 89. It is, officially, the shortest place name in France, and one of the shortest in the world. Its residents receive mail addressed to the Rue de l'Église, Y, 80190. The postman presumably has opinions about this.

Y is not a typo or an administrative accident. It is an ancient name, probably derived from the Picard word for water (eau), in use since at least the 13th century. The commune has survived every administrative reform, every map revision, every postal-code redesign. It is still Y.

Y France's shortest commune name — one letter, population 89, département 80 (Somme)

The two-letter club

Beyond Y, thirteen communes manage their affairs with just two letters. They are spread across France — from Eu in Seine-Maritime (pop. 6,591, the largest of the group) to Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées (pop. 36, the smallest). The Pyrénées are particularly economical: Py and Ur both sit in the Pyrénées-Orientales. The Norman tradition gives us Ry and Eu in Seine-Maritime. Oô in Haute-Garonne — two letters, two identical — manages to be both very short and pleasantly symmetrical.

The bell curve of naming

Most commune names fall between 6 and 22 characters, with a peak around 7–8 letters (names like Morteau, Valloire, Beaumont) and a long right tail from hyphenated compound names. The average is 11.8 characters; the median is 10. At both extremes — Y at one end, 45-character fusions at the other — something unusual is happening.

The 45-letter champion

At the other end sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne. Forty-five characters. The name is essentially two former commune names joined with a hyphen and a connector — the result of a 1973 merger between Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. France has roughly 1,600 of these communes nouvelles created by merger since the 1970 Marcellin law, and many carry both names in the title to preserve local identity. Administrative efficiency and historical sentiment make awkward bedfellows.

The runner-up — Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) — is another merger. So are most of the top ten. The pattern is consistent: the longer the name, the more towns it used to be.

One in eight communes is a saint

France has 35,014 communes, and 4,437 of them — 12.7% — have the word saint somewhere in their name. That is nearly one in eight. The explanation is historical: for most of France's settled history, the local church was the dominant public institution, and the parish name — usually the patron saint — became the commune name. The Revolution tried briefly to rename many: Saint-Omer became l'Omer, Saint-Malo became Port-Malo. By 1800 most had reverted. The saints survived the Republic.

Martin is the single most common saintly root — 255 commune names carry it. Pierre (180), Jean (170), Germain (120), and Julien (88) follow. The top names chart below shows how dramatically saints dominate the most-shared names.

The most common names — and what they share

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

Sainte-Colombe — named for a 3rd-century Christian martyr beheaded in Sens — is the single most duplicated commune name in France. Twelve different communes carry it, in twelve different départements, with nothing in common except the saint. Together they have a combined population of about 6,000. If you address a letter to Sainte-Colombe without a postal code, it could end up in any of twelve places, from Hautes-Alpes (pop. 59) to Yonne (pop. 185). The postal system's patience is infinite.

In total, 1,450 commune names are shared by more than one commune — covering 3,700 communes in all. France solved this administratively by requiring the five-digit INSEE commune code on all official addresses. But the names themselves remain gloriously, stubbornly non-unique.

What's in a name

Every one of the 35,014 names in the register is the product of geography, history, religion, dialect, and chance. Gex is ancient Gaulish. Oz (Isère) predates French. Concarneau is Breton.
