From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

35,014 communes, from a single letter to 45 characters — what French place names reveal about saints, rivers, and administrative mergers gone long.

2026-05-13 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y.

Not a typo. Not an acronym. Y — one letter, officially written in uppercase, population 89, département de la Somme. It holds a record that is, by definition, impossible to beat: the shortest commune name of any country that uses the Latin alphabet.

The shortest commune name in France
Y
Somme (80) · 89 residents · INSEE code 80829

Y is followed by 13 communes with two-letter names. Most are in the north and east — a toponymic pattern that reflects the presence of older Germanic and Frankish roots that survived the Romanisation of French place-name conventions.

A bell curve that peaks at 7–8 characters

The median commune name is 10 characters long. The modal length — the most common — is 7 or 8 characters (3,695 communes each). From there the distribution tails off in both directions: steeply toward the very short, and gently toward the very long, with a second small hump in the 15–20 character range caused by compound hyphenated names.

The long end: merger artifacts

At the other extreme is a commune in the Marne whose name is the administrative equivalent of a Russian novel title:

The longest commune name in France — 45 characters
Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 491 residents · a 1972 merger of three villages

The runner-up is nearly as unwieldy: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône). Third place: Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados). The pattern is obvious — these are all communes nouvelles, administrative mergers that preserved both original names by concatenating them. The longer the merger chain, the longer the name.

France has been merging communes since at least the Revolutionary era, with waves of consolidation in the 1970s and again since the loi NOTRe of 2015. Each merger is a negotiation. Sometimes one village name wins outright. Sometimes both survive — hyphenated together, lengthening the map one bureaucratic compromise at a time.

One in nine communes is a saint

3,891 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with Saint or Sainte. That is not a linguistic curiosity. It is a sediment layer of medieval Christian parish geography: the church was, for most of French history, the administrative unit that gave a settlement its identity. The saint's name was the parish name. The parish name became the commune name. There it stays.

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — shared by 12 different communes across 9 départements. Sainte Columba was a 3rd-century martyr venerated across the Romanised west, and she left her name on hillsides from the Gard to the Côte-d'Or. Saint-Sauveur comes second (11 communes), followed by Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each).

89% are one of a kind

Despite the saints and the duplicates, most French communes have a name found nowhere else in the country. 31,314 of 35,014 — 89.4% — are the only commune in France with that exact name. French place names have been shaped by Gaulish, Latin, Frankish, Occitan, Breton, Alsatian, Basque, and Catalan roots, with centuries of local phonetic drift on top. The result is a catalogue of singularities: Oô, Uz, Bû, Ry, Eps, Voh.

1,991 communes on a river

The second-most productive naming convention after the saints is the river preposition. 1,991 communes include -sur- in their name — Asnières-sur-Saône, Charnoz-sur-Ain, Conflans-Sainte-Honorine-sur-Seine. A further 974 use -en- (in a territory or forest). Together these two prepositions mark half the topographic vocabulary of French commune naming: you are either beside water, or inside a landscape.

The data is all there in the names. 35,014 communes, one letter at a time, from Y to the Marne.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the communes-apl.geojson produced for the medical-deserts story — a join of DREES APL 2023 scores to the Etalab contours-administratifs 2025 boundary file, covering 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name analysis was performed in Python from this source; centroids were computed from polygon vertex averages. A lightweight index (communes-index.json, ~3 MB) with names, codes, départements, populations, and centroids is available in this story's folder for reuse.
