What's in a commune name?

35,014 communes, one single-letter name, and 1,450 cases of déjà vu.

2026-05-22 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. They have names like Saint-Rémy-lès-Chevreuse and Villers-sous-Saint-Leu and Montigny-le-Bretonneux. They have names like L'Abergement-Clémenciat (a real place in Ain, population 832). And then there's one commune — population 89, département de la Somme — whose official name is a single letter.

Y A commune in the Somme. Population 89. The shortest commune name in France — one character, legally recognised, entirely official.

Y is not a typo. It is not a placeholder. It is a town of 89 people that has existed, under that name, since at least the twelfth century. The name most likely derives from a Gallo-Roman root — a watery place, a confluence. Etymologically reasonable. Typographically spectacular.

From Y to a 45-character monster

The name spectrum runs from that single letter all the way to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — a 45-character Marne commune of 491 people that apparently needed two saints in its address. The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The chart below shows how that mass distributes.

The distribution is right-skewed but fairly tight. Most names cluster between 8 and 18 characters. Single-word names account for 57.7% of communes — names like Beaulieu, Beaumont, Villemur. Three-part hyphenated names (the "Saint-X-de-Y" pattern) make up another 19.5%. Only 79 communes have names longer than 30 characters.

The Saint factory

The single most common pattern in French commune names is the saint prefix. 3,596 communes — one in ten — start with Saint-. Add the 295 Sainte- prefixes and you reach 3,898 communes, or 11.1% of the total. That's more than the combined count of communes in any single département except Pas-de-Calais.

The distribution isn't uniform. Ardèche (dept 07) has 97 saint-prefixed communes out of 335 total — 29% of its entire commune roster. Creuse (23), Loire (42), and Haute-Vienne (87) follow close behind, all above 27%. These are old Catholic heartlands where the parish network survived intact into the revolutionary period and simply got bureaucratically encoded.

When names collide

89.4% of French communes have a name that belongs to them alone. The other 10.6% share. There are 1,450 names that appear in more than one commune — and the collision count can get surprisingly high.

Sainte-Colombe holds the record: twelve separate communes, spread across twelve different départements, each legally distinct, each officially named the same thing. If someone tells you they're from Sainte-Colombe, that could be the one in Charente-Maritime, or the one in Gironde, or the one in Haute-Saône, or — well, nine other places.

The pattern isn't random. Nearly all the most-duplicated names involve saints or generic landscape terms — Beaulieu (beautiful place), Beaumont (beautiful hill), Verrières (glassworks). These were named independently, by different communities, before France had a national registry that cared about collisions. The problem got real when the post office arrived.

France has been building this list for over a thousand years. Names got added at different rates in different places — sometimes by the church, sometimes by royal decree, sometimes by the commune itself. The result is a catalogue that runs from the sublimely terse to the administratively baroque, from Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, with 35,012 stops in between.

The shortest took one letter. The longest took two saints, three prepositions, and four hyphens. Both count as one commune each, weighted exactly the same in any official tabulation. That's the point of the registry: every commune is equal, regardless of how hard it is to fit on an envelope.

A note on the data. Commune names and boundaries come from Etalab's 2025 contours administratifs (1000m simplification). Population figures are from the APL 2023 dataset (DREES, base INSEE 2021). The 35,014 commune count includes overseas territories. Centroids were computed from polygon geometries using the shoelace formula.
