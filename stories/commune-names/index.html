What's in a name?

35,014 French communes — one called Y, 3,898 called Saint-something, and a village in the Marne whose name runs to 45 characters.

2026-05-19 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. The smallest name belongs to a hamlet in the Somme, population 89, sitting on a hill above the Noye valley. Its full official name, as recorded in the INSEE commune register, is a single letter: Y.

Y is not an abbreviation. It is not a placeholder. It is the name. The commune has been called Y since at least the 12th century — the letter derives from the Old Picard for a small stream. It is the only one-letter commune in France, and probably in Europe.

Y is not alone in its brevity. Thirteen communes manage with just two letters. They include Eu (Seine-Maritime, a port town of 6,591 people), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, population 36 — the smallest of the two-letter group), and Oô, a Pyrenean village whose name requires a circumflex. All thirteen:

One name in nine starts with Saint

France was Christianised early and thoroughly, and the commune map shows it. Of the 35,014 communes in the register, 3,898 — exactly 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. If you made those communes into a country, it would have roughly 6.6 million people.

After Saint and Sainte, the most common openers are La (1,054 communes), Le (758), and Les (328). Articles account for another 6.3% of commune names. Put saint-names and article-names together and you've explained nearly one in five.

The same name, in twelve places

France has more communes than any other country in the EU — a legacy of the Revolutionary-era decision to make every village an administrative unit. With 35,014 communes to name, France eventually ran out of ideas. Of the 32,764 unique names in use, 1,450 are shared by two or more communes. That puts 2,250 communes — roughly 1 in 16 — in a naming collision.

The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in twelve different départements. Saint-Sauveur follows with eleven. The French postal system handles this by tying commune names to their five-digit INSEE code (not a zip code — France has separate postal codes). Without that code, a letter addressed to Beaulieu could end up at any of ten different villages.

The long and the short of it

The median commune name is 10 characters long. The mean is 11.8, pulled upward by the long tail of hyphenated behemoths. Most names — over 16,000 — fall in the 6-to-10 character range. But 50 communes have names longer than 30 characters, relics of the 1970s fusion de communes movement when neighbouring villages were merged and nobody could agree on whose name to drop.

Communes by name length

The longest name in France belongs to a village of 491 people in the Marne, department 51 — wine country, halfway between Reims and Troyes:

Longest commune name in France — 45 characters
Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · population 491 · INSEE code 51513 · APL 4.09 (well-served)

The name is not a typo. It encodes four separate localities — Saint-Rémy, Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — that were merged into a single commune in 1972. The hyphens are load-bearing. Every clause is a former village, now an address line on a birth certificate.

Names as a map of history

The commune name register is a sediment record. The Saint- prefix carries the medieval church. The Villers- cluster (105 communes) comes from the Latin villare, a farm. The fused names of the 1970s mark a decade of administrative rationalism that mostly failed — many mergers were reversed in the 1980s. And Y, sitting on its hill, predates all of it.

France counts its communes obsessively. Each one has an INSEE code, a mayor, a tax rate, and a name that may well be unique — or may be shared with eleven others. The data doesn't judge. It just counts.

Commune names and properties come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories, joined to commune boundaries from the Contours administratifs 2025 (Etalab).
