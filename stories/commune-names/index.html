What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes — from the one-letter Y in the Somme to a 45-character hyphenated colossus in the Marne.

2026-05-20 5 min read

Y is a real commune. It sits in the Somme, département 80, population 89. It has the shortest place name in France — one letter, no accent, no ambiguity. Its full postal address reads Y, 80190 Y. Eighty-nine people live there. The name most likely descends from Old Norse ey, meaning something like "water meadow" or "island" — the Somme basin being very much that kind of landscape.

At the other end sits a mouthful: 45 characters, five hyphens, two saints, and one conjunction. It's a merged commune in the Marne, population 491 — two formerly separate villages stitched together by administrative decree, the et in the middle doing the work of explaining why the name is so long. France has dozens of these: wherever you see -et- in a commune name, you're reading a merger.

Saint/Sainte communes

35,014 communes, but only 32,764 unique names. That means 2,250 names are shared by at least two different communes — places that happen to have arrived at the same name independently, in different parts of France, centuries apart. The post office and the GPS tell them apart by département code. History gave them the same name.

The shape of French place names

Most commune names cluster in a comfortable range. The mode is 7–8 characters. Beaulieu is 8. Bordeaux is 8. Grenoble is 9. But the distribution has a noticeable right tail — all those hyphenated compound names, especially the saint dedications followed by a geographic qualifier, push names well beyond 20 characters.

One commune in eight is a saint

3,891 commune names start with Saint or Sainte — just over 11% of all communes. Medieval Christianity left a denser mark on French geography than any other force. The most popular saint by far: Martin. Not Saint-Martin exactly — there's no single commune with that plain name that dominates — but 202 communes whose name begins with Saint-Martin, each followed by its own qualifier (Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-du-Tertre, Saint-Martin-le-Vieux, and 199 more). Saint-Jean and Saint-Pierre trail him.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most common exact commune name in France is not Saint-Martin or Saint-Pierre. It's Sainte-Colombe — twelve communes, twelve different places, each named after the third-century Gallic martyr Colombe de Sens. Sainte-Colombe-de-Peyre in the Lozère. Sainte-Colombe-de-la-Commanderie in the Pyrénées-Orientales. Sainte-Colombe-sur-Gand in the Loire. And nine more, spread across nine other départements. They share a name and a patron saint, and practically nothing else.

A layered text

The names of France's 35,014 communes are an archaeology. Y is Old Norse — a Viking-age toponym that survived Carolingian administration, the Revolution, and five centuries of bureaucratic reform intact. Beaulieu (10 communes) is Norman French. The saints are medieval Christianity's imprint on the landscape, applied village by village over a thousand years. The hyphenated colossi are 20th-century mergers — administrative logic dressed up in whatever names the constituent villages happened to have.

2,250 communes share a name with at least one other commune. The 32,764 names that are genuinely unique: a vocabulary just big enough for a country.

A note on the data. Commune names and boundaries come from DREES's APL commune-level dataset (2023 vintage), joined to the Etalab administrative boundary file (2025 vintage, 1000m simplification). The source GeoJSON covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories (Guyane, Martinique, Guadeloupe, La Réunion, Mayotte). Paris, Lyon, and Marseille arrondissements appear as individual entries. All name analysis is based on the communeName field as provided by the source data.
