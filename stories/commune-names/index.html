What's in a commune name?

35,014 commune names: one letter long, 45 letters long, 3,882 saints, and 12 places all called the same thing.

2026-05-28 4 min read

France has a commune named Y. Not an abbreviation, not a code — the name, in full, is a single letter. It sits in the Somme, population 89, a small farming village whose name has puzzled mail carriers and cartographers for centuries. The commune of Y has been officially named Y since at least the medieval period, when Norman scribes recorded it as such. It holds the distinction of being the shortest place name in France, and quite possibly in Europe.

On the other end of the alphabet — and the alphabet — sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne that requires 45 characters just to write out. It came about through a series of municipal mergers, each one appending its old name to the growing chain. France has 35,014 communes in total. Their names span every length from 1 to 45 characters, carry the residue of every historical force that shaped the country — feudalism, the Church, Revolution, language — and tell a story about French identity that no census ever quite captures.

The saint invasion

The Catholic Church left a bigger mark on France's map than any other institution. 3,587 communes begin with Saint-, and a further 295 with Sainte-: together, 3,882 communes — or 11.1% of the total — carry a saint's name as their prefix. That share is consistent across centuries of geography: Dordogne (24) leads with 128 saint-named communes, followed by Gironde (33) with 121 and Isère (38) with 105. The south and west are thick with them; the far north and east, shaped by Protestant history and Germanic occupation, markedly less so.

The most common commune name in France — not just among saint-names, but overall — is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 different communes across 10 départements. Every Sainte-Colombe is a genuine place; each one named for the same third-century martyr, Saint Columba of Sens. Saint-Sauveur is second with 11; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu tie for third with 10 each. The duplicate problem

Of 35,014 communes, 32,764 have unique names — but 3,700 (10.6%) share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in France. There are 1,450 distinct names that belong to more than one place. Sainte-Colombe is the extreme: twelve entirely separate communes, twelve different mayors, twelve different post offices, all answering to the same name. The post code and INSEE commune code are the practical disambiguators; the name alone is not always enough.

The duplication pattern isn't random — it follows the same saint-prefix logic. Names like Saint-Martin, Saint-Pierre, Saint-Georges proliferate because Catholicism provided a shared naming vocabulary for thousands of independent medieval settlements with no knowledge of each other. Every bishop's seat could consecrate its own Saint-Martin; every coastal fishing village its own Saint-Pierre. France unified politically before it unified its names.

The shortest names

Below Y, there are thirteen 2-letter communes: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591 — the largest 2-letter commune), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Oz (Isère), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Most of them are tiny; most cluster in the Pyrenees or Normandy, reflecting either pre-Roman Basque and Gascon roots or old Norse influence from the Viking settlement of Normandy.

The marathon name

Longest commune name in France — 45 characters
Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · Population: ~500 · Created by merger of three communes in 1972

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne, at 45 characters. It was created in 1972 when three communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — merged into one and agreed to keep all three names. The result is a name that barely fits on a road sign, and has required special dispensation from the French postal authority. Second longest: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône, at 43 characters — also a merger product.

The pattern is consistent: almost every very long commune name is the product of a commune nouvelle or earlier merger, each constituent community insisting its identity be preserved in the new hyphenated chain. It's a bureaucratic record of local pride: nobody wanted to be the part of the name that got dropped.

The shape of French commune names

Most commune names are between 6 and 22 characters long, with a peak around 7–8 characters. The distribution has a long right tail — a few dozen communes with names above 30 characters, pulling the average up from what would otherwise be a fairly tight cluster. The one-character outlier (Y) and the 45-character outlier (the Marne marathon) sit at the extreme ends of a distribution that's otherwise surprisingly well-behaved.

The 35,014 names are a sediment record of France: Norman invasions (the two-letter coastal names), Carolingian Christianity (the saint-prefix tide), Revolutionary bureaucracy (the numbered départements that never replaced the commune names), and twenty-first century mergers that tried to erase old borders and couldn't quite manage it. Y is still Y. Sainte-Colombe is still twelve places at once. And somewhere in the Marne, a village called Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is filling out a form with a very small box.
