What's in a name?

35,014 French communes, from Y (one letter, 89 residents) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 letters, 7 hyphens). One in nine is named after a saint.

2026-05-21 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. They range from tiny hamlets to cities of millions. Their names span one letter to 45. A remarkable number of them are named after the same saints, carry the same hyphens, or share a name with a dozen other places entirely. The administrative directory of France turns out to be a kind of folk poetry — very repetitive folk poetry, with an unusual affection for Saint Martin.

The saints take over

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,882 — 11.1% — start with Saint- or Sainte-. That's more sainted municipalities than most European countries have municipalities at all. They invoke 1,020 distinct saints, from the expected (Martin, Jean, Pierre) to the obscure (Héand, Nizier, Pezenne).

202 communes are named Saint-Martin. That's more Saint-Martins than there are communes in Luxembourg.

202 communes. Luxembourg has 100.

The chart below shows the twenty saints with the most communes named after them. Saint-Martin leads by a clear margin, ahead of Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144). The top four — Martin, Jean, Pierre, Germain — account for 609 communes on their own. After the top dozen the numbers thin out quickly, but the long tail is vast: hundreds of saints each commemorated in exactly one or two communes.

The extremes

At one end: Y. A single letter. Department 80, the Somme. Population: 89. It was called Yons in the Middle Ages and the name eroded — across several centuries and administrative reforms — to its present one-character form. France's post service has had to deal with this since 1793.

Thirteen more communes manage exactly two characters. Some are equally blunt — Eu (Normandie, pop. 6,591), Oz (Isère, pop. 213), Gy (Haute-Saône, pop. 1,001). Thirteen two-letter communes scattered across France, each a small puzzle for a mail sorter.

At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. Seven hyphens. A commune in the Marne with 491 residents, formed by the merger of three older villages — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — sometime in the nineteenth century. Nobody shortened it. The name is a small monument to administrative stubbornness.

A bell curve of names

The distribution of name lengths isn't quite normal — it's right-skewed, pulled by the long tail of hyphenated compound names. The median sits at — characters. Most communes cluster between 8 and 18 characters: a single-word name or a modest two-part compound like Saint-Aubin or La Ferté. The extreme long names are rare; the extreme short ones, rarer.

When the same name exists in twelve departments

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 distinct names — meaning 2,250 names are used more than once. Sainte-Colombe is the most duplicated: twelve communes in twelve different departments all carry that name, from Sainte-Colombe (Ardèche) to Sainte-Colombe (Yonne). If you address a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France," the post office needs more information.

Key numbers

The duplicates aren't random — they're almost always saint names or descriptive geographic names (Beaulieu, "beautiful place"; Le Pin, "the pine"). When the medieval church dedicated a chapel to a popular saint, nearby villages often followed suit. Centuries later, their descendants share a name and sometimes a confused postal code.

The commune names are a compressed history of France. The saints mark where the church planted its roots. The compound hyphenated names mark the mergers: nineteenth-century administrative rationalizations that joined villages but couldn't quite bring themselves to drop a name. The short names — the Ys and the Eus — are just what happens when you stop trimming a name and start trimming the language around it.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the INSEE administrative boundaries file (Contours administratifs 2025, 1000m simplification). The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. A small number of entries are sub-municipal units (Paris/Lyon/Marseille arrondissements) counted here as separate entries, consistent with how INSEE codes them. Population figures are from APL 2023 (INSEE 2021 base).
