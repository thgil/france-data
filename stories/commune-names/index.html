One letter, 35,000 names

France has a commune called Y. It also has 4,195 saints, 2,007 rivers, and a 45-character monster in the Marne.

2026-06-01 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Most of them have perfectly sensible names — Lyon, Bordeaux, Strasbourg. Others are geographical descriptions hyphenated into something medieval. And one of them is called Y.

Y is a commune in the Somme département (code 80), population 89. It sits at the confluence of three rivers, one of which — the Avre's old tributary — was historically called Y in local Picard dialect, from a Germanic root meaning water. The commune took the river's name. The river's name was one letter. This seemed fine to everyone at the time.

The alphabetically challenged

Y is the only single-letter commune in France. Below it, there are 13 two-letter communes: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Oz (Isère), Py (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). Many are in mountain valleys — short names for short valleys, perhaps. Several share the same département as their near-homophone neighbours.

Most names are 10 characters long

The average commune name is 11.8 characters. The median is 10. The distribution is right-skewed — the long tail extends well past 30 characters, pulled by hyphenated compounds that accumulated over centuries of mergers, ecclesiastical boundaries, and topographic precision.

45 characters · Marne (51) · population 491 · the longest commune name in France

The longest commune name in France — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — contains three separate place names hyphenated together. It is a commune nouvelle: a municipality formed by the merger of smaller communes. France's 2015 law encouraging mergers has been generating some formidably long names ever since, as merged communities insist on preserving all their constituent parts.

One name in eight starts with a saint

France has 4,195 communes whose name starts with Saint or Sainte — 12% of the total. Add in the communes where a saint appears elsewhere in the name (Saint-Jean-sur-Erve, Port-Saint-Louis) and the total rises to 4,443 communes with a saint somewhere.

The most popular saints, by commune count, tell a story of medieval parish geography. Saint Martin (202 communes) was the patron saint of the Frankish kingdom. Saint Jean (155) and Saint Pierre (144) followed. Saint Germain (108) reflects the influence of the bishop of Paris whose name spread across Carolingian Gaul. The cult of local saints — Hilaire, Georges, Julien, Laurent — then filled in the gaps.

This is not just a trivia problem. Before postal codes were introduced in 1964, letters sent to "Sainte-Colombe" would reach the wrong village. The solution was to add the département name: Sainte-Colombe-sur-l'Hers, Sainte-Colombe-de-Peyre. France evolved an entire disambiguation grammar, built on hyphens, prepositions, and the names of whatever river or hill was nearby.

France is a country of rivers

2,007 commune names include the word sur — "on" or "above" — as in Châlons-sur-Marne or Boulogne-sur-Mer. It is France's most effective disambiguation tool: if two villages share a saint's name, tell people which river yours is on. The Loire and Seine are each responsible for 70 "sur" communes. The sea (sur-Mer) is the single most common suffix, with 99 communes.

The vocabulary of French geography

Strip away the saints and rivers and a second vocabulary emerges. Martin, Jean, Pierre are the great saints. Bois (wood), Mer (sea), Val (valley), Mont (hill), Château (castle), Forêt (forest) — these are the physical features that communities reached for when they needed to say where they were. And Villers, Villeneuve, Villiers, Neuville: variations on villa, the Frankish word for settlement, scattered across the north and east of France like a linguistic fossil record.

The 35,000 names are not random. They are a sediment of Latin, Frankish, Norse, Gaulish, and Occitan — compressed into hyphenated sequences that the post office has learned to read and the GPS occasionally gets wrong. Y sits at one end of the distribution. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson sits at the other. The 34,998 in between are the accumulated decisions of parishes, notaries, and cartographers across a thousand years of French territory-making.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are drawn from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to administrative boundaries, which covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories. Centroids are computed from commune polygon geometries. The communes-index.json file (2.7 MB) is the canonical reference for this story.
