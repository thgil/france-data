What France calls its villages

35,014 communes — one named Y, 3,891 starting with Saint, and the longest clocking in at 45 characters. French place names are a compressed history of the country.

2026-05-19 5 min read

France has more communes than Germany, Italy, Spain, and the UK combined. The 35,014 municipalities that tile the hexagon — and its overseas territories — are the basic unit of French public life. They collect your rubbish, run your école primaire, and issue your carte de séjour. They also have names, and those names are a compressed history of everything that made France France.

Religious conversion. River crossings. Viking raids. Frankish land grants. Latin lingering into the Middle Ages. Every one of the 35,014 names encodes something that happened, or was hoped for, or was imposed by someone with a pen and a stamp.

I — The shortest

Y is a place

The commune of Y sits in the Somme, population 89. One letter. No hyphen, no article, no saint. The French postal code is 80829. It appears in official census documents, on road signs, in the national commune registry, as a single capital letter followed by a blank.

1 The shortest commune name in France. One letter. The commune of Y, in the Somme.

Below Y (alphabetically if not in length), France has thirteen two-letter communes. Most are in mountainous or border territory where Gallo-Roman names survived with minimal French embellishment. Eu, in Seine-Maritime (pop. 6,591), is the most substantial. Uz, in the Pyrénées, has 36 residents.

II — The most devout

The Saint republic

One in nine French communes begins with Saint or Sainte — 3,891 of them, or 11.1% of the total. This is the long echo of Christianisation: every valley got a patron, every patron got a church, and the church named the village. The pattern is densest in the west (Bretagne, Poitou, Maine) and the south-west, and thinner in Alsace and the Mediterranean where different settlement histories left different traces.

3,891 Communes starting with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of all communes in France.

Saint-Martin leads by a wide margin. With 203 communes bearing the name — one for roughly every 175km² of French territory — Martin of Tours turns out to be the best-franchised saint in the French countryside. Saint-Jean (155), Saint-Pierre (144), and Saint-Germain (108) fill out the top four.

III — The copies

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

Of 35,014 communes, 32,764 have a unique name — which means 2,250 names are duplicated somewhere. In total, 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. The most duplicated name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which exists in twelve different locations across the country. If you tell someone you live in Sainte-Colombe without specifying the département, they may correctly guess your village on the twelfth try.

The pattern is almost entirely saints and topographic adjectives: Beaulieu (beautiful place), Beaumont (beautiful hill), Le Pin (the pine), Verrières (glassworks). Wherever settlers arrived in a new valley, they reached for the same small vocabulary.

IV — The rivers

Water wrote the map

2,007 French communes include the word sur — meaning the settlement sits on, or at, something. On a river. On a mountain. On the sea. The construction Ville-sur-Chose is the original French GPS coordinate: if you know the river, you can find the town.

2,007 Communes that are -sur- something — situated on a river, a mountain, or the sea.

-sur-Mer (on the sea) edges the other big rivers at 99 communes, reflecting France's long coastline and the habit of naming fishing villages by their one defining feature. The Loire and Seine follow at 70 each. The Marne (55), the Meuse (43), and the Saône (34) round out the inland waterways that gave thousands of settlements their identity.

V — The extremes

From one letter to forty-five

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters. Most names cluster between 8 and 16 characters — long enough to carry meaning, short enough to fit a road sign. At the top of the distribution, things get complicated.

France's longest commune name is a 45-character marathon, the result of an administrative merger that preserved both original names rather than choosing one:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Marne (dept 51) · formed by merger

Runner-up, at 43 characters: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (Haute-Saône). Third: Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 characters, Calvados). Every name above 35 characters is a compound created when French municipalities fused in the 1970s under the loi Marcellin — and each one represents a village that insisted on staying nominally present in its new bureaucratic identity.

Below 11.8 characters lie thousands of ancient one-word names: Breton, Gascon, Basque, Alsatian, Occitan survivals that resisted Frenchification. The country's linguistic patchwork is legible in the distribution of name lengths alone.

The names persist

France's 35,014 communes are the most durable institution in the country. Dynasties rise and fall, constitutions replace constitutions, departments get numbered and renumbered, but the commune endures — and with it, the name. Y has been Y for centuries. The twelve Sainte-Colombes were named before France was fully France. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is a mouthful, but it is the name of a real place that real people call home, and they would like you to spell it correctly on their mail.
