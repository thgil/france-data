What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes — one called Y, twelve called Sainte-Colombe, and 3,898 that start with Saint.

2026-05-19 4 min read

France is a country of 35,014 communes. Most have names you'd recognise — Lyon, Strasbourg, Bordeaux. But peel back the roster and you find a long tail of oddities: names that are one letter long, names that are forty-five letters long, and names that are shared — identically — by twelve different places at once. The commune name is a compressed piece of history: a saint's patronage, a river, a feudal lord, a merger hammered together by bureaucratic necessity. Read enough of them and France starts to look like a very large, very confusing filing cabinet.

One letter

Y

There is a commune in the Somme, in northern France, with 89 residents and one of the most efficient names in all of French administration. It is called Y. Just Y. Postal code 80700. INSEE code 80829. The name is believed to derive from a Celtic or Gaulish root — possibly related to eau (water) — though nobody is quite sure. What is certain: if you ever need to alphabetically list French communes, this one takes very little space.

Thirteen communes manage two-letter names — a small club of brevity scattered across the country: By (Doubs), Bû (Eure-et-Loir), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py and Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), Ri (Orne), Ry (Seine-Maritime), Sy (Ardennes), Us (Val-d'Oise), and Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées). The Pyrénées-Orientales contributes two, perhaps reflecting the influence of Catalan, which does not waste letters.

The age of saints

France is, nominally at least, a secular republic — but its geography still belongs to the church. Of the country's 35,014 communes, 3,898 start with Saint or Sainte. That is 11.1% of the entire country, named in honour of a Christian martyr or confessor. The patron saint system, formalised in medieval parish records and locked in by the Napoleonic administrative codification of the early 1800s, means that France's map is partly a hagiography. Saint Martin alone — patron of France itself — shows up in hundreds of variants: Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-en-Bresse, Saint-Martin-d'Hères, Saint-Martin-Valmeroux. Finding the right one without the département is genuinely difficult.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The most duplicated name in France is Sainte-Colombe. It appears twelve times — in the Ain, Ardèche, Charente, Côte-d'Or, Gironde, Rhône, Saône-et-Loire, Var, Vienne, Yonne, Eure, and Hérault. When a French national office sends a letter to Sainte-Colombe, it must also specify the département. When a historian writes about a medieval incident in Sainte-Colombe, ambiguity is guaranteed. Saint-Sauveur, at 11 occurrences, comes close; Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu follow at 10 each.

In total, 1,450 distinct names appear in two or more communes — accounting for 3,700 instances. One in ten French communes shares its exact name with a stranger somewhere else in the country.

Forty-five characters of bureaucracy

At the other end of the spectrum sits the longest commune name in France: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne (Champagne). Forty-five characters, five hyphens, two saints. It is a commune nouvelle — a merged municipality — formed in 1972 when several communes were administratively joined. The French policy of merging small communes into larger administrative units, accelerating since the 2010 law on communes nouvelles, has produced a rash of hyphenated compounds that push name lengths well above historical norms. Longest commune names

    A nation of hyphens

    38.3% of French communes — 13,410 of them — contain at least one hyphen. This is extraordinary by European standards: German Gemeinde and English parishes rarely hyphenate at all. The French hyphen does two jobs. First, it joins a generic to a specific: Saint-, Le-, sur-, en-. Second, in the era of communes nouvelles, it links former rivals: the merger of Roche-sur-Linotte and Sorans-les-Cordiers in Haute-Saône produced the forty-character Roche-sur-Linotte-et-Sorans-les-Cordiers, each half retaining its name. The hyphen is the diplomatic compromise of French municipal politics.

    The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The median is 10. When you see a commune name that is comfortably under that, it usually predates the Revolution. When you see one that runs to thirty or forty characters, a merger is almost certainly involved — and somewhere in the archives there is a vote, a bureaucratic battle, and a compromise nobody was fully happy with.

    A note on the data. Commune names come from the INSEE commune codes embedded in the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name lengths are computed on the official name as it appears in the INSEE register. The communes-index.json source file includes centroids computed from administrative polygon boundaries.
