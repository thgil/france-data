Y is a village

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Another is 45 letters long. The names are the history.

2026-05-23 5 min read

In the Somme, about 40 kilometres east of Amiens, there is a commune whose official name is a single letter.

Y

Population: 89. Département: Somme (80). APL score: 2.78 — technically adequate for GP access, barely.

It is not an abbreviation. It is not a placeholder. Y is the name. It appears that way on road signs, on tax documents, on envelope addresses. Postmen have delivered to it for centuries. The commune's origins are medieval; the name likely derives from a Gaulish or Old Frankish root meaning water or island, compressed over generations into the one letter that remained.

The two-letter club

Y is alone at one character. But France has 13 communes with two-letter names — enough to fill a postage-stamp atlas of brevity.

The short names are mostly Norman and Celtic survivals — consonant clusters compressed by time and dialect into phonetic atoms. Eu (Seine-Maritime, population 6,591) is the largest of the two-letter group, old enough to have given its name to a royal forest mentioned in 11th-century charters. Oô (Haute-Garonne, population 103) sits at 1,290 metres in the Pyrenees and carries its two letters with improbable grace. Oz (Isère) is a ski resort; its name predates the wizard by several centuries.

At the other end: the merger names

The longest commune name in France belongs to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne (Champagne region), population 491. Forty-five characters. It reads less like a place name than a legal merger agreement — which is, in fact, what it is.

Most of these marathon names are communes nouvelles — administrative fusions of two or three villages that kept all their historical names joined by hyphens, producing compounds that defeat road-sign designers. France has been merging communes since the 1971 Marcellin law, with a fresh wave after the 2015 loi NOTRe. The names accumulate like sediment.

The shape of a commune name

The histogram has two personalities. Short names — one to eight characters — form a clean rising curve peaking at seven or eight letters, the natural length of a place name across most of Western Europe. Then, where you'd expect a decline, there's an unexpected plateau stretching from 15 to 22 characters and beyond. That's the saint effect.

The masculine Saint- outnumbers the feminine Sainte- by more than 12 to 1 — 3,601 Saint- communes versus 297 Sainte- communes. The theological logic is murky; the geographical pattern is not. Saint-naming clusters heavily in Bretagne, Normandie, and the Atlantic coast — regions where Catholic parish identity ran deep and where a village's identity was inseparable from its patron saint.

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — which appears 12 times across 10 different départements, from Hautes-Alpes to Yonne. There is no dominant Sainte-Colombe; they are all equally real, all equally small, each one insisting on its distinction from the other eleven.

Names as deep map

152 communes begin with La Chapelle-. Each one marks a place where a chapel once stood — or still stands — that was large enough to organize the community but not large enough to be a church. The chapels are often gone; the names remain as the last coordinate of a vanished religious infrastructure.

The names also carry the old landscape: Fontaine (spring), Mont (hill), Pont (bridge), Vaux (valley), Bois (wood). These are not metaphors. They are literal. If you drive to a commune called Fontaine-Quelquechose, there is, or was, a spring. The name is a geological annotation on the land, pressed flat and compressed into civic identity over a thousand years.

At one end of the spectrum: Y. At the other: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Between them: 35,012 communes, every one of them carrying its history in its name.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the INSEE APL dataset (DREES 2023), which covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories. The analysis uses communeName fields as recorded by DREES; a small number of communes with missing names (~170) were excluded. Name-length counts and frequency rankings reflect the dataset as downloaded in April 2026.
