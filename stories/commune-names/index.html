What's in a name?

From Y (population 89) to a 45-letter hyphenated epic — the full atlas of France's 35,014 commune names.

2026-05-28 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes. They range in population from Paris (2.1 million) to a handful of depopulated communes nouvelles in the Alps, and they range in name from a single letter to a four-word hyphenated compound longer than most Twitter handles.

The names themselves are a census of French history: Latin saints, Germanic hill-chiefs, Gaulish rivers, and the occasional clerical accident that stuck.

Most names live in the 6–12 character range

The median commune name is about 10 characters. Short names (under 4 letters) are rare — they tend to be survivals of pre-Romance languages, where place-names were terse and phonetically simple. Long names (over 25 letters) are almost always merger artefacts: two or three communes joined administratively, their names stitched together with hyphens rather than discarded.

One in nine communes is a saint

3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — have names beginning with Saint- or Sainte-. They're not all the same saint. There are 9 Saint-Michels, 10 Saint-Aubins, and 12 Sainte-Colombes: the same hagiographic vocabulary, applied independently in twelve different regions by twelve different village priests in twelve different centuries.

The most duplicated single name in France is Sainte-Colombe, shared by 12 communes from Ain to Gironde. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. The top 15 most common names below are almost entirely saints.

Most common commune names

10.6% of communes share their name

There are 32,764 distinct commune names across 35,014 communes. That means 2,250 names are used more than once — and 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. If you receive a letter addressed to "Saint-Aubin, France," it could plausibly land in any of 10 different towns. The INSEE commune code (a 5-digit number) is the real address; the name is decoration.

The duplication is not random. It clusters around generic geography words: Beaumont (beautiful hill), Beaulieu (beautiful place), Le Pin (the pine), Verrières (glassworks). These were not copied from each other — they were coined independently, in different regions, to describe the same landscape feature.

The thirteen two-letter communes

Below Y, the next tier is two letters: 13 communes whose names survived millennia of administrative churn in their minimal form. Most are phonetically opaque to modern French — Gy, Bû, Oô — remnants of Old Norse, Frankish, or pre-Latin roots. Eu in Seine-Maritime is the largest, with 6,591 residents; Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées the smallest, with 36.

Old names, new codes

Most of these names predate the Ancien Régime. The 35,014 communes were formalised by the National Assembly in 1789–1790, often by simply codifying whatever name was already in use. A handful were renamed after the Revolution (saints replaced by revolutionary slogans), but most reverted within a generation. Today, Saint-Remy, Saint-Aubin, and Sainte-Colombe are back — multiplied across the hexagone, each one a small argument for the durability of names over ideology.

And Y is still Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes) joined with the Etalab administrative-boundary GeoJSON (2025 vintage, 35,014 entries including overseas territories). Name analysis performed on the merged dataset of 35,014 features. Centroids are computed as polygon centroid averages and stored in communes-index.json.
