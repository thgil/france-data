What's in a name? All 35,014 of them.

France has a commune called Y. Twelve communes called Sainte-Colombe. One in eight communes carries a saint's name. The naming logic of a country, compressed into 35,014 data points.

2026-05-14 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, about 25 kilometres south of Amiens, called Y. One letter. Official. On road signs, on INSEE records, on tax bills sent to its 89 residents. It is the shortest place name in France, and probably one of the shortest in the world. Y's existence is not a typo. It derives from Old Picard i, meaning water — the same root that gave us eau, rerouted through centuries of phonetic drift until the spelling became a single vowel standing alone.

At the other end of the alphabet, the commune of Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône runs to 45 characters — a four-commune merger whose hyphenated name reads like a legal contract. Between Y and that 45-character behemoth lie the 35,014 commune names of France.

The same name, twelve times over

France's 35,014 communes share only 32,764 distinct names. That means 2,250 names are used by more than one commune — spread across different départements, often with no awareness of each other. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times: in Ardèche, Côte-d'Or, Gironde, Haute-Garonne, and eight other départements. When you post a letter to Sainte-Colombe, you need the postcode.

The pattern is immediately legible: saints lead, then generic landscape words — Beaumont (beautiful hill), Beaulieu (beautiful place), Le Pin (the pine). France named its villages the way a child names things: simply, repeatedly, and with affection for the obvious.

4,437 communes — 12.7% of all French communes — carry a saint's name. That's roughly one for every 15 km² of French territory. The saints aren't evenly distributed: the south-west and the Atlantic coast concentrate them most thickly, a legacy of medieval parish organisation that carved the countryside into canonised plots. Northern France, with its denser network of older Frankish and Roman place names, leans more on landscape words: Mont, Bois, Villiers, Val.

The saint prefix is also the engine of duplication. Of the 20 most-duplicated names, 14 begin with Saint or Sainte. The patron-saint naming system was practical — every village needed a church, every church needed a patron — and completely indifferent to uniqueness. There was presumably never a moment at which someone said: "perhaps we should check whether there's already a Saint-Aubin."

The letter that wins

S starts more commune names than any other letter: 5,598 of them, or about 16% of the total. This is almost entirely the saints. Remove Saint/Sainte and S falls to somewhere around the middle of the pack. The second most common first letter is L (4,279 communes), powered by the article names — Le, La, Les, L' — plus place-name roots like Longwy or Lons. C ranks third (3,696), fed by Château-, Champ-, Cours-, and the Latin-rooted cluster of names that spread during Romanisation.

The minimal communes

Y has company. There are 13 two-letter communes: By (Seine-et-Marne), Eu (Seine-Maritime), Gy (Haute-Saône), Oz (Isère), Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales), and eight others — all legible traces of Picard, Gascon, Breton, or Occitan roots compressed by centuries of administrative transcription. They're not abbreviations. They're what happened when Old French consonant clusters eroded and vowels were all that remained.

The place name is the oldest data we have. It precedes the census, the cadastre, the satellite image. It records what the first settlers saw — a pine tree, a stream, a hospitable saint — and holds that observation in amber, compressing centuries of geography, religion, and linguistic drift into a word a postman still reads every day.

Y, Sainte-Colombe, Saint-Aubin, Le Pin. The same logic, twelve times over. A country naming itself, over and over again.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 commune dataset, which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Names are the official INSEE commune names as of 2023. The 35,014 figure includes some communes whose official name spans multiple hyphenated components (merged communes). Length statistics use the full official name string.
