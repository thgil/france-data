What's in a French commune name?

35,014 communes. One letter. 4,070 saints. The shortest, the longest, and why a dozen different villages all chose the same name.

2026-05-26 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme — département 80, between Amiens and the Channel coast — there is a village called Y. Just the letter. One character. 89 residents. It is the shortest commune name in France, and possibly the shortest in the world.

Y doesn't explain itself. It was "Y" in the Middle Ages and it is Y today. Administratively it is commune code 80829, and its official APL score for GP access (2.78) suggests it is adequately served, if not spectacularly. It is an ordinary village that happens to be named with a single glyph from the alphabet.

France has 35,014 officially recognised communes. Their names span from Y (1 character) to a 45-character ribbon of hyphenated saints in the Marne. Between those extremes lies a naming culture that is deeply Catholic, slightly redundant, and — once you look closely — quietly strange.

One name in eight belongs to a saint

The most common word in French commune names is not a word about geography. It's a word about religion. Saint appears in 4,070 commune names — one in every 8.6. If you pick a random commune from the list, there is a better than 11% chance it is dedicated to a Christian saint.

3,882 communes begin with "Saint-" or "Sainte-" — that's 11% of France named after a male or female saint. Their combined population is about 6.5 million. More people live in communes named after saints than live in the Paris metropolitan area's inner suburbs.

The most common first name attached to a saint? Martin. There are 250 communes with "Saint-Martin" somewhere in their name. Then Jean (170 occurrences across all compounds), Pierre (180), Laurent (86), Julien (88).

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

If one saint per commune isn't confusing enough, consider Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them. Scattered across the Charente-Maritime, the Doubs, the Gironde, the Ille-et-Vilaine, the Landes, the Lot, the Manche, the Rhône, the Seine-Maritime, the Seine-et-Marne, and the Yonne — twelve separate villages, twelve administrative codes, one name. Their combined population is about 6,600.

Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin appears 10 times. Beaulieu — not even a religious name, just "beautiful place" — appears 10 times independently. France ran out of creativity in some regions and settled for reuse.

On rivers, under mountains, beside other villages

French commune names are full of small prepositions that place a village in its landscape. -sur- is the most common: 1,991 communes sit "on" something — usually a river, occasionally a road. There is a Saint-Martin-sur-le-Pré and a Saint-Martin-sur-Nohain and a Saint-Martin-sur-Oust.

-de- appears in 1,145 names, expressing possession or origin. -en- in 974, locating the village inside a region or pays. -lès- (or -les-) in 463 — a now-archaic French preposition meaning "near," as in Boulogne-sur-Mer's neighbour Condette-lès-Boulogne. -sous- (under) in 257 — usually under a castle or a mountain ridge.

Together these connectives encode a kind of folk geography. The name tells you the village sits by water, or in a valley, or beside a fortified hill. It's a positioning system that predates GPS by several centuries.

The very short ones

Y is alone at one character. But it has company at two: fourteen communes have two-letter names. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the largest — a Norman town with a proper château and a Château-Musée. Gy (Haute-Saône, pop. 1,001) is a small town in Burgundy. The rest are genuinely small: Sy (Ardennes, 54 residents), By (Doubs, 86), Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, 36).

Some of the two-letter names carry accented characters: Oô (Haute-Garonne, 103 residents) has a circumflex that gives it an unusually formal appearance for such a small village. Bû (Eure-et-Loir, pop. 2,022) is the largest accented two-letter commune.

The shape of French commune names

The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. The distribution peaks around 9–13 characters — the sweet spot of names like "Saint-Marcel" or "Montpellier." Names shorter than 5 characters are genuinely rare (888 communes). Names longer than 25 are curiosities, almost always the result of municipal mergers that concatenated multiple names into one administrative string.

45 characters

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · population 491 · 45 characters

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne. Forty-five characters. It is the product of a municipal merger — three communes (Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson) fused into one administrative unit while preserving all three names. Population: 491 people.

It narrowly edges out Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône (43 characters) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados (41 characters). All three are merger products — France's way of combining villages while honouring the historical record.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the INSEE/DREES APL 2023 dataset, which contains 35,014 communes with names, departmental codes, and population figures (base INSEE 2021). Name analysis was performed directly on the communeName field of that dataset. Minor encoding differences (accented vs. unaccented variants) were preserved as-is.
