The shortest name in France

Somewhere in the Somme, département 80, there is a commune whose full official name is a single letter:

Y

Population: 89. INSEE code: 80829.

It is not an abbreviation. It is not a typographical error in the database. Y is one of the oldest place names in France, derived from the Old French word for water — the same root as eau. The commune sits on the Somme river and has been called Y for at least 800 years. It is officially the shortest commune name in the French Republic.

Y does not stand alone for long. Thirteen communes have two-letter names, and the list reads like a random string: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz. Every one of them is a real commune. Most are small. Bû (Eure-et-Loir), population 2,022, is the largest.

One in nine is a saint

"3,898 of France's communes — 11.1% — bear the name of a saint."

France is officially a secular republic. Its commune names are not. Of the 35,014 communes in the country, 3,898 — roughly one in nine — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. This is not a rounding error or a data artefact. It is the accumulated weight of medieval Christianity pressed into official administrative geography.

The saint names are not evenly distributed. The Vendée, Manche, and Mayenne — the old Catholic heartland of western France — have the highest densities. The Var and Hérault in the south have fewer. But no région is without its saints.

Which saint appears most often? Not Francis, not Louis, not Jeanne d'Arc. It is Martin — Saint Martin of Tours, the soldier who cut his cloak in half for a beggar. There are 203 communes named Saint-Martin (in various compound forms). Jean comes second (155), Pierre third (144).

The feminine saints cluster differently. Sainte-Marie (36 communes) leads, followed by Sainte-Colombe (12) and Sainte-Croix (11). The distribution is steeper than the masculine list — sainthood for women was rarer, and that rarity shows up in the geography of French names.

The duplicate problem

Of France's 35,014 commune names, 32,764 are unique. The remaining 2,250 names are shared — there are 1,450 distinct names that appear in two or more places. In total, 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other French commune.

At the top of this list: Sainte-Colombe. Twelve separate communes carry this name, scattered from Gers to Côtes-d'Armor to Haute-Savoie. They have no particular connection to each other beyond a shared medieval patron saint. If you address a letter to "Sainte-Colombe, France", the French postal system has to guess which one you mean.

Most duplicated commune names

The pattern is predictable: saint names dominate the duplicate list. They were chosen independently, in parishes across France, each one naming itself after the same revered figure without knowledge of the others. In the absence of a centralised naming authority, the saints multiplied. The rationalisation never came — the French Revolution renamed plenty of streets, but commune names mostly survived.

The 45-character commune

At the other extreme from Y sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne (department 51) with 45 characters in its official name — more than this sentence has words.

It was created by the merger of several medieval villages: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson. The merger happened but no one simplified the name. The result is a seven-hyphen administrative monument that requires its own line on any official form.

The long-name list is dominated by the same process: post-Revolutionary or 20th-century mergers that concatenated historic village names without abbreviation. The Haute-Saône commune of Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters) was similarly assembled from four separate localities. The longer the name, the more villages were involved — and the less anyone could agree on a short form.

35,014 accidents of history

France's commune names are not a designed system. They are sediment — medieval parishes, revolutionary enthusiasm, merger paperwork, the inertia of bureaucracy, and the stubbornness of local identity, all compressed into official strings of text. The result is Y living alongside Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, twelve Sainte-Colombes ignoring each other across 700 km, and 3,898 saints keeping the old faith alive in the administrative record.

Every commune name is a small argument that the past is still present — just filed under a different code.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to administrative contours (DS-APL, DS-CONTOURS), covering 35,014 communes including overseas territories. The communes-index.json file built here — 35,014 entries with name, INSEE code, département, population, and centroid — serves as the shared commune reference for future stories. Name length counts and duplicate analysis were computed from this index.
