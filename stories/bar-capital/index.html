The bar capital

49,385 bars, a population count, and a completely different map. Which commune really drinks the most — per resident?

2026-05-25 4 min read

The previous story mapped every débit de boissons in France — 49,385 bars, placed at their real addresses. By raw count, Nord and Pas-de-Calais together edged out Paris. Finistère punched above its weight. The familiar diagonale du vide showed up, again.

But count is a lousy proxy for density. Paris has 3,120 bars and 2.1 million residents. A commune in the Hautes-Alpes with 51 bars and 700 permanent residents is playing an entirely different game. So: what happens when we divide by population?

First: 23,000 dry communes

Before asking who drinks the most, note how many communes drink nothing at all. Of 35,014 communes with population data, 23,265 — 66 per cent — have no registered bar. That's not a quirk. It reflects a genuine geography: small rural communes, the central plain, the outer suburbs of big cities. A village of 200 people in Creuse may have had a café du commerce fifty years ago. Today it almost certainly doesn't.

The largest bar deserts are suburban new towns: Villefontaine (Isère, 19,000 residents) has no registered bar. Fontenay-le-Fleury (Yvelines, 13,000 residents), same. Built in the 1970s for cars and apartments, not cafés.

The top of the list is a ski resort

Among communes with at least 500 permanent residents, the most bar-dense places in France are overwhelmingly ski resorts: Les Deux Alpes (Isère), Morzine (Haute-Savoie), Orcières, Risoul, Châtel. Twenty bars per thousand residents — one bar for every fifty people. But those "residents" are the year-round population, a fraction of the winter peak. The licences stay open twelve months; the skiers fill them four.

Corsica leads. The Alps follow.

At département level the picture sharpens. Haute-Corse (2B) has 2.11 bars per thousand residents — the highest in metropolitan France. Corse-du-Sud (2A) ranks third. Corsica's café culture, its year-round tourist economy, and its deeply embedded village square tradition put it clear of the mainland. After Corsica: Lozère (1.76, the least-populated département in France), Cantal, and the Hautes-Alpes — remote mountain counties where the village bar is still the only game in town.

At the opposite end: Territoire de Belfort (65 bars, 143,000 residents, 0.45 per thousand). Essonne. Seine-et-Marne. The suburban ring — massive population, attenuated café culture, the same pattern as the suburban bar deserts at commune level.

Paris: the 1st is another country

Paris as a whole sits at 1.46 bars per thousand — good, but not exceptional. Within Paris, the variation is dramatic. The 1er arrondissement has 123 bars for 15,919 residents: 7.73 per thousand, one bar for every 130 people. The Louvre, Les Halles, tourist bars open at noon. The 15e, by contrast — 148 bars, 228,000 residents, 0.65 per thousand — is a residential arrondissement that empties out at 9 pm. Same city, eleven-fold difference.

The per-capita map doesn't replace the count map — it complicates it. Raw count shows infrastructure: where bars exist, where licences cluster, the industrial density of the north and the coastal rim. Per capita shows culture and accident: ski-season inflation, tourist-town economics, Corsican stubbornness, the 1970s town-planner's vision of a car-friendly suburb with no need for a zinc. Both are true. The same 49,385 bars, two completely different Frances.

A note on the data. Bar counts come from the same INSEE SIRENE dataset used in Story 4 — active débits de boissons (NAF 56.30Z) with valid geocoded coordinates, March 2026. Population figures are INSEE 2021 census figures attached to DREES APL 2023 commune-level data, the same source used in Story 2. Communes with no population entry in that dataset are excluded. Per-capita figures for the commune ranking use a 500-resident minimum to exclude micro-communes with 1–2 bars and inflated ratios. Overseas communes (971–976) are excluded from the département per-capita ranking. Paris arrondissements are treated as separate communes in both datasets, consistent with their INSEE codes (75101–75120).
