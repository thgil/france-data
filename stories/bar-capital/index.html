France's bar capital is a ski village

When you divide 49,385 bars by the residents who actually live there year-round, the league table looks nothing like you'd expect.

2026-05-15 5 min read

If you count by raw numbers, the rankings are about what you'd imagine. Lille has 444 active bars — the most of any single commune. Paris's 11th arrondissement has 386, Nice has 364, Bordeaux and Nantes tie at 341. Big cities, famous for nightlife: the leaderboard confirms the story you already believed.

Now divide by population. Among the 11,201 French communes with at least 200 permanent residents and a matched bar count, the #1 commune has 205 bars per 10,000 residents. It is not Lille (18.8). It is not Paris (the best arrondissement, the 1er, reaches 77.3). It is Gavarnie-Gèdre, a Pyrenean village of 341 souls at the foot of the Cirque de Gavarnie, with seven registered bars — one for every 49 permanent residents.

The mountain takeover

The chart below shows the top 30 communes by bars per 10,000 permanent residents. The colours tell the story immediately.

Of the top 10 communes, eight are ski resorts or mountain tourist villages — five of them in the Hautes-Alpes alone (Puy-Saint-Vincent, Montgenèvre, Orcières, Risoul, and Vars). Morzine in Haute-Savoie has 51 registered bars for 2,660 permanent residents. Les Deux Alpes, the Isère ski station, has 39 bars for under 2,000 people. The pattern is not a coincidence.

Bars for tourists, counted against locals

The explanation is structural. SIRENE — the national business registry — records the address of each establishment and the permanent population of each commune. A ski resort like Les Deux Alpes might welcome 100,000 visitors a season, but its commune's census population remains under 2,000. The denominator stays small; the bar count grows to serve the tourist peak. The licence stays registered year-round.

The same logic applies offshore. The fifth-ranked commune is Île-d'Houat — a Breton island in the Morbihan, accessible only by ferry, with 216 permanent residents and 4 bars. No ski slopes, no Alpine huts. Just an island economy that fills in summer and empties in winter, with licences that persist through January.

Gavarnie-Gèdre has roughly 11× more bars per capita than Lille, which itself leads France's major cities. The mountain distortion is not a rounding error — it's a feature of how France's tourism economy is distributed into very small communes with very permanent bar licences.

How the cities actually rank

Among France's major cities and Paris arrondissements, the picture is more nuanced. Rouen leads at 20.8 per 10,000, narrowly ahead of Lille (18.8). Grenoble (16.1) and Saint-Étienne (15.0) punch above their weight — both are post-industrial cities with dense working-class neighbourhood café cultures. Toulouse, France's fourth-largest city by population, is surprisingly low at 6.4 per 10,000.

Within Paris, the 1st arrondissement stands alone at 77.3 per 10,000 — nearly the rate of an Alpine ski resort. But the 1st has a permanent population of only 15,919 and hosts several million visitors a year. The tourist distortion applies here too, just at city scale rather than mountain scale. The 15th arrondissement, by contrast, is a quiet residential neighbourhood with 148 bars for 227,000 residents — a sedate 6.5 per 10,000, below the national median.

What the median tells you

The national median — among communes with at least 200 residents — is 10.8 bars per 10,000. One bar for every 926 permanent residents. A typical French commune, by this measure, has about one café for a village of just under a thousand people. That's the baseline of the French café economy: one place, a few regulars, a terrace in summer.

The raw count story is about density and scale — big cities accumulate bars by weight of numbers. The per-capita story reveals something more interesting: the bars that look wildest relative to population are built not for the people who live there, but for the people passing through. The Pyrenean pilgrim routes, the Alpine descent lines, the Breton ferry landings — places where the traffic is temporary and the licences are permanent.

France, counted carefully, still has one bar per 926 residents. But the bar capital is a ski village, and the runner-up is Les Deux Alpes. The story was always in the denominator.

A note on the data. Bar locations and counts come from INSEE's SIRENE business registry (active establishments, NAF code 56.30Z — débits de boissons), March 2026 snapshot, geocoded via the INSEE SIRENE Géolocalisation file. Population figures are from INSEE's 2021 census as used in the DREES APL dataset (commune-level). The join covers 11,201 communes with at least 200 permanent residents where both a bar count and a population figure were available. This analysis builds on the national bar dataset from Story 4 (Where France drinks).
