What is Y?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another is Oô. And 11% of the rest are named after saints.

2026-05-19 4 min read

Y is a commune in the Somme. Population 89. It sits in flat northern farmland, a few kilometres from Roye, and it has been called Y for longer than France has been France. The name is Gaulish — a derivative of an old word for water. The post office sorts mail to it. The INSEE registers it as commune 80829. It is, by one measure, the least-named place in the republic.

The minimum

Y is not alone. France has 14 communes with names of one or two letters. Each has a story: Eu in Seine-Maritime was a medieval county town where William the Conqueror reportedly met Harold before the invasion of England. Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées is a mountain village with 36 residents. Ry in Seine-Maritime, population 775, is thought to be the model for Flaubert's Yonville in Madame Bovary.

The saints

11.1% of French communes — one in nine — start with Saint or Sainte. That's 3,882 villages consecrated to a patron, spread across the country like a religious census frozen in the twelfth century. The Catholic geography of medieval France is still the administrative map of modern France.

Saint-Martin is the most popular: 202 communes bear his name. If France held elections only among its Saint-Martins, they would elect their own small parliament.

Saint-Martin, bishop of Tours in the fourth century, divided his cloak with a beggar in a famous scene. He is the patron of soldiers, beggars, and innkeepers. His name is also the single most popular commune name in France — 202 communes, spread across dozens of départements, all named for the same man. Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144) follow him.

The copies

2,250 communes share their name with at least one other commune in France. This was tolerable in the era of the horse-drawn cart, when Sainte-Colombe-en-Savoie and Sainte-Colombe-sur-Gand would never need to be distinguished. Today it is a mild administrative nuisance — and a vivid illustration of how independently thousands of local name choices could converge on the same vocabulary of saints and geography.

Sainte-Colombe holds the record: 12 communes in 12 different départements carry exactly that name. They are scattered across Burgundy, the Charente, the Somme, and the Rhône valley. None of them is particularly famous. All of them are, in the national data, ambiguous.

The fusions

At the other extreme from Y, France has communes with names so long they barely fit on a road sign. Most of them are recent creations — rural villages merged by decree under the 2015 loi NOTRe and its predecessors, their compound names preserving both original identities like hyphenated surnames after a wedding.

The longest commune name in France belongs to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne — 45 characters, two former villages stitched together, population 491. The second-longest, Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in the Haute-Saône, is the work of four villages that merged and apparently wanted everyone to know it.

    38.3% of all French communes have a hyphenated name. The hyphen is doing civic work — it compresses centuries of territorial politics into a single character.

    The grammar of place

    Taken together, the 35,014 commune names form an unintentional document. Saints dominate because medieval France was christened by dioceses. Repeated names proliferate because naming was local, not national. Long hyphenated names multiply because merging villages is easier than erasing memory. And Y survives because no bureaucrat ever dared rename it.

    The French state registers, administers, and standardises everything — down to the last débit de boissons licence and APL score. But the names of its 35,014 communes are a reminder of how much history still lives underneath the spreadsheets.
