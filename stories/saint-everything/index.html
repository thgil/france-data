France has a town called Y

35,014 communes, 32,764 unique names — and 3,882 of them start with "Saint." The naming patterns of France, counted.

2026-06-06 4 min read

The commune of Y sits in the Somme département, about 100 kilometres north of Paris. It has 89 residents, a town hall, and the shortest official commune name in France: one letter. Just Y. It is not an abbreviation. It is not a typo on some 18th-century register. Y is its name, traced back to a pre-Latin root meaning a place near water — the river Noye runs nearby. The French post office knows exactly what to do with a letter addressed to Y, 80829.

Knowing that Y exists — and that it coexists with the perfectly ordinary Saint-Sauveur appearing eleven times across the country — says something about how France got its 35,014 commune names. This is that story.

The alphabet end of France

Y is the only one-letter commune in France. But it has company: thirteen communes have two-letter names, from By in Doubs to Uz in the Hautes-Pyrénées. Most of them end in a vowel — Breton, Basque, and Norman toponyms that were already short when written records began. Eu in Seine-Maritime is the largest, with 6,591 residents; Sy in the Ardennes the smallest, at 54.

Saint-everything

One in nine French communes — 11.1% exactly — carries the name of a Christian saint. That's 3,882 communes: 3,587 starting with Saint- and 295 with Sainte-. The phenomenon isn't random. Medieval France was organised around parishes, each anchored to its patron saint, and when the Revolution created the commune as an administrative unit in 1789–1792, it mostly codified what was already there on the maps. You got to keep your saint.

"One in nine French communes carries the name of a Christian saint — a naming convention set by the parishes of the ancien régime and preserved, almost entirely, by the Revolution."

The most common saint name? Saint-Sauveur — the Holy Saviour — appears eleven times, in eleven different départements, none of them adjacent. Saint-Aubin (a Normand corruption of Saint Albinus, a 6th-century bishop of Angers) appears ten times. And then there's Sainte-Colombe, the feminine saint most likely to be confused with another commune of the same name: twelve different Sainte-Colombes, spread from Ain to Yvelines.

The 2,250 communes with a twin

The Saint names are only part of the duplication story. Overall, 3,700 communes share their exact name with at least one other commune — meaning 2,250 distinct names are used by more than one place. The most duplicated secular name is Beaulieu ("beautiful place"), which appears ten times. Le Pin (nine times), Beaumont (eight times), Verrières (eight times) — the pattern is medieval and geographical: desirable features of a landscape got named the same way everywhere.

How long is a commune name?

The typical French commune name is 7 or 8 characters — the distribution peaks sharply there and then spreads out in a long right tail of hyphenated compound names. At one extreme, Y. At the other: the Breton commune of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne, which runs to 45 characters and exists because three hamlets refused to fully merge in 1972. French administrative history is long and the names remember everything.

What the names remember

A commune's name is a compressed history: its saint (the medieval parish), its landscape (Beaumont, Beaulieu, Le Pin), its feudal lord (Montfort, Villers, Pontoise), its language of origin (Breton Ry and Gy, Basque Ur and Uz, Norse Eu). The 1789 revolutionaries who invented the commune system considered renaming every settlement in France after rivers, mountains, or republican virtues — the kind of clean-slate logic that gives you streets called Rue de la Liberté in every town. They mostly didn't. The saints stayed. Y stayed.

35,014 communes. 3,882 saints. One letter. The names are the country's longest-running data set.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which lists all 35,014 French communes (including overseas territories) with their INSEE codes and names. Name length includes all characters, including hyphens, spaces, and diacritics. The Revolution-era commune count is from the 1793 administrative register; the current total reflects post-2016 commune mergers. The longest name (Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters) is cross-checked against the INSEE Code officiel géographique.
