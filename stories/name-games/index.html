What's in a Name?

35,014 communes. 32,764 distinct names. One is just the letter Y.

2026-05-19 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — the most of any country in the European Union, by a large margin. They range from Paris (2.1 million people) to hamlets with a handful of residents. But what do you call them all? It turns out: sometimes the same thing.

Of those 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have distinct names. The other 3,700 share a name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. If you say you're from Beaulieu, you might mean any of ten different places. From Saint-Sauveur — any of eleven.

The shortest name in France

At the extreme short end, France has a commune with one letter for a name.

Y
Somme (Dept. 80) · 89 residents

Y is a real commune in the Somme département, 130 km north of Paris. Population: 89. It has a church, a town hall, and presumably residents who have to explain their address on every form they fill out. Its postal code is 80190. The name probably derives from a Gaulish or Latin root — linguists have been arguing about it for decades.

Below Y, there are thirteen communes with two-letter names — all of them real places, all of them apparently happy with the arrangement.

The communion of saints

The most striking pattern in French commune names isn't the short ones — it's the Saint- ones. 3,587 communes start with Saint-, and another 295 with Sainte-. Together, 3,882 communes — 11.1% of the entire country — are named after a saint.

The most common single commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, appearing 12 times across 12 different départements: from Charente to Yonne, from Corrèze to Seine-et-Marne. They share a patron saint and not much else. The one in Rhône (population 1,953) is a different Sainte-Colombe from the one in Landes (622) or the one in Haute-Garonne (59).

Another 2,140 communes start with the definite article: Le, La, or Les (6.1% of all communes). France genuinely has a Le Pin in nine different départements. In French grammar, you travel au Pin, à la Roche, aux Bruyères — the geography speaks in perfect agreement.

The longest name in France

If one letter is the floor, what's the ceiling? The longest official commune name in France runs to 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (Dept. 51) · 45 characters

It's a merger name — multiple former villages fused into one administrative unit, each adding its name to the hyphenated chain. France went on a wave of commune mergers in the 1970s under the loi Marcellin, creating a generation of names that read like legal documents.

Runner-up: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône, at 43 characters. Third: Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados, at 41. The pattern is consistent — they're all merger names, all hyphens and saints and geography stacked end to end.

Names as history

Commune names are a compressed archive of French history. The saints come from the medieval church; the Le/La/Les prefixes from how people described their landscape in the vernacular; the hyphenated meganames from 1970s administrative reform. The one-letter Y sits in there too, older than all of it, its etymology lost.

Every form you fill out in France asks for a commune de naissance. If you were born in Sainte-Colombe, that's your answer — but the clerk might still ask: which one?

A note on the data. All commune names and statistics are derived from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Commune names are as recorded in the INSEE commune codes (code officiel géographique). The name Y is genuine; so is the 45-character marathon. Populations are INSEE 2021 estimates.
