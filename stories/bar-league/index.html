The Bar League

96 French departments, ranked by bars per resident. Corsica leads. The Paris suburbs trail. A Pyrenean valley town of 341 people wins the commune crown.

May 2026 · 5 min read · Data: INSEE SIRENE March 2026, DREES 2023

France has 49,385 registered bars — but they are not distributed equally. Divide each department's bar count by its population and a precise geography of French drinking culture appears: mountain peaks and Mediterranean islands on one end, the Paris suburbs on the other.

The national average is 7.45 bars per 10,000 residents. That single number hides a factor-of-seven spread between the most and least bar-dense departments in metropolitan France.

Corsica at the top, IDF at the bottom

Haute-Corse leads the nation at 21.1 bars per 10,000 residents — one bar for every 474 people. Its sibling department Corse-du-Sud sits third at 16.9. Corsican café culture runs deep: the village café-tabac is as much community notice board as drinking establishment. Tourism amplifies the count, but the density holds even in the island's remote interior communes.

Second place goes to Lozère — France's least-populated department, 76,000 people spread across the Massif Central highlands, with 17.6 bars per 10,000. Rural Auvergne and the Massif Central consistently rank well above national average. In small communes, one bar can serve the entire village; the per-capita math reflects that social reality.

Paris (75) lands 7th at 14.6 — well above the national average, but not the story. It is the IDF suburbs that anchor the bottom: Essonne last at 3.1, Yvelines and Hauts-de-Seine at 3.3, Val-d'Oise at 3.7. That's a 6.9× gap between Corsica and Essonne — the same geography that appears in the medical desert map, the bakery density map, and seemingly every service-distribution story France has to tell.

The Alps effect

Five of the top-25 communes by bars per capita are in Hautes-Alpes (05). The mechanism is simple: ski stations register permanent residents in the hundreds while serving visitors in the thousands. The SIRENE data counts bars by location; the population denominator counts only who lives there.

Les Deux Alpes in Isère: 39 bars, 1,933 residents → 201 bars per 10,000. Morzine in Haute-Savoie: 51 bars, 2,660 residents → 191 per 10,000. Val d'Isère: 21 bars, 1,580 residents → 132 per 10,000. Courchevel: 30 bars, 2,311 → 129 per 10,000. The pattern is precise enough to use as a proxy map of French ski resorts.

But the top commune on the list isn't in the Alps at all.

A Pyrenean village wins the crown

Gavarnie-Gèdre, in the Hautes-Pyrénées department, has 341 permanent residents and 7 registered bars. That's 205 bars per 10,000 — above every ski resort in the sample. Gavarnie is not a ski station; it's the village at the foot of the Cirque de Gavarnie, a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of France's most-visited natural landmarks. Hikers arrive from spring to autumn, staying in gîtes and eating in small restaurants. The bars stay open year-round. The village is small enough that seven cafés changes the per-capita maths completely.

Just behind it: Île-d'Houat, a tiny island in Morbihan's gulf — 216 residents, 4 bars, 185 per 10,000. The same logic: islanders are year-round, the tourist season brings ten times the daytime population.

23,265 communes with no bar at all

At the other end of the spectrum: out of 35,014 communes in France, 23,265 — two thirds — have no registered bar. The map of bar-less communes overlaps almost exactly with the map of medical deserts, empty schools, and closed post offices. The same diagonale du vide that shows up in every service-access story is the bar desert too.

The bar's disappearance from rural France tracks the decline of the village café-épicerie, which served as grocery, post office, café, and social hub for hamlets too small for individual services. Where population density can no longer support even one establishment, the functions collapse together — and then disappear entirely.
