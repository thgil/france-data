The bread gap

Paris has 8 bakeries per 10,000 residents. Its suburbs have barely 5. And 607 of Île-de-France's 1,300 communes have none at all.

2026-05-25 4 min read

Île-de-France has 6,299 bakeries. That's the same number you've seen on the dot-density map — 7,378 SIRENE establishments, of which 6,299 fall inside the 1,300 communes tracked by INSEE's Base Permanente des Équipements. The question isn't how many there are. It's how evenly they're distributed.

The answer, bluntly, is not very.

Paris (dept. 75) 8.0 bakeries per 10,000 residents
IDF suburbs (depts. 77–95) 4.7 bakeries per 10,000 residents
IDF average 5.3 bakeries per 10,000 residents

Paris proper has 70% more bakeries per capita than the rest of Île-de-France. Cross the périphérique and bread gets measurably scarcer. Not because the suburbs are rural — Hauts-de-Seine alone has 1.6 million residents — but because the spatial pattern of bakeries tracks commercial density and pedestrian footfall, both of which collapse outside the city limits.

The eight départements

Within the suburbs the variation is narrower than you might expect. Seine-Saint-Denis — often cited as France's poorest département — actually leads the outer ring at 5.2 per 10k, nearly matching Seine-et-Marne. Essonne and Val-d'Oise trail the pack at 4.3 and 4.4. The practical floor for a suburban IDF département seems to be around 4–5 bakeries per 10,000, regardless of income levels or population density.

The most bread-rich communes

Within Paris, the arrondissement rankings are unsurprising in their logic but still striking in their numbers. The 1st arrondissement — home to the Louvre, the Palais-Royal, the most expensive office space in France — has 20.8 bakeries per 10,000 residents. That's one bakery for every 481 people. The tourist-heavy central arrondissements (1st, 4th, 8th) dominate because the bakery count is calibrated to daytime population and visitor demand, not just residents.

The first non-Paris commune in the per-capita ranking (among towns of 5,000+ residents) is Enghien-les-Bains in Val-d'Oise at 11.9 — roughly where Paris's 10th arrondissement sits.

Jouy-le-Moutier sits in Val-d'Oise, 30 km northwest of Paris. It has 16,589 residents and one registered bakery. That's a per-capita rate of 0.6 per 10,000 — one baker for every 16,589 people. No other IDF commune with a population above 5,000 comes close. The runner-up, Méry-sur-Oise (9,260 residents, 1 bakery), still manages 1.1 per 10k.

These aren't towns without commercial infrastructure — they're suburbs with car-dependent planning, large residential footprints, and a supermarket where the boulangerie never materialised. The bread is there; it just arrives on a shelf, wrapped in plastic, labelled tradition.

607 communes: zero

607 of 1,300 IDF communes have no registered bakery — 47%

Nearly half of Île-de-France's communes have no bakery at all. Most of these are small rural communes in Seine-et-Marne (301 of its 514 communes have zero), but two have populations over 5,000: Saint-Germain-lès-Arpajon in Essonne (9,338 residents) and Nandy in Seine-et-Marne (5,814 residents). Both are périurban commuter towns with no registered boulangerie.

Alongside these are 301 communes in Seine-et-Marne alone with no bakery — more than half of the département's 514 communes. At one end, the village with 80 residents and a weekly market. At the other, a town of 9,000 where bread means a drive to a retail park.

The pattern behind the pattern

The bread gap is a proxy for something larger. Bakeries require pedestrian density — foot traffic that justifies a 4 AM start. They require commercial rent levels that aren't astronomical (central Paris defies this, but the 8th arrondissement baker also sells to 50,000 daily commuters). They require a critical mass of residents who walk past the shop on the way somewhere else.

These conditions exist inside Paris and collapse in the suburbs. The same pattern shows up in pharmacies, in doctors (Story 2), in bars (Story 4). Every trade that depends on neighbourhood-scale foot traffic maps almost identically. The bread gap isn't about bread. It's about what a city does to the space immediately outside itself.

A note on the data. Bakery counts and population come from INSEE's Base Permanente des Équipements (BPE) for Île-de-France, as republished by Région Île-de-France on data.gouv.fr. The BPE vintage is circa 2014; the population figures are from the 2010 census. Both figures are slightly dated, but the spatial pattern — Paris outpacing its suburbs by a stable 1.7× ratio — is unlikely to have changed materially.
