What France is called

35,014 commune names — from a single letter to 45 characters. An inventory of saints, hyphens, and the village named Y.

2026-05-23 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Just the letter. Population 89, in the Somme département, the shortest legal place name in Europe. It has a mayor, a church, and presumably a very concise mailing address.

On the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, 45 characters, population 491, in the Marne. It contains the word "Saint" twice. There are 44 characters of name for every 11 people. These two communes define the outer boundaries of a spectrum that says a lot about how France names places.

The republic of saints

The most structurally dominant force in French toponymy is the Catholic calendar. 3,882 communes begin with Saint- or Sainte- — 11.1% of the total. Add communes where "Saint" appears later in the name and the figure reaches 4,437. One in eight French communes owes its name to a canonised life.

Saint-Martin leads by a wide margin: 202 communes bear that name in some form — Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-sur-Armançon, Saint-Martin-de-Seignanx and 199 others. Saint-Jean follows at 155, Saint-Pierre at 144. The top three saints account for 501 communes on their own.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The proliferation of saints creates a naming collision problem on a national scale. France currently has 12 communes named Sainte-Colombe — in the Gironde, the Yonne, the Seine-Maritime, the Manche, and eight other départements. They each have their own INSEE code, their own mayor, their own postal address. They have coexisted in administrative ambiguity since the Revolution.

In total, 3,700 communes share their exact name with at least one other. That's 1,450 names in collision. Below them: Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each). The practical solution is appended context — Sainte-Colombe (Lot), Sainte-Colombe (Seine-Maritime) — a parenthetical nation of disambiguation.

The length problem

The average commune name is 11.8 characters. The median is 10. Most names cluster between 6 and 9 characters — the right length for a village name that fits comfortably on a sign, in a letter, in a conversation. But France also has a long-tail problem.

Distribution of commune name lengths

Each bar is one name length in characters. The peak at 7–8 characters is the modal French village name. The secondary plateau at 14–22 characters reflects the Saint-[name]-de-[place] pattern.

38.3% of all commune names contain at least one hyphen. This is not just a quirk — it's a compression strategy. Merged communes, revolutionary renaming, religious qualifiers: the hyphen absorbs them all. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters) was created by merging four villages that collectively refused to let any of their names be dropped.

The one-letter club

Y is not alone in brevity. France has 13 two-letter communes, forming a small alphabet of minimal place names. Most are in the north and east — short names are a linguistic feature of the Germanic and Latin-influenced borderlands. Uz, population 36, in the Hautes-Pyrénées, may be the least-named-per-resident commune in France: 36 people share a name two characters long.

The contrast with the long-name end of the spectrum is total. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson has 45 characters for 491 residents — roughly one character per 11 people. Uz has two characters for 36 residents — roughly one character per 18 people. France manages both.

Names are infrastructure. They appear on every official document, every road sign, every INSEE dataset. France's commune names carry 1,000 years of religious, linguistic, and administrative history in a median of 10 characters. The hyphens are not decorative. The saints are not metaphorical. And Y, in the Somme, is simply called what it's called.

A note on the data. All name analysis uses the APL commune dataset published by DREES (2023 vintage), which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Names are as stored in the INSEE commune code field (communeName) — they reflect the official administrative name at time of publication. Merged communes (communes nouvelles) carry the combined name, which accounts for some of the longer entries.
