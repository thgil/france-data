A village called Y

France has 35,014 communes — and one of them is named Y. A data portrait of 35,000 place names, from the shortest to the longest.

2026-05-21 4 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union — 35,014 at the last count. Most of them have names that stretch several syllables across the tongue. And then there is Y.

Y
Official name Y Département Somme (80) Population 89 Name length 1 character — the shortest in France

Y sits in the Somme département, about 80 km north of Amiens. It has 89 residents, a church, and a name that has confused postal workers and map designers for centuries. The name predates French — it comes from the Picard word for water, the same root as the Yères river. That does not make it less absurd.

These names are almost all of pre-French origin — Gaulish, Latin, Picard, Basque, Occitan. Eu (Seine-Maritime) is named after the Epte river and has 6,591 residents, making it by far the biggest village in this group. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées) is the smallest, with 36. Oô is what it looks like: a Gascon place name pronounced somewhere between "aw" and "oh," sitting at 1,400 metres in the Pyrenees.

Saint nation

11.1%
of French communes begin with Saint or Sainte — roughly 3,900 municipalities

The most common starting word in French commune names is not La, not Le — it is Saint. Nearly 3,600 communes open with Saint-, and another 300 or so with Sainte-. Add La Chapelle (152 communes) and you start to see the shape of medieval France: a landscape organised around parishes, chapels, and patron saints.

That creates an obvious problem: what do you call the next commune dedicated to the same saint? France solved it with hyphens and geography. Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times. There are 11 villages called Saint-Sauveur and 10 called Saint-Aubin. Each one is unique to its residents; to the postal service, they are a disambiguation exercise.

The duplicate problem

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have a unique name. That leaves 2,250 communes — about 6.4% — sharing their name with at least one other. This is not just a postal curiosity. It is why French addresses require a département code: sending a letter to Sainte-Colombe without specifying whether you mean the one in the Ain, the Côtes-d'Armor, or any of the other ten is a gamble.

The shape of a name

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long; the median is 10. Most names cluster between 6 and 14 characters — a comfortable handful of syllables. The distribution has a long right tail: names that run past 20, 25, 30 characters are usually fusion communes created in the 20th century by merging two or three villages administratively while keeping all their old names joined by hyphens.

The other end of the spectrum

The longest commune name in France, at 45 characters, is:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · population 491

It is a fusion commune in the Marne, combining the names of three historic villages. Its runners-up are equally hyphenated, equally Frankenstein: Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 chars, Haute-Saône) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41 chars, Calvados). These names are not accidents — they are the paper trail of administrative mergers carried out, debated, and partially reversed over two centuries.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson: the same country, the same legal category, a difference of 44 characters. France's communes are a very old, very heterogeneous archive — and the names are where the history sits closest to the surface.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level file joined to administrative boundaries — the same dataset used in Story 2 (medical deserts). The file covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. Commune names are the official INSEE communeName field; a small number of communes may carry slightly different names in other official registries.
