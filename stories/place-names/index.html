35,000 names

One commune has a single-letter name. Twelve call themselves Sainte-Colombe. One in nine communes begins with Saint.

2026-06-03 4 min read

In the Somme, not far from Péronne, there is a commune called Y. Not a question. Not an abbreviation. Just the letter Y — the shortest place name in France, and possibly in Europe. Eighty-nine people live there. Mail gets delivered.

At the other end of the spectrum, in the Marne, sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. It took three villages, two hyphens, one et, and a municipal merger to arrive at that name, and the 491 residents have presumably made peace with it.

Between those two extremes lie 35,014 commune names — the full register of French administrative toponymy. Together, they are one of the stranger cultural documents the Republic produces: medieval saints, Norman rivers, Occitan echoes, and the occasional administrative collision.

A long tail

Most commune names are short. The distribution peaks sharply at 7–8 characters — just enough for a decent village name: Cognac, Lourdes, Libourne. But the tail stretches far to the right. Over 1,800 names exceed 20 characters, and a long series of mergers has pushed 128 names past 30.

Below the peak, the names get stranger. There are 14 communes with names of three characters or fewer — including, beyond Y itself, a small catalogue of consonant-cluster puzzles:

Oô (two characters, Haute-Garonne, population 103) is perhaps the most alarming to type. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) is the largest of the two-letter communes, and the only one that functions passably as an English word.

In the name of Saint Martin

3,882 communes — 11.1% of all of them — begin with Saint or Sainte. The pattern reflects not ecclesiastical devotion exactly, but the medieval practice of naming new settlements after the patron saint of the local church. The saint who won this toponymic lottery most decisively is Martin: 202 communes carry his name as a prefix, ranging from plain Saint-Martin (just five of them) to Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados (41 characters).

Jean (155) and Pierre (144) trail Martin but remain dominant. Étienne, André, Laurent — the apostles and early martyrs fill the list predictably. Some names on it have effectively become surnames for dozens of unrelated villages: there are 108 communes beginning with Saint-Germain, and they range from a hamlet in Lozère to a suburb of Paris.

The twelve Sainte-Colombes

32,764 names for 35,014 communes means 2,250 communes share their name with at least one other. In most cases this is a pair: two villages called Beaulieu in adjacent départements that grew up independently. But in the extreme cases it becomes absurd. Sainte-Colombe appears twelve times, spread across twelve différent départements from Gironde to Yonne.

Name Communes

The French postal system handles this through the département prefix on postcodes. The official INSEE commune code adds a five-digit identifier. But the names themselves remain stubbornly identical — a standing argument that nominative diversity was never the organizing principle of the commune system.

France has 35,014 communes. Reading their names aloud, at a steady pace, would take longer than driving from Dunkerque to Perpignan. The names are a sediment layer: medieval dedications on top of Roman placenames on top of Gaulish roots, with a layer of administrative mergers on top of everything. Y is at the bottom. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is somehow at the top.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (data.gouv.fr), which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories, using INSEE commune boundaries and 2021 population base. Name lengths are computed from the communeName field as supplied — official spellings including diacritics and hyphens.
