What's in a Name

France has 35,000 communes. Most have a unique name — but 3,700 share one with a village they've never met, somewhere else in the country.

2026-05-27 5 min read

France has 35,014 communes — more than all other EU countries combined. Each was, at some point, a village with its own church, its own market, its own notary who knew every family in the valley. Each got a name. The surprise is how often, two or three hundred kilometres away, another village did too — and chose the same one.

In all, 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other commune in a different département. The duplicates aren't typos or database errors. They're independent places that independently chose to name themselves after the same patron saint, the same geographic feature, the same Frankish overlord. France's administrative map is a palimpsest, and the names show the layers.

The fifteen most common commune names

Which name wins? Sainte-Colombe, with twelve communes spread across twelve départements from Manche to Rhône, from Yonne to Seine-Maritime. They are all small — the largest, near Lyon, has 1,953 residents — and they are all, officially, distinct places. Their saint, Colombe de Sens, was a 3rd-century martyr whose cult spread widely through medieval France. The name followed.

Twelve villages, twelve different France

The twelve Sainte-Colombes span the whole country. They are in Norman bocage, Breton hills, Charentais vineyard country, Bordeaux suburbs, Lot valleys, Pyrénées foothills, Burgundy plains, Haute-Saône farmland, the Marne champagne belt — and the Seine-et-Marne commuter belt, thirty kilometres east of Paris. The same dedication, the same canonical form, twelve utterly different landscapes.

Saint-Martin leads; the army is large

4,437 communes — 12.7% of all of them — are named after a saint. That's a standing army of toponymy. Saint-Martin-* is the undisputed champion with 202 communes, more than any other country on Earth has communes at all. Saint-Martin of Tours, the Roman soldier who cut his cloak in half to share with a beggar, won more parishes than almost any other Christian figure. Saint-Jean comes next (155), then Saint-Pierre (144), Saint-Germain (108).

Most common saint-name prefixes (commune count)

Saints dominate, but not exclusively. La Chapelle-* appears 152 times — every medieval hamlet built around a chapel, still there, still named for the building. Villers-*, from the Latin villare (farmstead), appears 105 times. Villeneuve-* (new town) 70 times. The French commune map reads like a condensed history of Latin and medieval French, with parish dedications stamped on top.

From one letter to forty-five

Not all commune names grew in medieval piety. Some are startlingly short. The shortest commune name in France is Y — one letter, a village in the Somme with 89 residents. The name is genuinely old, probably from a Gaulish root meaning "water" or "place by water," shortened over centuries into a single vowel. It has a post office. It has a mayor. Its address is, literally, Y, 80190.

The longest name — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson at 45 characters — is a merger artifact. France has 358 communes whose names contain the hyphenated -et-, the grammatical trace of a fusion between two formerly independent villages. Most date from the regroupement communal policies of the 1970s, which tried to consolidate small rural communes and mostly failed: many fused villages have since separated again, leaving behind either the hyphenated name or an awkward administrative scar.

The linguistic fingerprints

Names aren't just history — they're geography. In Pyrénées-Orientales (département 66), you find Py, Ur, Err, Eus, Llo — Catalan monosyllables surviving intact inside the French administrative system. Basque Country has Ossès, Ayherre, Ustaritz. Alsace has the Germanic -heim and -hoffen endings. Brittany has Plougastel, Locquirec, Plounéour — the Breton plou- prefix for a parish, from Latin plebs. The Republic uses one language. Its communes remember others.

89.4% of France's communes have a name that appears nowhere else in the country. Each one is a unique fingerprint of whoever built a church there, named a hill, remembered a martyr, or arrived from somewhere with a word in their pocket. The 10.6% that share a name are the exceptions — but they are the most French of all, because they show the reach of a shared cultural memory, laid down over fourteen centuries, across 35,000 villages.

A note on the data. All commune names and population figures are derived from the DREES APL 2023 commune dataset (34,886 communes) joined to the Etalab administrative boundaries file (2025 vintage). The total of 35,014 features includes overseas communes. Name-frequency counts treat the communeName field in the APL GeoJSON as canonical — hyphenated variants count as distinct names. Saint-prefix analysis extracted the first segment after the hyphen.
