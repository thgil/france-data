The naming of France

35,014 communes, one letter, and a republic that still runs on saints.

2026-05-16 4 min read

France is a republic with 35,014 communes. The smallest unit of French administration, the commune is officially named, numbered, coded, and mapped. Every commune has an INSEE code — five digits, unique, immutable. And one of those communes is called Y.

Y is in the Somme (département 80). It has 89 residents. It has been called Y since at least the 12th century, probably from a Gallo-Roman root meaning "water." It is, definitively, the shortest commune name in France — one character, no ambiguity. It is also, by some margin, the most surprising thing you can tell a foreigner about French administrative geography.

A secular republic, saintly by name

France separated church and state in 1905. The law of laïcité is foundational — schools are secular, the state is secular, the republic is secular. And yet, of France's 35,014 communes, 3,891 — 11.1% — carry the name of a saint.

One in nine French communes is named after a saint. Nobody changed them when the republic went secular. Nobody's changing them now.

These names predate the republic by centuries. They were given by the Church, ratified by custom, and baked so deeply into local identity that the 1905 law didn't touch them. Changing a commune's name is a bureaucratic ordeal requiring a decree from the Conseil d'État. The saints remain.

The most popular saint, by a clear margin, is Martin. Saint-Martin and its variants (Saint-Martin-de-Belleville, Saint-Martin-d'Ardèche, Saint-Martin-le-Beau…) appear 202 times. Jean comes second with 155. Pierre has 144.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

If you address a letter to Sainte-Colombe, France has a problem. There are twelve of them — in twelve different départements, each with its own local identity, its own mayor, its own post office. Saint-Sauveur exists eleven times. Saint-Aubin ten times.

The solution is the INSEE code. Every commune's five-digit code is unique: the first two digits are the département number, the last three are the commune number within that département. When systems talk to systems, they use the code. When people talk to people, they add the département: Saint-Aubin (Côte-d'Or). Saint-Aubin (Eure). Saint-Aubin (Jura).

Seven hyphens and a merger

Not all commune names are short. France also has 13,410 communes with at least one hyphen — 38.3% of the total. Most have one or two. But a small number have turned hyphenation into an endurance sport.

The champion is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne (département 51). Forty-five characters. Seven hyphens. It holds the record for the longest commune name in France. To understand why, you need to understand the fusion de communes — the administrative merger process that has been quietly consolidating France's thousands of tiny communes since the 1970s. When two or three villages merge, they often combine their names. Saint-Remy was one village. Bouzemont was another. Saint-Genest-et-Isson was a third. The resulting name carries all of them, joined by hyphens and the word en.

The -sur- construction (1,991 communes) is France's way of locating a place on a river: Châtillon-sur-Seine, Neuilly-sur-Marne, Pont-sur-Yonne. The river becomes part of the address. Similarly, 2,140 communes start with an article — Le, La, Les, or L' — which creates pleasant confusion in alphabetical lists: do you sort by L or by the noun? French administrative databases have an opinion. It varies by system.

The oldest commune names are Gaulish or Latin. The newest are merger artifacts from the 1970s onward. In between: Norman Viking names ending in -bec and -tot, Breton names in -ac and -oc, Alsatian names in -heim and -willer, Basque names that break every French phonological rule. Each region's naming layer is a sediment of conquest, migration, and local stubbornness compressed into a string of characters on an INSEE spreadsheet.

And at the top of that spreadsheet, alone in its cell: Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset — a national commune-level file of 35,014 entries covering metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name statistics were computed from the communeName field of that dataset. The APL dataset is used here purely for its commune list; doctor-access scores do not appear in this story.
