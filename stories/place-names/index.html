What's in a name?

France has a commune called Y. It has 35,013 others. Together, their names are a map of saints, mergers, and medieval imagination.

2026-05-26 4 min read

There is a commune in the Somme, 30 kilometres south-east of Amiens, whose official name is a single letter: Y. Population 89. Postcode 80190. If you need to write the full address, the first line reads Y, and so does part of the third. Its APL score — the measure of local GP access — is 2.78, which means it is not a medical desert, which is more than can be said for many places with longer names.

Most are phonetic relics of Old French, Frankish, or Gallo-Roman place names worn down to a stub by centuries of copying. Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591) preserves the Gaulish word for yew tree — the same root as if in modern French. Oô (Haute-Garonne, pop. 103) sits in the Pyrenees and is pronounced roughly like the English oh-oh. Ry, in Normandy, inspired Flaubert — it is the real village behind the fictional Yonville-l'Abbaye in Madame Bovary.

89.4% of France's communes have a name that appears nowhere else — 31,314 unique names out of 35,014 communes. The remaining 3,700 share their name with at least one other place. This is less a coincidence than a feature: France has an unusually large number of communes (the most of any EU country, by far), and medieval naming conventions recycled the same patron saints and geographical words across every province.

A nation of saints

The single most common word across all 35,014 commune names is saint. It appears 4,090 times. Add sainte (360 times) and you have over 4,450 name fragments that reference a holy figure — before you even count compounds like Saint- prefixed names.

Exactly 3,898 communes — 11.1%, or one in nine — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. They range from tiny hamlets to cities: Saint-Étienne, Saint-Denis, Saint-Nazaire. The most replicated saint is Colombe: there are twelve distinct communes named Sainte-Colombe, spread across twelve different départements from the Hautes-Alpes to the Yonne. From Y to a 45-character merger

The median commune name in France is 10 characters long. The distribution runs from 1 (just Y) to 45, and it peaks around 7–8 characters — long enough for something like Morlaix or Béziers, short enough to fit on a sign. The tail on the right is long: there are 1,095 communes whose names exceed 15 characters.

The names at the extreme right are almost all merger names — communes that fused multiple villages into a single administrative unit in the 1970s under the Marcellin Act, stitching the old names together with a hyphen. The longest name in France belongs to a commune in the Marne:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (dept. 51) · population 491 · 45 characters · two saints, four former villages, one name

It was created by merging four separate villages in 1972. Its name contains two saints. It has 491 residents. At 45 characters, it is twelve times longer than Y, but they sit in the same administrative tier — both are communes, both pay the same bureaucratic taxes, both can theoretically have a mayor. France is large enough to hold them both.

The same map, different layer

Saint names cluster in the west and the south — regions that were Christianised deeply and early, where medieval parishes gave every settlement a patron. The north-east has more Germanic roots: place names ending in -heim, -willer, -bach, traces of Alsatian and Lorrain history. The Basque Country has names from a language that predates every other in Western Europe. And somewhere in the middle, in the Somme, population 89, there is just Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL commune file (2023 vintage), which covers 34,886 metropolitan communes. Overseas-territory communes are included via the same administrative source, bringing the total to 35,014. Name lengths and frequencies are computed from the communeName field in the medical deserts dataset. The APL score for Y (2.78) is from the same source.
