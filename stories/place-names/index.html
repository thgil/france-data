What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. And one name runs to 45 characters.

2026-05-18 4 min read

In the Somme département, an hour north of Paris by car, there is a commune called Y. One letter. The post office delivers there. The road signs spell it. 89 people live there. France's shortest place name is not a quirk or an abbreviation — it is the full official name, derived from a Picard word for a forking river.

On the other end of the scale sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 45 characters in its name. It was formed by the merger of three villages, and kept all three names. The two extremes capture something about France: a country where history accumulates in the landscape, and the map bears the weight of it.

The saint invasion

France has 3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — one in every nine. They are the trace of a Christianisation that gave villages the names of their patron saints starting in the early medieval period, and a post-Revolutionary administrative codification that locked those names in place. Today they are a density pattern on any map of commune names: the south-west, the Massif Central, and the Atlantic façade run deepest.

Saint-Martin leads at 202 communes, trailed by Saint-Jean (155) and Saint-Pierre (144). The saint names broadly track medieval Frankish Christianity: Martin of Tours, the soldier-turned-bishop who became the patron saint of France, wins by a wide margin. Jean and Pierre reflect the universal prestige of Gospel figures. Further down the list, regional saints appear — Denis (patron of Paris), Vincent (wine country), Rémi (Champagne's apostle).

The département with the highest proportion of saint-named communes is Ardèche, where 97 of 335 communes — 29% — carry a Saint prefix. The Creuse (28.2%), Loire (27.8%), and Haute-Vienne (27.2%) follow. These are overwhelmingly rural, ancient Christian territories where the parish network survived the Revolution largely intact.

The duplicate problem

France has 35,014 communes, but only 32,764 unique names — a shortfall of 2,250. That means 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. Send a letter addressed only to "Beaumont" and it might go to any of 8 different communes. Address it to "Sainte-Colombe" and there are 12 possible destinations.

The duplicates are mostly a saint problem: the same patron saint gave his or her name independently to villages in many different regions. But secular names repeat too. Beaulieu appears 10 times, Le Pin nine times, Beaumont eight. These are generic descriptive names — "beautiful place," "the pine tree," "beautiful hill" — that arose naturally wherever the landscape fit the label.

The chapels and the grid

Beyond saints, the most revealing prefix is La Chapelle. There are 152 communes in France whose name begins that way — more than any other geographic descriptor. The Chapelle was the rural church, often the first permanent stone structure in a hamlet, and it gave each settlement its name before the commune system was even invented. Fontaine (58 communes), Mont (51), Val (47) follow — each one a landscape feature so common that it was independently named in dozens of territories.

What this map of names reveals is a landscape settled slowly, named locally, and then frozen in place by two centuries of administrative stability. France reformed its currency, its calendar, its constitution — but it has almost never renamed its communes. The medieval village is still there in the postal address. The geography of 800 AD is still legible in the street sign.

A note on the data. All commune names and codes are drawn from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (35,014 communes), which uses the INSEE commune reference as its backbone. Name analysis was performed on the communeName field. The dataset includes metropolitan France and overseas territories. Commune mergers since 2023 may affect the count slightly.
