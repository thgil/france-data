The Saints of France

11.1% of French communes are named after a saint. Saint-Martin alone claims 202 of them. And then there's Y — one character, 89 residents, somewhere in the Somme.

2026-05-30 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Type them into a list and a pattern immediately emerges: Saint-Pierre, Saint-Martin, Saint-Jean, Sainte-Marie — saint after saint after saint, interspersed with Le Pin, La Roche, Les Essarts. The country's administrative geography is a medieval document that nobody ever revised.

3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — begin with "Saint" or "Sainte." That's one in every nine French addresses starting with a name that most of the residents have never thought about. They're home to 6.5 million people.

Saint-Martin wins, by a lot

When you group saint communes by the saint's name — collapsing Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-d'Auxigny, Saint-Martin-en-Bière, and 199 more into a single count — Saint-Martin dominates. 202 communes bear his name in some form. Saint-Jean follows with 155, Saint-Pierre with 144, Saint-Germain with 108.

Martin of Tours was a Roman soldier turned bishop who founded French Christianity in the 4th century. He may not be the most famous saint — that's probably a three-way tie between Pierre, Jean, and Paul — but he built the network. The French had centuries to name towns after him before the competition arrived.

The duplicate-name problem

The most common exact commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe — 12 communes with the same name, in 12 different départements. Saint-Sauveur comes second with 11. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu tie at 10.

This creates genuine administrative comedy. If you tell a courier you live in "Saint-Aubin," they'll need a département code to sort it from nine other communes with identical names. The French postal system resolves it with the five-digit code postal, but GPS databases still occasionally lose their minds.

Saint, Le, and everything else

Saint-/Sainte- isn't the only pattern. 2,140 communes start with Le, La, or Les — roughly 6% of the total. Together, the two patterns account for about one in six French communes with a structured prefix.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson

At one end of the spectrum: the shortest commune name in France is a single letter. Y. It's in the Somme, département 80, population 89. It's a real place with a real mayor and a real IGN coordinate. It is not a typo.

Eleven other communes make do with two characters: Sy, By, Oô, Oz, Ri, Uz, Py, Ur, Gy, Eu, Ry. Together they form a small club of the administratively minimal, mostly in rural corners of France, mostly with populations under 1,000.

Longest commune name in France

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson — 45 characters, Marne (51), population 189. It contains two saints, a river, and a hyphen budget that would bankrupt most countries.

The same government that runs a European democracy, high-speed rail, and a nuclear deterrent also maintains an official record of a village called Y — and has done so, more or less continuously, since the Carolingians. Both facts are on the same cadastral map.

France's commune names are not optimised for search engines, GPS systems, or courier routing. They are the accumulated choices of a thousand years of local history, preserved in amber. Saint-Martin named 202 of them. Y named itself.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, joined to administrative boundary data (Etalab Contours-IRIS, 2025 vintage). The full dataset covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name analysis was done by counting communes whose communeName field begins with "Saint-" or "Sainte-". The population figure of 6.5M for saint communes uses the same INSEE 2021 population base as the APL dataset.
