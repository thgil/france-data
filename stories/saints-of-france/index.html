One in eight French communes is a saint

4,426 communes named after saints, 12 called Sainte-Colombe, and one named Y. The map of France is, in part, a map of medieval Christianity.

2026-05-26 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme with a population of 89 and a name that is exactly one letter long. It is called Y. It has a mayor, a church, a post code (80190), and a Wikipedia article. It is the shortest commune name in France — possibly the shortest official municipality name in the world. It is not, however, the strangest thing about French commune naming.

The strangest thing is the saints.

France has 35,014 communes. Of those, 4,426 carry a saint's name — either opening with Saint- or Sainte-, or fusing a saint's name into a hyphenated compound like Bonneville-et-Saint-Avit-de-Fumadières. That is 12.6% of the national total. One commune in eight is a saint. The map below shows which ones.

The saint belt

The pattern is not random. Click "Southwest" on the map and you'll see it: an amber tide washing across Dordogne, Ardèche, Creuse, and the Massif Central. These are the départements where medieval parish naming ran deepest and where, after the Revolution's abrupt attempt to rename everything (Saint-Malo briefly became Port-Malo; Saint-Étienne became Armeville), the old names mostly came back.

Creuse tops the list at 30.6%: nearly one in three of its 255 communes is a saint. Ardèche is at 29.3%. Dordogne has the most saint communes in absolute terms — 145, out of 503. When you drive through Périgord and stop counting the Saint- signs, you are experiencing a genuine statistical phenomenon.

Alsace, Corsica, and the secular exceptions

The other end of the map is equally striking. Click "Alsace" on the map: almost no amber. Bas-Rhin has 514 communes and just 7 saint names (1.4%). Haut-Rhin is at 2.5%. This is not coincidence — Alsace-Lorraine was part of the German Empire from 1871 to 1918 and inherited a different administrative and ecclesiastical tradition. French saint-naming conventions, partly shaped by the medieval French Catholic infrastructure and the post-revolutionary renaming process, never fully applied here.

Click "Corsica": even fewer. Corse-du-Sud has 124 communes and one saint name (0.8%). Haute-Corse has 236 communes and just one (0.4%). Corsican communes follow Genoese and Corsican naming traditions — place names rooted in landscape, family, or Corsican dialect rather than the French Catholic calendar. The île de beauté is, nominally, very Catholic; it just doesn't name its villages like it.

Paris has zero saint communes. Its 21 arrondissements use ordinal names. The Hauts-de-Seine, with 36 communes, has one.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

The canonization of French geography produced one further absurdity: there are 12 communes in France called Sainte-Colombe — in Ain, Gironde, Haute-Saône, Ille-et-Vilaine, Loire, Rhône, Saône-et-Loire, Seine-Maritime, Somme, Vaucluse, Vendée, and Yonne. Each one is, officially, a Sainte-Colombe. None is the Sainte-Colombe. (In France, you distinguish them by adding the département in parentheses on official correspondence.)

Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin and Saint-Pierre, 10 times each. The top ten most common commune names in France — all of them saints. France effectively ran out of naming creativity for its villages and solved the problem by repeating the same liturgical calendar across the entire country.

Y is not a saint

Back to Y. The commune in the Somme is, as far as anyone knows, named for the shape of the river Noye at that point — a Y-fork in the water. It has 89 residents, an APL score of 2.78 (not a medical desert), and a certain pride in its brevity. It shares the two-letter club with Sy (Ardennes, 54 people), By (Doubs, 86), Bû (Eure-et-Loir, 2,022), Oô (Haute-Garonne, 103), Oz (Isère, 213), and eight others.

Communes with the shortest names (1–2 letters)

At the other extreme, the longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne — 45 characters. It is, fittingly, named after two saints. Its population is 491.

France is a country of 35,014 communes, ranging in name from one letter to forty-five. In between, it named 4,426 of them after saints, repeated those saints 12 times over in some cases, and assembled the whole into a map that is — if you squint at it correctly — a partial atlas of medieval Christianity laid over a modern republic.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the communeName field in the DREES/Etalab APL commune dataset (34,886 metropolitan + overseas communes), with coverage of 35,014 communes total. A commune is classified as "saint-named" if its name starts with Saint or Sainte, or contains -Saint- or -Sainte- as part of a hyphenated compound. This captures the 145 Dordogne cases correctly but excludes very rare cases where Saint appears at the end of a compound name.
