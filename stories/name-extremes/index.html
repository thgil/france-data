What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. The names between those extremes tell a story of saints, geography, and the strange arithmetic of French place-naming.

May 2026 5 min read Culture

The village of Y sits in the Somme, population 89. Its name has been in use since at least the 10th century — derived from the Old Picard word for a yew tree, if. Over the centuries the spelling contracted, the way place names sometimes do, until it was down to a single letter. This is now the shortest commune name in France.

Y's APL score — the official measure of GP accessibility — is 2.78. Barely above the 2.5 threshold that separates adequate coverage from medical desert. Eighty-nine people, one letter, not quite a desert. France in miniature.

The two-letter club

Beyond Y, there are 13 communes with two-letter names. They are scattered across France with no obvious geographic logic: Eu in Seine-Maritime (population 6,591, the heavyweight of the group), Uz in Hautes-Pyrénées (population 36, the smallest), Oô in Haute-Garonne (population 103, whose name includes a circumflex and therefore technically contains diacritics), and nine others.

Most are ancient. Eu — the largest — was an important Norman city, residence of William the Conqueror's court before 1066. The name comes from the Latin Aqua. Oz in Isère (population 213) is today a ski resort whose name predates French by several centuries.

The other end

At the far extreme is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a commune in the Marne with 45 characters in its official name. It was created in 1972 when four neighbouring villages merged, each insisting on recognition. The result is a name that takes longer to type than to drive through.

The runner-up is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône, at 43 characters — also a merger commune. The pattern holds throughout the longest-name list: almost all of France's unwieldy place names are the product of municipal fusions, where political compromise produced hyphenated compounds that nobody wanted to shorten.

Name length: the full picture

The average commune name is 11.8 characters. The distribution peaks around 10–14 characters — long enough for something like Saint-Martin or Villefranche but short of the merger-driven outliers. The red bar at the left is Y, alone at 1 character.

The twelve Sainte-Colombes

Of the 35,014 commune names, 10.6% are shared by at least one other commune. The most-repeated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times. Twelve separate places, in twelve different corners of France, all named for the same 3rd-century martyr from Sens.

They range from a village of 59 people in the Hautes-Alpes (APL: 0.00 — no GP at all) to a town of 1,953 in the Rhône (APL: 3.31). Combined population: about 6,400. None particularly famous. All sharing a name.

The second-most-common name is Saint-Sauveur (11 communes). Third is Saint-Aubin (10). Spot the pattern. Of the 20 most-shared names, 16 are saints' names.

The saint factory

France has 3,898 communes whose names begin with Saint or Sainte — 11.1% of all communes. That's roughly one in nine. They include 3,392 distinct saint names: the saints are spread thinly, with most appearing only once. The 12 Sainte-Colombes are the exception, not the rule.

One in nine French communes is named after a saint. They span 3,392 distinct saints — most of them appearing on exactly one commune's sign.

The naming of communes after saints reflects a centuries-long process in which parishes became administrative units under the Revolution and then the Napoleonic settlement. The commune as a legal structure was created in 1789, and thousands of villages simply took their existing parish church's dedication as their official name. Saint-Martin alone accounts for the patron saint of France — though in the commune-name data, it appears nine times, not the hundreds that popular references sometimes claim once you account for compound names like Saint-Martin-de-Bellevue or Saint-Martin-sur-Ouanne.

A note on the data

Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes — metropolitan France and overseas territories — as of the 2025 administrative boundaries. A handful of very recent communal fusions are not yet reflected. The 10-character mean covers a range from Y (1) to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45). Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base used in the APL calculations.
