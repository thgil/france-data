What France calls itself

35,014 communes. One is called Y. Another has 45 letters. And 12 villages share the exact same name.

2026-05-27 4 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. One letter. Population 89. It sits in the Somme, département 80, between Amiens and Saint-Quentin, and it has been called Y for as long as the administrative record runs. Nobody is quite sure why. It might be from an old Picard word. It might be a corruption of something longer, worn smooth over centuries until only a vowel was left.

France has 35,014 officially recognised communes. Most of them have ordinary names — Saint-Martin, Bordeaux, Lyon. But the distribution of those names is one of the stranger statistical documents the country has produced: a bell curve with a one-letter outlier on the left and a 45-letter hyphenated monster on the right.

A bell curve, lightly skewed

Most French commune names land between 8 and 14 characters long — Marseille (9), Bordeaux (8), Saint-Malo (10). The median is 10. The distribution has a gentle right tail from all the double-barrelled and hyphenated names, but the core of it is reassuringly normal.

A merger of three historical communes, each keeping its name

The longest names are mostly accidents of French administrative history. When communes merge, they can keep all their constituent names, joined by hyphens. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (45 characters, Marne) fuses three villages into one administrative unit. Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône) does the same. These are not exotic exceptions — they are what happens when France takes seriously the idea that no place should lose its name.

Saint everything

The most striking pattern in the name distribution isn't the extremes — it's the saints. 4,437 French communes, 12.7% of the total, have Saint or Sainte somewhere in their name. More than one commune in eight is dedicated, in some degree, to Catholic martyrdom.

The most common single commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears 12 times. Twelve villages, spread across different régions, each independently dedicated to a third-century Roman noblewoman martyred for refusing to renounce Christianity. The 12 Sainte-Colombes do not form a cluster on the map; they simply share a dedication. Saint-Sauveur exists 11 times. Saint-Aubin 10 times. The top 20 repeated commune names are almost entirely saints.

The saintliness is not evenly distributed. In Creuse (département 23, in the Massif Central), 30.6% of communes — nearly one in three — carry a saint's name. Ardèche (29.3%) and Dordogne (28.8%) follow close behind. These are among France's most rural, most Catholic, most historically settled areas. In Alsace and the Paris région, the proportion falls below 10%.

The remarkable majority

What's more striking than the repeated names is how many aren't. 31,314 of France's 35,014 communes — 89.4% — have a name that exists nowhere else in France. Roquebrun. Mortagne-au-Perche. Maulévrier. Villefranche-de-Rouergue. Each one unique, each name a piece of local history that has survived administrative consolidations, revolutionary renaming drives, and two centuries of the centralising French state.

France tried to abolish saints' names after 1789 — Saint-Denis became Franciade for a few years; Saint-Nicolas-du-Port became Port-sur-Moselle. Most communes went back. The saints stuck. So did Y.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures are from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to INSEE administrative commune boundaries (2025 vintage, 1000m simplification). The dataset covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Name analysis is case-sensitive and searches for the literal strings Saint and Sainte; variants like San, Santo, or Breton Sant are not counted.
