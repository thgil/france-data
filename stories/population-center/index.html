Demographics

Centre of gravity

Weight every French commune by its residents, and the centre of 65 million mainland lives falls in a village of 1,004 in the Cher — 201 km from Paris.

2026-05-11 4 min read

France has 34,791 mainland communes, from Paris (2.1 million residents) to Rochefourchat in the Drôme (one resident, officially). Assign each commune its geographic centroid, weight it by population, and sum. The result — the population centroid, the point that minimises the total travel distance for every French person — falls at 47.06°N, 2.64°E.

The nearest commune to that coordinate is Farges-en-Septaine, a quiet agricultural village of 1,004 people in the Cher département (18), 19 km south-east of Bourges. Most French people have never heard of it. France's geographical soul lives there anyway.

Paris is 201 km away

The usual story about France is that everything flows through Paris — the railways, the roads, the cultural gravity. And Paris does pull: without its 2.1 million residents (and the 12.2 million in Île-de-France), the centroid would sit about 13 km further south and east. The capital tugs the nation's demographic centre of mass slightly north-northwest. But only slightly. At 201 km, Paris is not the centre of France — it is a heavy weight on one end of a long beam.

Bourges, the capital of the Cher, is 19 km from the centroid. The nearest large city is either Paris or Lyon (both about 200 km), and the centroid sits almost exactly between them on a north-south axis. That is not an accident: France's two biggest cities roughly balance each other out in the calculation.

A surprisingly even country

The 18 km offset between the population centroid and the unweighted geographic centroid (the average commune position, regardless of residents) is the most striking number in this calculation. In many countries, the two centroids are hundreds of kilometres apart — because population clusters tightly in one corner of the country while the rest is nearly empty. France is not like that.

This isn't because France is uniformly dense. Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Lille, and Bordeaux all stand out sharply on any population map. But they roughly balance each other across the country's area. The diagonale du vide — the low-density diagonal running from Meuse to the Pyrenees — is countered by population on every side. The result is a country whose demographic centre of gravity stays close to its geographic centre.

The 301 km circle

Draw a circle of 301 km radius around Farges-en-Septaine. Half of mainland France's population lives inside it. That circle reaches Paris to the north, Lyon to the south-east, Nantes to the west, and into Alsace to the east. Widen it to 400 km — four hours by car — and it catches 80% of France.

Share of mainland population within radius of the centroid

The numbers make the sparseness of France's geographic interior legible. At 50 km from the centroid, only 0.6% of French people live — the centre is genuinely rural. At 200 km, still only one in five. The population is not in the centre; it is around it, distributed in a rough ring of cities. That ring, and its centre of gravity, is Farges-en-Septaine.

A note on the method. Commune positions are the arithmetic mean of each commune polygon's outer-ring vertices (a centroid approximation). The population centroid is the population-weighted mean of those positions across 34,791 mainland communes (excluding overseas territories). Population figures are from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which uses an INSEE 2021 population base. The "geographic centroid" computed here is the unweighted mean of all commune positions — not an area-weighted polygon centroid — which explains why it differs from monument-based claims in Bruères-Allichamps or Nassigny. The 301 km half-population radius is computed by sorting communes by distance from the centroid and finding where the cumulative population crosses 50%.
