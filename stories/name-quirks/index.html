What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes — and 3,700 of them share their name with at least one other. One is called Y. One takes 45 characters to say.

2026-05-13 4 min read

The French state maintains 35,014 communes — the smallest administrative units of the Republic, dating in their current form from the 1789 Revolution. Each one is supposed to have a distinct identity. Most do. But 3,700 of them share their official name with at least one other commune somewhere else in the country.

The winner: Sainte-Colombe, twelve times over

The most popular commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them — in twelve different départements, spread from Gironde to Haute-Saône to Seine-et-Marne. Colombe is the French for dove, and Saint Columba was a 6th-century Irish missionary whose name spread across Gaul as Christianity organised itself around local patron saints. The dove is everywhere.

The runners-up are a roll-call of the same logic: Saint-Sauveur (11 communes), Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu (10 each), then a cluster of nine — Saint-Marcel, Saint-Michel, Le Pin, Saint-Pierre, Sainte-Marie. Six of the ten most common names are saints.

One in nine communes is a saint

Among all 35,014 communes, 3,898 — 11.1% — begin with Saint or Sainte. That's 3,392 distinct saint names, from the familiar (Pierre, Jean, Michel) to the deeply obscure. The Catholic calendar runs to a saint for nearly every day of the year, and for centuries, newly christianised villages adopted the nearest martyr or bishop as their patron and built their toponym around it. The result: one in nine French communes is, officially, a saint.

From Y to Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont

At the other end of the spectrum from Sainte-Colombe's repetition: the sheer range of lengths. The average commune name is 11.8 characters. The shortest is a single letter. The longest could fill a tweet on its own.

The shortest commune in France — by name — is Y:

Y Somme (département 80) · population 89 · INSEE code 80829 · 1 letter

Y is a real village in the Somme, roughly halfway between Amiens and Saint-Quentin. Its population is 89. Postal address: Y, 80190. The name likely derives from a Gaulish or Germanic root, not the letter of the alphabet — but the effect is the same. Nobody confuses it with anything else.

Then there are thirteen communes with two-letter names — By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz — before the field opens up into the familiar world of French toponyms: Acq, Aix, Arc, Boe…

At the other extreme, the longest commune name in France is:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (département 51) · population 491 · INSEE code 51513 · 45 characters

This Marne commune's name is the product of the 19th-century fusion policy: when several neighbouring hamlets were administratively merged, their names were concatenated with hyphens rather than discarded. The result is a 45-character, three-part name that contains, almost inevitably, another saint.

The French commune map is, among other things, a sediment of history: Roman roads, Carolingian parishes, Revolutionary reorganisation, 19th-century mergers. Every name is a story. The 12 Sainte-Colombes are all independent communities that happened to pick the same patron dove. Y outlasted the Gaulish tribe that named it. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont carries its whole administrative biography in its hyphenated name. There are 35,014 of these stories — and 3,700 of them are sharing.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset (34,886 metropolitan communes and overseas territories), reused from the medical deserts story. The total of 35,014 features in the GeoJSON includes a small number of arrondissements (Paris, Lyon, Marseille) in addition to communes proper. Name analysis was performed in Python using the communeName field from the APL GeoJSON.
