A commune called Y

France has 34,924 communes. One is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. And one has a 45-character name.

2026-05-16 4 min read

Y is a commune in the Somme, département 80, a few kilometres south of the Oise river. Eighty-nine people live there. The name is not an abbreviation, a typo, or a postal oddity: Y is the full official name, and it has been since at least the twelfth century. The village appears on Cassini's great map of France in the 1750s. It pays taxes, elects a mayor, and appears in the national SIRENE business directory.

Y is the only one-letter commune in France. Thirteen more have exactly two letters.

The northern names — Ry, Gy, By — are partly Germanic and Norse in origin, carried in by Frankish and Viking settlements. Eu, near Dieppe, was once a royal seat: Edward I of England held court there in 1260. The southern names at the foot of the Pyrenees — Ur, Uz, Py, Oô — come from Basque and Gascon substrates, where short vowel-rich roots are common. None of them chose their brevity; they simply retained the oldest layer of the landscape.

Of France's 34,924 named communes, 3,898 — roughly one in nine — begin with "Saint" or "Sainte." The top five departments for saint-named communes are all rural, Catholic, and heavily fragmented into small ancient settlements.

Brittany is especially dense with saints — but Breton saints are often local figures unrecognised by Rome, which is why you find names like Saint-Thégonnec and Saint-Pol-de-Léon that don't appear in the Roman martyrology. The Catholic map of France is embedded, saint by saint, in the commune name register.

The name you share

The most common commune name in France is Sainte-Colombe: twelve separate communes carry it, from the Ardèche to the Gironde. Saint-Sauveur has eleven. Saint-Aubin has ten. These are not duplicates — each refers to a different local saint's cult, a different medieval chapel, a different village that grew up around it without coordinating with the others.

Across all 34,924 communes, 1,450 names are used by more than one commune, accounting for 3,700 communes in total — more than 10% of the country. If a letter arrives addressed to "Sainte-Colombe," twelve postmasters may briefly wonder.

From two letters to forty-five

Most commune names are 6–12 characters long. That's where the distribution peaks — long enough to be a real place name, short enough to fit a road sign.

But at the far end, 490 communes have names longer than 25 characters. Most of these are the product of administrative mergers.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters · Marne · population 491

This is France's longest commune name. It was created in 1973 when three adjacent villages in the Marne merged under the Marcellin Law, which offered financial incentives for communes to consolidate. Each village insisted its name appear in the combined result. The outcome is a 45-character mouthful that would exceed the character limit of many web forms.

Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur
43 characters · Haute-Saône · population 923

The second-longest follows the same logic: four constituencies, one merger, every village clutching a syllable of identity. The Marcellin Law produced relatively few mergers overall — French communes pushed back fiercely — but it did produce some remarkable names among those that complied.

Somewhere between Y (89 residents, one letter) and Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson (491 residents, 45 letters) lies the full range of how France has named its 34,924 pieces of itself: saints and rivers, Frankish roots and Pyrenean syllables, forced mergers and ancient stubbornness. Every name is a decision someone made a very long time ago, and France has been living with it ever since.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to Etalab contours administratifs 2025, both already in the repository from the medical deserts story. The 34,924 figure excludes a small number of French overseas collectivities (French Polynesia, New Caledonia, Wallis-et-Futuna) that carry department code 98 in the source. Overseas departments (Guadeloupe, Martinique, Guyane, La Réunion, Mayotte) are included.
