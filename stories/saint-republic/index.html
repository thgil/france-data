The Republic of Saints

France has 35,000 communes, one named Y, and 3,898 that begin with Saint. A tour of the strangest address book in Europe.

2026-05-24 4 min read

There is a commune in France called Y. Not Gy. Not Ry. Just Y — one letter, pronounced like the French word i. Population: 89. Département: Somme. APL score: 2.78 (adequate, as it happens). It has been there since the Middle Ages, and it remains the shortest place name in France and one of the shortest in the world.

Y is not quite alone at the bottom of the alphabet. Thirteen French communes have two-letter names: Eu in Seine-Maritime (a medieval city with a real château), Gy in Haute-Saône, Oz in Isère, Oô in Haute-Garonne. The pattern suggests ancient occupation, compressed over centuries into the minimal syllable that survived.

And at the other end

The longest commune name in France clocks in at 45 characters:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · population 491 · 45 characters

It was formed by the merger of three smaller communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — and it managed to fold all three names into one. Twenty-eight communes in France contain the word "saint" twice. The longest of them won the naming contest by a comfortable margin.

One name in ten starts with Saint

3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint or Sainte — just over one in ten of the 35,014 total.

The pattern goes deep. "Saint-Martin" — just that prefix, before any geographic qualifier — appears in 202 commune names. Saint-Jean follows with 155. Saint-Pierre and Saint-Germain together account for another 252. The saints aren't distributed evenly: Dordogne leads with 145 saint-named communes, followed by Gironde (125) and Seine-Maritime (122). These are old dioceses, old trade routes, old Catholic France writing itself into the map.

You can't address a postcard to Sainte-Colombe

Of France's 35,014 commune names, 32,764 are unique. The remaining 2,250 communes share their name with at least one other. At the top of the sharing list is Sainte-Colombe: twelve communes across twelve different départements carry the name — Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, Yonne. A letter addressed simply to "Sainte-Colombe, France" has no legal destination.

A map made of saints, hyphen by hyphen

38% of all French communes — 13,410 of them — contain a hyphen. The hyphen is the administrative connective tissue of rural France: it fuses the saint to the landscape (Saint-Laurent-sur-Mer), the village to the river (Pont-de-l'Arche), or one hamlet to its neighbour after a forced merger that nobody quite accepted (Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson).

Y has no hyphen. Y has no saint. Y has 89 residents and a very short name. And yet it is in the same register, the same official catalogue, the same postal system as its 45-character cousin in Marne. France maps everything — from the one-letter village to the hyphenated parish that refused to forget its own history.

A note on the data. Commune names, codes, populations and département assignments come from the APL 2023 dataset published by DREES, joined to administrative contours by INSEE/Etalab. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name length and duplicate analysis was performed on the communeName field as found in the GeoJSON — names follow the official INSEE spelling. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 population base embedded in the APL file.
