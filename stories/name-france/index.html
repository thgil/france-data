What's in a name?

One commune is called Y. Another needs 45 characters to introduce itself. France's 35,014 commune names reveal a thousand years of geography, religion, and municipal mergers.

2026-05-17 5 min read

In the Somme département, 89 people live in a commune whose official name is a single letter: Y. Not short for anything. Not an abbreviation. Just Y — the letter, the word, the place. It sits at 49.8°N, roughly equidistant between Amiens and Saint-Quentin, on a plain that has been continuously farmed for at least two thousand years. Whatever it was once called, the record collapsed to a single character somewhere in the Middle Ages, and no one ever bothered to expand it.

Y is the shortest commune name in France. At the other extreme is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, a single commune in the Marne département whose name runs to 45 characters and manages to pack in two saints, a river, and a "et" — the French ampersand of rural consolidation. It has 491 residents. They presumably learn to abbreviate early.

Most are in the north and north-east — Gy in Haute-Saône, Eu in Seine-Maritime (the town that gave its name to a treaty and a château), Ry in Seine-Maritime (likely the model village for Flaubert's Madame Bovary, under the alias "Yonville"). Oô, in the Hautes-Pyrénées, earns a special mention for containing a circumflex on a vowel that barely has room for one.

The shape of France's names

Plot the length of all 35,014 commune names and something unexpected appears. You'd expect a smooth bell curve — a single peak somewhere around 8–10 characters, then tapering off. Instead, the distribution is bimodal. There's a first peak at 7–8 characters (simple village names like Vesoul, Orléans, Libourne), and then, remarkably, a second hump between 15 and 20 characters that refuses to fall away.

That second hump is the fingerprint of centuries of municipal mergers. France has always had a lot of communes — over 40,000 at the Revolution's peak — and various waves of consolidation have fused villages together, preserving both names in a hyphenated compound rather than choosing one. Montigny-Mornay-Villeneuve-sur-Vingeanne. La Vacquerie-et-Saint-Martin-de-Castries. The name grows; the population doesn't always.

The saint-industrial complex

The most striking pattern in French commune naming isn't the extremes — it's the middle. Exactly 3,898 communes (11.1%) begin with Saint or Sainte. That's more than one in nine. Add those that contain a saint reference mid-name — Pont-Saint-Esprit, Bagnols-sur-Cèze — and the Church's grip on French geography becomes even clearer.

Saint-Martin is the undisputed champion with 202 communes carrying his name. Martin of Tours, the Roman soldier who cut his cloak for a beggar on the road to Amiens, became the patron saint of France, and the map never forgot it. He is followed by Saint-Jean (155), Saint-Pierre (144), and Saint-Germain (108). These are the four most common first-names in the French landscape.

The identity problem

Of France's 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have a truly unique name. The other 3,700 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in the country. There are 1,450 names in common use across multiple communes — and at the top of the list sits Sainte-Colombe, which appears in twelve different départements.

This is not a clerical error — it reflects how French communes formed independently within their own local context. Each Sainte-Colombe chose its patron saint without any awareness of the others. They're spread from Normandy to the Pyrenees to the Rhine, each one entirely distinct. The French postal system, and more recently the INSEE commune code system, uses 5-digit codes (like 80829 for Y) to disambiguate what the names can't.

Geography encoded in language

Beyond saints, the most common structural feature in French commune names is geographic positioning — specifically, the use of three prepositions as connectors: -sur- (on the…), -en- (in the…), and -sous- (under the…). These encode the relationship of a settlement to its natural feature: the river it sits on, the valley it occupies, the ridge it shelters under.

Châteauneuf-sur-Loire tells you there's a new castle (châteauneuf) on the Loire. Clermont-en-Argonne places a fortified hill (clermont) in the Argonne forest. Chennevières-sous-Bois puts a settlement growing hemp (chennevières) under the woods. The name is the map, before maps were cheap enough to consult.

France has 35,014 communes. Each one has a name. Most of those names are between 5 and 30 characters, start with a consonant, don't involve a saint, and are quietly unique. But the edges of the distribution — the Y, the 45-character amalgam, the twelve Sainte-Colombes, the 202 Saint-Martins — are where the history shows through.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 French communes including overseas territories and uses official INSEE commune codes. The name data was not independently cross-checked against the Code Officiel Géographique (COG) — minor discrepancies in hyphenation or accents are possible for a small number of entries. Population figures are from the same dataset (base INSEE 2021).
