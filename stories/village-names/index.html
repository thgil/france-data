Y — the one-letter commune

France has 35,014 communes. Their names run from a single letter to a 45-character hyphenated sprawl. One in eight invokes a saint. Twelve of them are called Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-28 4 min read

France is divided into 35,014 communes — the smallest administrative unit, inherited from the revolutionary reorganisation of 1789. Every one of them has a name. Most names cluster around 7–8 characters. A few are absurdly short. A few are absurdly long. The average is 11.8 characters, pulled rightward by centuries of hyphenated merger names.

From one letter to forty-five

At one end: Y. A single letter. Population 89. Located in the Somme département, north of Paris, on a flat plain that has seen more than its share of history. It has a mayor, a church, a school, and a Wikipedia article considerably longer than its name.

At the other end: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson. Forty-five characters. A commune in the Marne, created when three villages merged in 1972. Population 491. The name is the result of democracy — nobody wanted to lose their village's identity — and it shows.

The distribution has a clear peak between 6 and 8 characters — the natural width of a simple French place name — then a long right tail that stretches to 45. That tail is mostly merger names, where post-war administrative consolidations joined two or three villages under a single hyphenated identity. The left tail is much shorter: only 168 communes have names of 3 characters or fewer.

The two-letter club

Below Y, there is nothing. But 13 communes have two-letter names — a small, geographically scattered set that ranges from Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591 — a proper Norman market town) to Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées, pop. 36 — a hamlet at altitude). Two of them, Oô and Sy, contain accented characters that make them even harder to type.

One in eight is a saint

4,437 communes — 12.7% of the total — have Saint or Sainte somewhere in their official name.

The Catholic Church's long hold on French civic life left a permanent imprint on the map. Every major saint has a cluster of communes named in their honour: Saint-Martin, Saint-Pierre, Saint-Michel, Saint-Jean. The prefix Saint- appears as the first word in 3,596 commune names alone. Add Sainte- and you cross 3,890 before even counting compounds like La-Chapelle-Saint-Luc.

The Revolution tried to fix this — briefly renaming communes after rivers, mountains, and republican virtues. The names mostly didn't stick. France is still, cartographically, a very Catholic country.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes

Of France's 35,014 commune names, 32,764 are unique. The remaining 2,250 communes share their name with at least one other — a source of confusion for mapmakers and postal workers across the country. The most duplicated name is Sainte-Colombe, which appears twelve times, scattered from Gironde to Haute-Saône. If you live in Sainte-Colombe, your postal code is load-bearing.

All but two of the top twelve most-duplicated names contain Saint or Sainte. The exceptions are Beaulieu (10 communes) and Le Pin (9 communes) — both common rural descriptors that describe the view or the trees rather than the theology. The pattern holds for the wider list too: saints are the great namers of French villages, and the saints themselves keep repeating.

Somewhere in France, there is a mayor of Y who must write the full commune name — one letter — on official correspondence. There is a mayor of Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson who does the same. Both signatures arrive on the same desks in Paris. The map doesn't sort by dignity.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are drawn from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (used in Story 2), which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories with their INSEE codes, names, and 2021 census populations. Name analysis computed from that dataset. Commune counts reflect the 2023 administrative geography; France's commune count changes slightly each year due to mergers.
