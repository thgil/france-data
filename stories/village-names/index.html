Y is a village

From one letter to 45 — France's 35,014 communes run the full alphabet of odd, saintly, and barely-pronounceable names.

2026-06-04 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y.

Not "Y-something." Not an abbreviation. The full, official, INSEE-registered name of the commune in the Somme (département 80) is a single capital letter: Y. Population 89. INSEE code 80829. It has a mayor. It collects taxes. And if you mail a letter to it, the address line reads, improbably, "Y, 80190, France."

The two-letter club

Beyond Y, France has 13 communes whose official name is exactly two letters. They are scattered from Normandy to the Pyrenées, from the Rhine to the Haute-Saône — mostly small villages (the smallest, Uz, has 36 residents), though Eu in Seine-Maritime reaches 6,591 and qualifies as a proper town. One thing almost all of them share: a consonant–vowel syllable, often ending in a vowel, that reads like the leftover fragment of a longer medieval name.

The saint avalanche

At the other end of the frequency spectrum: saints. Exactly 3,898 French communes — 11.1% of the total — have names that begin with Saint- or Sainte-. That is more saint-named communes than the entire population of many of those communes combined.

The most duplicated name in all of France is Sainte-Colombe, which appears in 12 different communes across 12 different départements. Saint-Sauveur appears 11 times. Saint-Aubin, 10 times. Each is a genuinely different village; each has its own INSEE code, its own mayor, its own church.

Of 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have a distinct name. That means 1,450 names are shared by at least two different places — and navigating a map of France requires a département code almost as often as it requires a commune name.

The 45-letter monster

At the far right of the distribution sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson: 45 characters, the longest commune name in France. It is in the Marne (département 51), has 491 residents, and was born from the merger of several smaller villages. The hyphen-joined colossus is a common consequence of communes nouvelles — a 2010 law that encouraged neighbouring communes to merge administratively while keeping all their old names stitched together.

The runner-up is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in the Haute-Saône, at 43 characters. Third: Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados, 41 characters. The pattern is clear — the longest names in France are almost all the result of mergers, and almost all of them started with "Saint."

The shape of French commune names

The median commune name is 10 characters long. The mean is 11.8 — pulled rightward by the long-tailed distribution of merger names. The peak of the distribution sits squarely at 7 and 8 characters: simple two-syllable names like Bresles, Moreuil, Méricourt. Nearly half of all communes have names between 6 and 10 characters.

The long tail — communes with names above 25 characters — is almost entirely saint-merger territory. The handful of names above 30 characters are all the result of the same administrative logic: small villages that agreed to share a municipal budget but insisted on keeping every syllable of their identity.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which in turn uses the INSEE commune register. The dataset covers 35,014 communes including metropolitan France and overseas territories. Population figures are from INSEE 2021. The single overseas territory with a zero-population entry (Îles Saint-Paul et Nouvelle-Amsterdam, dept 98) is included in name counts but excluded from population-based analysis.
