What's in a commune name?

35,014 communes, 1,450 duplicate names, and one that's just the letter Y.

2026-06-01 4 min read

France has a commune called Y. Not as an abbreviation, not as a placeholder — the official, legally registered name of a commune in the Somme is the single letter Y. Population: 89. APL score: 2.78 (well-served, for what it's worth). Its full address on French government documents: Y, 80170, Somme.

Y is not alone in brevity. There are 13 communes with 2-letter names: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz. Most are ancient place names — pre-Frankish, some Latin, a few Gaulish roots so old the etymology is debated. Eu, in Seine-Maritime, was a royal residence; it has 7,300 residents and a medieval château. Gy, in Haute-Saône, has a cathedral. The short names survive not by accident but by administrative inertia — once an official commune name is set, it is extremely hard to change.

One in nine communes is a saint

France has 3,898 communes whose name begins with Saint- or Sainte- — 11.1% of all communes. Add in variants like St- and hyphenated compounds and that number climbs higher still. The length histogram's second hump — that bump between 15 and 22 characters — is almost entirely Saint-compounds: Saint-Martin-de-Quelque-Chose, Sainte-Marie-de-l'Île, names built from a saint's name, a preposition, and a description of the local geography.

The saints are not evenly distributed. Rural Normandie, Bretagne, and the centre-west have the densest concentrations. Urban créations post-1800 tend toward secular names — Villeurbanne, Vitry-sur-Seine, Gennevilliers — but every corner of France has at least a few.

The Sainte-Colombe problem

Of France's 35,014 commune names, 31,314 are unique. The remaining 3,700 communes share their name with at least one other. There are 1,450 name-groups with duplicates — and the most common name of all belongs to twelve different communes.

Sainte-Colombe appears 12 times, across 12 different départements: Hautes-Alpes, Charente-Maritime, Doubs, Gironde, Ille-et-Vilaine, Landes, Lot, Manche, Rhône, Seine-Maritime, Seine-et-Marne, and Yonne. Their populations range from 59 to 1,953. They are administratively distinct — each has its own INSEE code, mayor, and council — but when postal addresses must be precise, the postal system and the IGN (national mapping agency) silently append the département.

Runners-up: Saint-Sauveur (×11), Saint-Aubin (×10), Beaulieu (×10), Saint-Michel (×9). All 1,450 duplicate-name groups follow the same pattern — a generic religious or descriptive name that independently evolved in multiple localities over centuries, each one locally meaningful, nationally indistinguishable.

The other extreme

If Y is the minimum, what's the maximum? The longest official commune name in France runs to 45 characters:

It's in the Marne (département 51), population 491. The name reflects a 19th-century merger of three small communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — that were joined administratively but kept all three identities in the name. There are 107 communes with names over 30 characters, most of them mergers of the same kind.

A registry, not a language

French commune names are not designed; they accumulated. Some are Roman, some Frankish, some were invented by Revolutionary-era officials determined to replace saints with virtue words (Montagne-sur-Mer, Liberté), most of which quietly reverted. The result is a registry of 35,014 names ranging from one letter to 45, with a saint hiding in every tenth entry and twelve towns that share their name without ever having shared a postcode.

The histogram's bimodal shape is not noise. It's history: a peak at 7–8 characters for the old Gaulish and Frankish place names, a second hump at 15–20 for the medieval saint-compounds that arrived a millennium later, and a long right tail for the administrative mergers of the 19th century. The distribution is an accidental archive.

A note on the data. Commune names and INSEE codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers all 35,014 communes (including overseas territories). The name field is the official libellé commune as recorded in the INSEE administrative files. Lengths are counted on the raw Unicode string; diacritics and hyphens are included. A small number of communes may have slightly different official names in the most current INSEE COG (Code Officiel Géographique) due to mergers or renames since 2023.
