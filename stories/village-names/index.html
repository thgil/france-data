What's in a name?

One letter to 45. There are 35,014 communes in France and every one has a name. Here's what those names say about a country.

2026-06-05 4 min read

In the département of Somme, 150 kilometres north of Paris, there is a village of 89 people. It has a church, a town hall, and the shortest place name in France. The name is Y. Not an abbreviation. Not a placeholder in a database. Just the letter Y, standing alone on road signs and official documents and the village's own letterhead since at least the 10th century.

Y is thought to derive from a Gaulish word for water — the same root as the English "eau." It predates the French language. It predates most of the country's administrative structures. It is, officially, a commune, and it is officially the only one-letter commune in France.

The company Y keeps

Y is not alone at the short end. France has 13 other communes whose names fit in two characters. Most of them come from the same era of linguistic prehistory: Norse, Gaulish, Occitan, or Basque roots that were already worn down to syllables before French toponymy began smoothing everything into Saint- prefixes and hyphenated saints.

Some are tiny. Uz (Hautes-Pyrénées) has 36 residents. Sy (Ardennes): 54. Py (Pyrénées-Orientales): 79. But Eu — a town in Seine-Maritime whose name derives from the River Bresle, which the Norse called something that got compressed to two letters — has a population of 6,591 and a proper high street. The shortness is not a sign of smallness. It's a sign of age.

The shape of French place names

If you plot all 35,014 commune names by character length, you get a distribution that peaks at 7–8 characters — the natural length of a simple French place name like Morlaix or Auxerre — but drags rightward with a long tail of compound names. The average is 11.8 characters, pulled up by the hyphenated monsters at the far end.

The Saint factory

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with "Saint" or "Sainte." Add the communes beginning with La, Le, and Les and you've pre-determined the first word of roughly one in six French place names before a single syllable of actual geography enters the picture. The Church didn't just build cathedrals. It built the address book.

France has more communes named after saints than some countries have communes.

The saint names aren't uniformly distributed. Brittany and Normandy run hot — areas where Celtic and Norman Christianity settled early and named everything within reach. The Mediterranean south leans more toward landscape-derived names: Montrouge, Blanchefort, Fontvieille. The pattern is readable on a map of name-type density, though that would be a story in itself.

The most common name in France

Among all 35,014 communes, one name appears more than any other: Sainte-Colombe. There are twelve of them, spread across twelve different départements — from Charente to Seine-et-Marne, from Yonne to Landes. Each one is a distinct village with its own mayor, its own population, its own postal code. What they share is a 6th-century Gallo-Roman martyr from Sens whose cult spread so widely that a dozen unrelated communities ended up with the same name.

Saint-Sauveur (11 occurrences), Saint-Aubin (10), and Beaulieu (10) follow close behind. "Beaulieu" — meaning "beautiful place" — is the most common non-saint name on the list, which says something about either French landscape or French civic self-esteem. Wherever there was a beautiful place, someone named it that. Ten someones, apparently.

The monsters at the other end

At 45 characters, Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne is the longest commune name in France. It got there the way most of the long names did: merger. In 1972, three villages — Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — were administratively combined into one commune. Rather than choose a single name, they kept all three, hyphenated together into a small bureaucratic monument to French administrative compromise.

    These names aren't rare anomalies — they cluster in regions where the 1971 Marcellin Law drove rapid commune mergers. Some of the resulting names are so long that they've been informally shortened by local usage, road signs, and GPS systems, while the official name remains untouched in the prefecture records.

    Y, population 89, will never have that problem. Its sign fits on a single plank. Its name has been stable for a thousand years, longer than the French state. In a country that can produce Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, there is something clarifying about a village that just decided, once, on a vowel.

    A note on the data. Commune names and metadata come from the APL 2023 dataset published by DREES, which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. Name lengths are character counts of the official commune name string as it appears in that dataset. The same data powers the medical deserts story; here we ignore the health metrics entirely and look only at the names.
