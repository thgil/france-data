What's in a name?

One commune is called Y. Twelve are called Sainte-Colombe. The longest has 45 characters and three saints. France's 35,000 commune names are a compressed history.

2026-06-04 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in the European Union. Each one has a name, and those names have been accumulating since Gaulish tribes first scratched settlements into riverbanks. The result is an address book written across 2,000 years of geography, religion, Latin, Frankish, Occitan, Breton, and Alsatian — occasionally revised by prefectural decree, and very rarely made shorter.

The median commune name is 10 characters long. The average is 11.8. But the distribution is long-tailed, and the outliers are where the story lives.

One letter

There is a commune in France called Y. Population 89. Département 80 — the Somme. It sits between Roye and Péronne, on the edge of the Picardy plateau. It has a mairie, a post office code (80190), and no known etymology satisfying enough to survive. The letter Y is all that remains of a longer name that eroded over centuries of Picard speech.

One in nine communes is a saint

The most striking pattern in French commune names is the wholesale conscription of the Catholic calendar. 3,891 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. France did not just build churches; it named entire villages after the saints who supposedly warranted them.

The trouble is that France ran out of saints before it ran out of communes. So the same name appears again and again: Sainte-Colombe has 12 different communes, scattered across 12 different départements from Yonne to Gironde. Saint-Sauveur has 11. Saint-Aubin and Saint-Pierre each manage at least 9. Send a letter to Sainte-Colombe and you have an 8% chance of getting it right by accident.

La, Le, Les — the article army

2,220 communes (6.3%) begin with a definite article: La, Le, Les, or L'. These are not places that happen to have articles in their names — they are places whose entire identity is built around the article. Le Bourg. La Forêt. Les Essarts. The village isn't near the forest; it is the forest.

The article communes tend to cluster in regions where the definite article was a geographical fact before it became a postal one — the Pays de la Loire, Normandie, Bretagne. La Chapelle- appears as a prefix in at least 60 different commune names across France. France has a lot of chapels, and each one thought it deserved a definite article.

The marathon names

At the other end of the distribution sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson: 45 characters, six hyphens, two saints, one river valley, and one "et" doing a lot of diplomatic work. It is the longest commune name in metropolitan France. It was created by the merger of three separate villages in 1972 and has refused to simplify itself ever since.

This is the common story behind marathon commune names. France has been quietly merging its communes since the loi Marcellin of 1971, which tried to reduce the sheer number of communes by encouraging neighbours to combine. The result was a series of hyphenated compromises — each former village insisting that its name survive, however awkwardly, in the new composite. 9,103 communes (26.0%) now carry at least two hyphens.

The name of a French commune is a geological record. Layer down through the hyphens and you find old battles over which village mattered more, old dedications to saints no one worships, old articles insisting on the definiteness of a forest that may no longer exist. France simplified its governance many times. It has never, quite, simplified its names.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers all 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. The APL file was originally built for the medical deserts story and reused here. Name analysis was done in Python against the communeName field. The count of 35,014 differs slightly from the official INSEE commune count (34,886 in the APL source, ~35,000 in the 2025 administrative boundary file) due to minor vintage differences and commune mergers in 2023–2025.
