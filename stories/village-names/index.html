What's in a commune name?

35,014 communes — one letter long, forty-five letters long, and 3,882 named after a saint.

2026-06-05 4 min read

France has 35,014 communes. Most countries of comparable size have a few hundred cities and call it done. France has 35,014 places, each with its own name, its own mayor, its own mairie, its own entry in the national register. The average commune name is 11.8 characters long. That's the average. Then there are the outliers.

The one-letter commune

France's shortest commune name is a single letter: Y. It sits in the Somme (département 80), has 89 residents, and has been named Y since before anyone thought to explain why. It is a genuine village, with a mairie, a church from the 13th century, and presumably a postman who has seen everything.

Y France's shortest commune name — 1 letter, 89 inhabitants, Somme

Thirteen more communes manage two letters. They read like a strange Scrabble hand: By, Bû, Eu, Gy, Oz, Oô, Py, Ri, Ry, Sy, Ur, Us, Uz. These aren't abbreviations. They're among France's oldest place names — pre-Roman, Germanic, some from Gaulish roots — fossilised in the registry before anyone thought to add a qualifier.

The saint army

If there's one word that runs through French commune names like a thread, it's Saint. 3,882 communes start with Saint- or Sainte- — that's 11.1% of the entire country. Take every commune, feed all the names through a word-frequency counter, and the results aren't subtle.

Most common words in commune names

The word Saint appears 4,090 times. The next most common word — sur, the humble preposition marking a village on a river — clocks in at 2,007. Third is lès, an archaic preposition meaning "near" that survives only in commune names. Everything else is landscape: Bois (217 times), Pierre (180 times), Mer (109 times).

Which saint wins? Saint Martin, decisively. His name appears in some form — Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-d'Hères, Saint-Martin-en-Bresse and 247 more — 250 times across France. Saint Pierre is runner-up at 180 incarnations, Saint Jean at 170, Saint Germain at 120. The Dordogne leads all départements with 130 Saint- communes; the Gironde has 121.

The naming chaos

32,764 unique names cover 35,014 communes. That leaves 2,250 communes sharing their name with at least one other. The post office knows each commune by its five-digit INSEE code — the name is just a human convenience, and a frequently ambiguous one. If you mail a letter to Sainte-Colombe without the code, it could end up in any of twelve villages.

Shortest commune names

Sainte-Colombe wins the duplication championship with 12 separate communes. Saint-Sauveur follows with 11. Beaulieu — beautiful place — turns out to be a description that fits ten different spots across France. The pattern reveals something real: for most of French history, villages were named by the people inside them, with no coordination with the next valley over. The resulting collisions are a map of how local and self-contained rural France once was.

The hyphenation arms race

On the other end of the spectrum: 38.3% of French commune names contain a hyphen. When small villages merge in France — a legal process called a fusion de communes — they often keep both names, joined. When those merged communes merge again, the names stack. Over the centuries, a compound name can grow to spectacular lengths.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson France's longest commune name — 45 characters, Marne (51)

The champion is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson in the Marne: 45 characters, two saints, three formerly independent villages, all now governed as one. It contains its own history in a single string. Second place, in the Haute-Saône, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur at 43 characters — four villages, four identities, one envelope.

Longest commune names

The short names and the long names share an origin: both are accidents of history. Y was always Y. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson grew one merger at a time. In between, the 35,000 other communes got the names they got — saints, rivers, forests, hills, a postman's nightmare and a toponymist's paradise.

A note on the data. All commune names are drawn from the APL GeoJSON used in Story 2 (medical deserts): 35,014 commune polygons for metropolitan France and overseas territories, sourced from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to INSEE administrative boundaries. Name analysis computed directly from the communeName field in that dataset. INSEE commune codes are the five-digit identifiers used to disambiguate same-name communes.
