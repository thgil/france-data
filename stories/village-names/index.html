What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One of them is called Y. Another needs 45 characters just to say hello.

2026-05-10 4 min read

Every French commune has an official name registered with INSEE. Most of them are unexceptional — a saint, a geographical feature, a feudal memory. But the full list of 35,014 names is a linguistic fingerprint of French history: Roman, Frankish, Occitan, Breton, Basque, and administrative accident all jumbled together. Pull the list and the outliers jump immediately.

The commune called Y

The shortest commune name in France is a single letter: Y. It sits in the Somme département (code 80), population 89, APL score 2.78. Its full postal address is simply "Y, 80830". When the commune was assigned an INSEE code, the database field couldn't be empty, so there it sits — one character, no ambiguity, and presumably very convenient to pronounce.

Below Y are 13 two-letter communes. They cluster at the geographic and linguistic edges of France: Breton and Pyrenean roots produce names like Oô (Haute-Garonne), Py and Ur (Pyrénées-Orientales). Norman French gives us Eu (Seine-Maritime, pop. 6,591 — one of the larger two-letter towns). And then there are the pure monosyllables of uncertain origin: Gy, Ry, Sy, By, Ri.

The 2,250 communes with an identity crisis

France has 35,014 communes but only 32,764 unique names. That leaves 2,250 communes — 6.4% — sharing their name with at least one other commune elsewhere in the country. If you tell someone you live in Saint-Sauveur, there are 11 of them to guess from, spread across 11 different départements. Saint-Aubin appears 10 times. Beaulieu appears 10 times.

The record-holder is Sainte-Colombe — 12 separate communes carry that name, from Gironde to Isère to Côtes-d'Armor. Each has its own INSEE code, its own mayor, its own address system. Only the five-digit commune code resolves the ambiguity without fail.

3,882 communes (11.1%) begin with Saint- or Sainte-. The Dordogne leads with 130 such communes; Gironde follows with 121. The saint-name convention reflects the medieval parish system: a commune typically formed around its church, and the church was dedicated to its patron. The most common patron saint across all these names is Colombe (12 communes), followed by Sauveur (11) and Aubin (10).

The saint names are just the most visible element of a broader prefix economy. A further 2,220 communes begin with Le, La, Les, or L' — French articles frozen in place because the original name of the hill, the river, or the clearing got absorbed into the official register. Strip the articles and you still get Villers (105 times as a first word), Villeneuve (70), Val (67), Pont (63), Fontaine (58).

The 45-character tongue-twister

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson Marne (51) · 491 residents · 45 characters

The longest commune name in France has 45 characters and two saints. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson was formed by the merger of three hamlets — Saint-Rémy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — each of which kept its name in the combined toponym. The result is a 9-word postal address that defeats most address-validation systems and presumably gives postmen pause.

The runner-up, at 43 characters, is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur in Haute-Saône. Then 41-character Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière in Calvados. The pattern is always the same: a merger preserved multiple names in sequence, joined by hyphens and the occasional et. The hyphen is France's administrative scar tissue, recording every absorption and amalgamation in the official name forever.

The shape of all 35,014 names

Most commune names cluster between 6 and 10 characters — a saint's name, a preposition, a geographic marker. The distribution is roughly bell-shaped with a long right tail: every hyphenated merger pushes a few communes further right. The median is 10 characters. Y, at 1, is 9 characters below the median. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, at 45, is 35 above it.

38.3% of all commune names contain at least one hyphen. A hyphen means history: a merger, a compound toponym, a saint and a place bundled together. The 61.7% with no hyphen are the survivors — places old enough or isolated enough that no administrative revision ever required them to absorb a neighbour and carry its memory in their name. Y is one of them. One letter, no hyphens, no saints, no compound geography. Just a name that happens to be the shortest possible word.

A note on the data. Commune names come from the DREES APL 2023 commune table, which uses the official INSEE commune registry as its geographic base. 35,014 communes are included (metropolitan France plus overseas territories). The name length analysis counts Unicode characters including accents and the ô in Oô. A small number of records include arrondissement-level entries for Paris, Lyon, and Marseille; these are included in the name-length distribution but excluded from the shortest/longest rankings.
