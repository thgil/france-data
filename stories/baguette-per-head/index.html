The baguette capital

Which commune in Île-de-France has the most bakeries per resident? The answer is not where you think — and the map tells a richer story than the ranking.

2026-05-09 4 min read

There are 6,299 bakeries in Île-de-France, spread across 693 of its 1,300 communes. But which commune packs the most bread per resident? Counting total bakeries rewards the biggest cities. The more honest question is per capita — and the answer flips the map.

The small-commune paradox

Raw per-capita rankings are dominated by the smallest communes. Châtenoy, a village of 149 in Seine-et-Marne, has one bakery — giving it a theoretical density of 67 bakeries per 10,000 residents. That single bakery feeds half a dozen surrounding hamlets and almost certainly closes on Mondays. The number is real; the comparison to Paris is not meaningful.

Restrict the ranking to communes with at least 2,000 residents and the picture sharpens. At that threshold, the winner is Rebais — a market town of 2,151 in Seine-et-Marne — with five bakeries and a score of 23.2 per 10,000. One boulangerie for every 430 people.

Paris, arrondissement by arrondissement

Among the large communes, Paris dominates the top of the ranking — and the inner arrondissements more so than the outer ones. The Paris 1er leads with 20.8 per 10,000: 36 bakeries for 17,308 residents, or one boulangerie for every 481 people. The 4e and 8e follow. The gradient is steep: by the time you reach the outer arrondissements (19e, 20e), the density has halved.

But Paris's real competitor on the ranking isn't another city — it's the small historic market towns of the outer départements: Houdan (Yvelines), Fontainebleau (Seine-et-Marne), Enghien-les-Bains (Val-d'Oise). These are places where the local boulangerie still anchors the town centre the way it has for a century.

The 607 communes with nothing

Nearly half of IDF's communes — 607 of 1,300 — have no recorded bakery at all. Most are small villages where the count is expected. But some are surprisingly large: Saint-Germain-lès-Arpajon (Essonne, 9,338 residents) and Nandy (Seine-et-Marne, 5,814 residents) both appear with a zero. For residents there, the nearest baguette is in another town.

Even among communes that do have a bakery, the range is vast. Jouy-le-Moutier (Val-d'Oise, 16,589 residents) has exactly one — a score of 0.6 per 10,000, 35× less dense than the Paris 1er. The suburb is reachable by RER in under an hour. The bread isn't.

What the map tells you

Zoom into Inner Paris and the map glows: the historic centre is dark amber, the ring of grands boulevards still gold, the outer arrondissements fading toward pale cream. Pan out to the whole of IDF and the pattern reverses: the dense suburbs turn grey as bakery coverage thins. What looks like a Paris story is actually a density-of-density story — the same streets that produce the highest foot traffic produce the highest bakery counts, and foot traffic drops faster than population as you move outward.

The boulangerie isn't just a cultural symbol. It is a piece of commercial infrastructure that depends on pedestrian density, the same way pharmacies and cafés do. The golden core of the map is golden for the same reason in all three cases.

A note on the data. Bakery counts and population figures are from the INSEE Base Permanente des Équipements (BPE), published via Région Île-de-France. The BPE counts (circa 2014 vintage) cover 1,300 communes and Paris arrondissements in Île-de-France. Population figures are from the 2010 census. Commune boundaries are from the same dataset. The bakeriesPer10k field is pre-computed as (bakeries / population) × 10,000. This story uses the same commune boundary file as Story 1 (pharmacy myth).
