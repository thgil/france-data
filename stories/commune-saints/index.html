One in nine French communes is a Saint

France has 35,014 communes — more than any other country in the European Union. 3,882 of them open with "Saint" or "Sainte". The most common single name is shared by twelve separate villages, each going by Sainte-Colombe.

2026-05-28 4 min read

France's commune map is one of Europe's stranger inheritances. The Revolution of 1789 converted every parish into a commune, essentially freezing the medieval settlement pattern into law. The result: 35,014 named places, most of them tiny — the median commune has under 500 people. Each one needed a name, and the source that medieval France reached for most often was the canon of Catholic saints.

The saint army

3,882 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint- (3,587) or Sainte- (295). Add in communes where "Saint" appears elsewhere in the name (Saint-Gilles-du-Gard, Aulnay-sous-Bois aside) and the count rises to 4,443. One in nine villages in France carries a saint's name. By comparison, La (1,054 communes) and Le (758) are the next most popular opening words — geographic articles describing hills, valleys, and rivers.

Saint-heavy départements

The saint-heavy départements cluster in central and western France — Ardèche (29%), Creuse and Loire (28% each), Haute-Vienne (27%). These are the ancient heartlands of Gallo-Roman and medieval Frankish settlement, where the Church had centuries to brand the map. The Atlantic and Mediterranean coasts, colonised later or more seculary, are lighter on saints.

The most common names

Even within the saint canon, the distribution is long-tailed. The single most common exact name is Sainte-Colombe, with twelve communes sharing it, scattered across twelve different départements from Manche in Normandy to Hautes-Alpes in the Alps. Saint-Sauveur follows at eleven, then Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu at ten. The chart below shows the top twenty.

Twelve villages called Sainte-Colombe

Sainte Colombe was a 3rd-century Christian martyr whose cult spread widely in Roman Gaul. By the medieval period her name had been assigned to communities across the country — and because France never merged or renamed most of them, all twelve survive. Combined, they hold about 6,600 people. They have no relationship with each other beyond a shared patron saint.

The shortest name in France

Y A commune in the Somme, population 89. The shortest place name in France — and one of the shortest in the world.

France has exactly one single-letter commune: Y, a village in Somme, with 89 residents. The name derives from a Gaulish root meaning "yew tree" — via centuries of phonetic erosion, the full name compressed to a single letter. Below it, fourteen communes manage two letters, another small collection gets to three.

A medieval time capsule

Each commune name is, in effect, a stratigraphic sample: Gaulish roots under Latin overlays, saints layered onto pagan sites, aristocratic possessives (*-lès-* denoting "near"), Revolutionary renamings that mostly failed, and the occasional blunt toponym (Bel-Air, Beaumont, Villedieu). The French state has been consolidating communes since the 1970s — the number has dropped from over 38,000 in 1968 — but the great majority of names remain. The map still reads, in places, like a 9th-century parish register.

A note on the data. Commune names and codes come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset joined to the Etalab administrative boundaries file (DS-APL, DS-CONTOURS), which covers 35,014 communes including overseas territories. The 2025 merge cycle reduced the total from earlier vintages. Population figures are from INSEE 2021 communal estimates included in the APL dataset.
