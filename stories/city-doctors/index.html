The city that doctors forgot

Among France's large cities, GP access is almost universally good. The exception is an entire ring of Paris suburbs — and the largest is bigger than Nancy.

2026-05-07 5 min read

France has 11 million people living in medical deserts — communes where the official APL score falls below 2.5, meaning residents can access fewer than 2.5 standardised GP consultations per person per year. The maps of those deserts look rural: the diagonale du vide runs from the Meuse to the Pyrenees, picking up the emptied industrial towns of the Massif Central and the thin-soiled uplands of Bretagne. The mental image is a village without a doctor, a drive of an hour to the nearest surgery.

That image captures part of the truth. But if you sort France's cities — not by APL percentage, but by which large towns fall below the threshold — the picture looks different. In Île-de-France, it looks like a crisis.

Provincial cities: almost universally served

Among France's major provincial cities — towns of 50,000 or more outside Île-de-France — there is not a single medical desert. Strasbourg leads the ranking at APL 6.46, followed by Bordeaux (6.35), Lille (6.11), Amiens (6.06), and Angers (5.93). The worst-performing large provincial city is Orléans at 3.08 — twice the national average, and comfortably above the 2.5 threshold.

The pattern holds even for mid-sized cities: Bayonne (6.73), Caen (5.63), Metz (5.42), Rouen (5.40), Nancy (5.17). France's provincial urban system is, by this measure, remarkably well-served. The GP network built up over generations in these cities has not eroded; it has, if anything, deepened.

The IDF exception

In Île-de-France, the story is reversed. 29 towns of 30,000 or more in the outer Paris region fall below the APL 2.5 threshold. Together they hold 3.5 million residents — a quarter of France's entire medical-desert population, in a region covering less than 2% of the country's area.

The largest is Argenteuil, in Val-d'Oise: 107,221 residents, APL 1.74. Argenteuil is bigger than Nancy (104,000), bigger than Caen (108,000 — marginally), and officially a medical desert. Twenty minutes from the Gare Saint-Lazare on the Transilien L, and still below the threshold that France uses to define adequate primary care.

The worst, by score, is Houilles (Yvelines): 33,449 residents, APL 1.23. One in five people in Houilles lives, in national health statistics, as if there were no GP nearby at all.

Why the suburb was never served

The APL model measures access to GPs using a catchment-area calculation: it counts available consultation slots within a 15–20 km radius, weighted by the resident population's age structure. In a provincial city like Strasbourg or Bordeaux, the GP network was laid down over decades, aligned with the street pattern, the commercial logic, the density of neighbourhood life. A GP in Strasbourg's centre-ville draws patients from a walkable catchment. The system worked because the city was built at human scale.

The outer IDF suburbs were built differently. Chelles, Sartrouville, Argenteuil, Corbeil-Essonnes — these are postwar towns, many of them expanding after the 1960s with grands ensembles, pavillonnaires, and motorway junctions. The infrastructure built was commuter infrastructure: rail lines to Paris, zones artisanales, supermarkets on arterial roads. The neighbourhood café and the cabinet médical, which both depend on foot traffic and a patient base built over years, were never part of the plan. The suburb grew. The healthcare infrastructure did not follow.

The APL model also penalises the outer ring for its own growth. Val-d'Oise grew by 30% between 1980 and 2020. The GP count has not kept pace. The denominator — population — has risen faster than the numerator — available consultations. The resulting APL is arithmetically low, but the gap behind it represents a genuine shortage: too few GPs for too many people.

The other deserts: towns that industry left behind

Outside Île-de-France and the overseas territories, there are five medium-sized towns of 20,000+ in metropolitan France that nonetheless fall below APL 2.5. None are commuter suburbs. All are, in different ways, towns that lost their economic base and found that medical infrastructure leaves too.

Vierzon (APL 1.64) is a manufacturing city in the Cher — once a centre of agricultural machinery and porcelain — that has lost a third of its peak population since the 1970s. Le Creusot (APL 2.10) in Saône-et-Loire was the home of the Schneider steel empire; the works closed, the population drained, the GPs retired or left. Fougères (APL 2.08), historically a shoe manufacturing town in Ille-et-Vilaine, tells a similar story. Dreux (APL 2.26) in Eure-et-Loir is notable for being the only town of its size in metropolitan France that is a medical desert without being in IDF or a declining industrial zone — it is simply a mid-sized prefecture that has struggled to attract and retain GPs since several retired and were not replaced.

These five exceptions show that the IDF pattern — suburban sprawl without infrastructure — is not the only path to urban medical underservice. Decline works just as well, in the opposite direction. The population falls, the GP retires, and no one comes to fill the gap because the economic base that justified a practice is gone. The APL score drops. The commune crosses the threshold.

Size is not protection

The received narrative of French medical deserts is rural: sparse population, long distances, elderly patients who cannot travel. That narrative is accurate for the diagonale du vide and for the Guyanese communes with APL = 0. But it misses the urban dimension. The 29 IDF desert towns of 30,000+ hold more medical-desert residents than the entire rural Massif Central combined. Argenteuil alone outranks dozens of rural cantons in desert headcount.

France has built an excellent GP network in its provincial cities. It has not, on the evidence of the APL scores, managed to do the same in the towns it built for commuters.

A note on the data. APL scores (Accessibilité Potentielle Localisée) are from DREES 2023, the most recent vintage as of May 2026. APL measures estimated GP consultation capacity within a distance-weighted catchment, normalised to a standardised resident. The official desert threshold is APL < 2.5; severe deserts fall below 1.0. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 base included in the DREES dataset. Paris, Marseille, and Lyon are represented by arrondissement in the source data and are excluded from the city-level rankings to avoid double-counting. Overseas territories are excluded from the "exception" table but are included in national totals.
