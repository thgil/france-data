Pain and zinc

Île-de-France has 7,378 bakeries and 6,993 bars — nearly equal. But the city of Paris alone has 3,120 bars and just 1,962 bakeries. Its suburbs think differently.

2026-05-10 4 min read

Two things define the French street corner: a boulangerie and a zinc counter. The smell of bread in the morning, the sound of espresso cups at noon. They are, in some sense, the same infrastructure — small, independent, foot-traffic-dependent, neighbourhood-scale. And they're nearly equally common in Île-de-France.

The SIRENE national business registry records 7,378 active bakeries (NAF 1071C) and 6,993 active bars and cafés (NAF 56.30Z) across the eight IDF départements, as of early 2026. A gap of just 385 — barely 5%. If you were designing France from scratch, you might call that balance intentional.

Paris inverts the rule

Zoom into the eight IDF départements and the balance shatters. Seven of them have more bakeries than bars — Yvelines leads at 1.55 bakeries per bar, Hauts-de-Seine at 1.60. But the city of Paris, département 75, runs opposite: 3,120 bars against just 1,962 bakeries. For every boulangerie in Paris, there are 1.6 bars.

Inside Paris: the 11e and its 386 bars

Within Paris, the gap runs widest in the inner districts. The 11e arrondissement — Bastille, Oberkampf, République — has 386 bars and 147 bakeries: one bakery for every 2.6 bars. The 2e (Bourse, Sentier) runs to 4.3 bars per bakery, the most lopsided ratio in the city. The 1er is not far behind at 3.6.

Only three arrondissements flip back to bread-heavy: the 13e, the 15e, and the 16e. These are residential, relatively quiet, with morning commuter bakery runs. The others are all bars.

The bread towns and the bar towns

Move to the suburbs and the pattern reverses sharply. Élancourt — a new town in Yvelines, built from the 1960s — has 8 bakeries and 1 bar. Torcy, another new town in Seine-et-Marne, has 6 and 1. Noisiel, Vaujours, Garches: the same story. These were planned communities, built for young families and commuters, not for evening drinking. Their commercial strips were designed around morning routines: the bakery, the supermarket, the school.

The same streets, different decisions

The zinc — the traditional name for the tin or zinc bar counter of a Parisian café — appears throughout French history as the democratic meeting place, the place where workers ate standing up, where news travelled. The boulangerie is the other pillar: the daily bread queue, the morning ritual.

In dense urban Paris, the zinc won. Bar licences cluster where foot traffic is highest and evenings longest — the nightlife arrondissements, the tourist corridors. In the banlieue, bread won, because the banlieue was built for mornings and families, not for evenings and tourists.

Both are infrastructure. Both are maps of how a place was built and who it was built for. The ratio — 0.63 in Paris, 1.55 in Yvelines — is a number about urbanism.
