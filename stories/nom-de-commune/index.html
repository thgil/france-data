What's in a commune name?

35,014 communes, a 1-letter village, a 45-letter behemoth — and 3,700 places that share their name with at least one other.

2026-06-06 5 min read

Somewhere in the Somme, 89 people live in a commune called Y. That's the full name. One letter. No hyphen, no saint, no article. Just Y.

Y

Commune de Y — Somme (80) — population 89

It is, as far as place names go, about as efficient as it gets. France has 35,014 registered communes. Their names run from one letter to forty-five. The distribution between those extremes is its own small story.

The shape of French place names

Most commune names run between 6 and 9 letters. The distribution is pleasingly lopsided: a sharp rise from the tiny cluster of ultrashort names, a peak around 7–8 characters, then a long slow tail that stretches all the way to 45. That tail is mostly a 2010s artifact — France's loi de réforme des collectivités territoriales encouraged small communes to merge, and the resulting communes nouvelles often hyphenate their old names together rather than pick one.

The 2-letter club

Below Y in the brevity standings sit 13 communes with exactly two letters. They cluster in two language groups: short Basque and Pyrenean names in the south (Oz, Oô, Py, Ur, Uz), and older Norman or Frankish monosyllables in the north (Eu, Gy, Ry). Most are small — a few hundred souls at most. Eu (Seine-Maritime) is the largest, at 6,591, and the only one with real tourist traffic: it has a château and a cathedral.

The saint invasion

3,898 communes — 11.1% of the total — start with Saint or Sainte. That's not counting the many others where a saint's name appears later in a hyphenated string. If you include those, the proportion climbs further. The French administrative map is, in a real sense, a hagiographic document: a census of every local parish patron, every medieval chapel, every bishop whose cult survived the Revolution.

The most common first word in French commune names is "Saint" (3,596 communes), followed by "La" (1,054), "Le" (758), and "Les" (328). The saintly prefix leads the second-place finisher by more than 3-to-1.

Sainte-Colombe wins — twelve times

No name appears more often than Sainte-Colombe. Twelve communes in France go by that name, spread across nine different départements. The runner-up, Saint-Sauveur, appears eleven times. In total, 1,450 distinct names are shared by two or more communes — meaning 3,700 communes (10.6% of all) are not uniquely named. This is why French addresses include the département code: the name alone is not enough.

45 letters and a lot of history

At the other extreme sits the longest commune name in France:

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
Marne (51) · 45 characters · population 491

It is the product of successive mergers: three formerly separate communes — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — combined their names rather than erasing them. The result is logistically awkward for anyone who has to write it on an envelope, but it preserves a record of what the territory used to be. The top-ten longest names follow the same pattern: nearly all are communes nouvelles built from two or three older names joined by hyphens and conjunctions.

Place names are administrative labels, but they accumulate history the way old buildings accumulate layers of paint. Y is a Norse monosyllable that survived because nobody ever bothered to rename it. Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson is a democratic compromise that preserved three identities at the cost of fitting on a sign. Neither approach is wrong. They are both very French.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from DREES's APL 2023 commune-level dataset, which covers all 35,014 French communes including overseas territories. Name length counts are computed on the official commune name strings as provided, which may include accented characters and hyphens. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base used by DREES for APL calculations.
