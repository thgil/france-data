The names on the map

From Y (population: 89) to a 45-letter behemoth, France's 35,014 commune names carry eight centuries of saints, conquests, and mergers.

2026-06-02 4 min read

France has exactly one commune whose name is a single letter. It is called Y. It sits in the Somme, population 89, and it is not a typo. The name comes from Old French — ia, meaning water or marsh — and it predates the standardised French spelling we associate with most place names. It has been Y since at least the 12th century. When the French postal system needed to stamp its envelopes, they presumably had thoughts about this. They did it anyway.

Forty-five characters, seven hyphens

At the other end of the distribution sits Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne. Forty-five characters. Seven hyphens. It was formed in 1972 when three villages merged and chose a name that honoured all three. The result is not a name so much as a peace treaty. Population: 533. The mayor signs a lot of paperwork.

Between these two extremes lie 35,012 other communes. Their names average 11.8 characters, peak around 7, and follow a distribution that looks almost like a bell curve — until you reach the long right tail, where decades of mergers have stacked up syllables into something approaching prose.

One in eleven is a saint

Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,882 — just over 11% — begin with Saint or Sainte. This is not random: it is the sediment of eight centuries of Catholic missionary activity, mapped onto land that monks walked, chapels that survived revolutions, and parishes that became permanent. The saints are everywhere. Saint-Martin alone has at least nine communes. Saint-Pierre has nine more.

The most common saint name of all is Sainte-Colombe. It exists in twelve different places: from Normandie to the Pyrénées to the Rhône Valley. Each is named after the same third-century martyr — a young woman from Roman Spain who fled to Sens and was executed when she refused to renounce her faith. Monks built chapels in her honour along pilgrimage routes. The chapels grew into villages. The villages became communes. Twelve of them, still here, still sharing a name.

Twelve Sainte-Colombes, eleven Saint-Sauveurs

After Sainte-Colombe, the most repeated names tell a story about medieval devotion. Saint-Sauveur ("Holy Saviour") appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu ("beautiful place") each appear ten times. These are the names that spread along trade routes and pilgrimage roads as medieval France urbanised — not because any central authority decreed them, but because the same patterns of faith and landscape kept producing the same answers.

31,314 names found nowhere else

Most commune names are unique. Of the 32,764 distinct name strings in the dataset, 31,314 — over 95% — appear exactly once. The geography is entirely singular: one Quimper, one Strasbourg, one Uz, one Oô. The duplicates are the exception. They are also, frequently, the saints.

France is a country that simultaneously has room for a commune named Y and twelve communes named Sainte-Colombe. The map is stranger than it looks.

A note on the data. Commune names and population figures come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and overseas territories. The name-length analysis uses the communeName field as provided by DREES/INSEE; a small number of communes have names with accents or apostrophes that affect character counts. Commune centroids were estimated as the average of polygon vertices.
