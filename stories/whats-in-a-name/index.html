What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve share the name Sainte-Colombe. And one of them will take you a while to pronounce.

2026-05-19 4 min read

In the Somme, between Amiens and Péronne, there is a commune of 89 people. Its official name, as recognized by the French Republic, printed on maps, filed in tax documents, and addressable by GPS, is a single letter: Y.

Y is not an abbreviation. It's not a glitch in the data. It is simply what the village is called — derived from an old Picard word for water, related distantly to eau. The residents of Y are, by French administrative law, Ypsiloniens.

France has 35,014 of these places. Each has a name. The names are a record of saints, geography, Latin roots, Frankish memory, and — occasionally — admirable economy of expression.

One name, twelve communes

Of 35,014 communes, 32,764 have unique names — but that still leaves 1,450 names that appear more than once. The most repeated name in France is Sainte-Colombe, which belongs to twelve different communes across six départements. If you book a hotel in Sainte-Colombe, clarify which one.

Saint-Sauveur appears eleven times. Saint-Aubin and Beaulieu each appear ten times. The pattern is clear: France's most common place names are its most common saints, combined with its most common landscape descriptions. These are not coincidences — they are the residue of a thousand years of parish naming in a country that had a lot of saints and a lot of beautiful hills.

One in nine communes is a saint

Of the 35,014 communes, 3,882 — roughly 11.1% — begin with Saint- or Sainte-. Add the 152 communes whose name begins with La Chapelle, and the tally of places named after holy things starts to feel less like history and more like policy.

France is a constitutionally secular republic. Its communes, named mostly between the 9th and 18th centuries, have not received that memo.

3,882 communes start with Saint- or Sainte-. That's one in every nine.

The very short

Y is the only one-letter commune in France. But there are thirteen with exactly two letters. They are, collectively, a small philological mystery — monosyllables from Picard, Norman, Gascon, and Catalan, most of them older than the French language itself.

Uz (36 people, Hautes-Pyrénées) is the smallest by population. Eu (6,591 people, Seine-Maritime) is the largest — a Norman port town whose name comes from the Epte river and whose castle once hosted William the Conqueror. The two-letter universe spans from the Pyrenees to the Channel.

The very long

At the other end of the distribution sits the longest commune name in France. It is a record of two villages merged by administrative decree, and it has never been shortened despite having 45 characters, two hyphens connecting saints, and a conjunction in the middle.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson
45 characters · Marne (51) · population: 876

The second-longest is Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 characters, Haute-Saône). Both are the products of 19th-century commune mergers that preserved both original names in full, joined by et. The result is a place that takes longer to write than to drive through.

Names as history

The naming system is not a system — it is an accumulation. Each saint is a local cult that survived the Revolution. Each hyphen is a merger, usually forced, sometimes reversed. Each two-letter relic is a sound that persisted while the language around it changed completely. And Y is just a village that has always been called Y, and sees no particular reason to stop.

35,014 communes. 32,764 names. One is a single letter. Twelve are called the same thing. One takes eight seconds to say aloud. All of them are on the map.

A note on the data. Commune names, codes, and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes in metropolitan France and the overseas territories. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 census base. Commune names are the official INSEE administrative names as of the 2023 vintage.
