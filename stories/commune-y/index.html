Y, and 35,013 others

France has 35,014 communes. One has a single-letter name. Over 3,800 are named after saints. And one takes 45 characters to write out.

2026-06-05 4 min read

There is a commune in northern France called Y. Not Why, not an abbreviation. Just the letter Y. It sits in the Somme, a short drive south of Amiens, with 89 registered residents. France has many strange and wonderful place names, but Y is in a category of its own: the only single-letter commune in the country, and among the shortest official place names anywhere on earth.

Nobody is quite sure where the name comes from. The leading theory is a corruption of an old Picard word for water — the commune sits in a marshy plain — but that etymology is disputed. What is not disputed: when French bureaucracies ask for your commune of birth, there is one correct answer that is a single keystroke.

The two-letter club

Y is not entirely alone. Thirteen more communes make do with two letters. Together, the 14 shortest commune names in France span the country — from the Pyrenean valleys of Oz and Oô to the Norman coast of Eu, which at 6,591 residents is far more than a curiosity: it hosted a royal château where Louis-Philippe entertained Queen Victoria in 1843.

The distribution is not random. Several cluster in the Pyrenees (Py, Ur, Oô, Uz, Oz) — areas where place names descend from pre-Latin Basque and Iberian roots that have been worn smooth over two millennia. Others are old Norman villages whose Viking-era names simply never got longer. Sy in the Ardennes and Ri in the Orne share the same stripped-back quality. Two-letter names are not quirks; they are fossils.

The saint machine

3,898

communes — 11.1% of all of France — begin with Saint or Sainte

One in nine French communes is named after a saint. That is the enduring mark of medieval Catholicism on the administrative map: every parish was dedicated to a patron, and the village took the dedication. When the Revolution tried to erase the saints — renaming Saint-Denis to Franciade, Saint-Cloud to Marat, Saint-Flour to Mont-Cantal — the experiment failed. Most villages simply waited out the Republic and went back to their saints.

The most popular saint is Martin. Over 200 communes in France begin with Saint-Martin — but not a single one is just called Saint-Martin without qualification. Each has appended a geographic suffix to distinguish itself: Saint-Martin-d'Uriage, Saint-Martin-de-Ré, Saint-Martin-du-Var. The saint is shared; the identity is local.

The most shared name

With 35,014 communes and the alphabet as a finite resource, repetition is unavoidable. Of the 32,764 distinct commune names in France, 1,450 are shared by two or more communes — and 3,700 communes find themselves with at least one namesake somewhere else in the country.

The most duplicated name, exactly as written, is Sainte-Colombe. Twelve different communes bear that name, spread across twelve different départements from the Ardèche to the Marne. If you write a letter to Sainte-Colombe with no postal code, it could legally belong to a twelfth of France.

Where names cluster

Most French communes have names between six and nine characters long — the length of Bordeaux (8) or Limoges (7) or Chartres (7). The distribution is roughly bell-shaped around that range, then develops a long right tail: compound hyphenated names built from two villages merged by administrative reform or from a saint name plus a geographic suffix. The tail runs all the way to 45.

The longest name in France

At the far end of the distribution sits a commune in the Marne with a name that was already long before it became the longest in France.

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson 45 characters · Population 491 · Marne (51)

The name is a compound formed from three historically distinct settlements — Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, and Isson — merged into a single administrative unit and given the combined name. This is how France's longest names are made: not through grandeur, but through consolidation. The commune of 491 people carries a name longer than Bundesrepublik Deutschland.

Beneath the administrative layers — the merged villages, the revolutionary renamings, the Pyrenean fossils, the twelve Saintes-Colombes — France's commune names are an accidental archive. They record which saints mattered, which rivers named valleys, which conquerors left syllables behind. Y was here before the maps. The maps caught up.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the APL commune dataset published by DREES, which covers 35,014 French communes including overseas territories as of 2023. Population figures are from INSEE's 2021 census base. Name-length and frequency analysis was computed from the communeName field in that dataset.
