How France named 35,000 communes

Saint-Martin alone covers 202 of them. One commune is called Y. The longest name runs to 45 characters. The naming logic of France's 35,014 communes turns out to be a compressed history of the country.

2026-06-07 5 min read

France has more communes than any other country in the European Union — 35,014 at last count, against Germany's 10,800 and Spain's 8,100. They range from Paris (2.1 million residents) to a hamlet in the Haute-Saône that might muster a dozen. Every one of them has an official name, registered in the INSEE code civil, that it will defend with quiet administrative ferocity. Where did 35,000 names come from?

The saint republic

The single most visible pattern in France's commune names is the saints. Of the 35,014 communes, 4,443 — 12.7% — contain "saint" or "sainte." That's not a rounding error. It's a medieval filing system.

Christianity arrived in Roman Gaul from the second century onward, and the Church organised territory into parishes. Villages took their parish saint's name. The Revolution tried to scrub them — there was briefly a Commune-Affranchie where Lyon used to be — but most reverted after 1795. The saints stuck.

Who is the most popular? Not Mary, not Paul. It's Martin — a 4th-century Roman soldier born in what is now Hungary, who allegedly cut his cloak in half to share with a beggar outside Amiens. He became Bishop of Tours, patron of France, and namesake of 202 communes that start with Saint-Martin.

Jean comes second (155), then Pierre (144), Germain (108). The top 15 reads like a roll call of early medieval Gaul — Julien, Laurent, Hilaire, Georges. These are not just names. They are the organizational skeleton of the Frankish kingdom, still visible in commune signs across the country.

The duplicate problem

When multiple communes share the same root saint, they've had to find ways to distinguish themselves. Since the 19th century, French communes have resolved name collisions by appending geography: a river (-sur-Loire), a mountain (-en-Montagne), a neighboring locality. The result is a naming convention that doubles as a map legend.

1,991 communes contain "-sur-" in their name — positioned on a river, a ruisseau, a body of water real or metaphorical. Another 2,140 begin with Le, La, or Les. And 13,410 — 38% of all communes — are hyphenated, the dash marking the junction between identity and location.

The most duplicated exact name is Sainte-Colombe — 12 communes across France share that name without any distinguishing suffix. How do they cope? The INSEE commune code (a 5-digit number) is the real identifier. The name is just what the sign on the road says.

Most common exact commune names

Y

France's shortest commune name is not an abbreviation. It is not an error. It is a village in the Somme département, population 89, that has been called Y since the Middle Ages. The name appears to derive from the Latin aqua (water), via a long chain of phonetic erosions through Picard dialect, arriving at a single letter sometime before the 12th century. The commune is not embarrassed about it.

Y is not alone at the short end. Sy (Ardennes, 54 residents), By (Doubs, 86), Bû (Eure-et-Loir, 2,022), Oô (Haute-Garonne, 103). Many of these are in areas with strong Celtic or pre-Roman substrate where place names resisted French standardization. The further you get from the Île-de-France heartland, the odder the names get.

The average commune has a 12-letter name

Across all 35,014 communes, the mean name length is 11.8 characters. The distribution is right-skewed: most names cluster between 6 and 14 characters, but a long tail of hyphenated mergers runs out past 30.

45 characters

The longest commune name in France is Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne. It is 45 characters long, contains two saints, references three former villages (Bouzemont, Saint-Genest, Isson), and was assembled through a series of mergers that France's administrative tradition handles by simply concatenating the names of everything that was absorbed. Population: 491.

The second and third longest — Beaujeu-Saint-Vallier-Pierrejux-et-Quitteur (43 chars) and Saint-Martin-de-Bienfaite-la-Cressonnière (41) — follow the same logic. These are not exotic outliers. They are a predictable outcome of a system that prioritized local continuity over administrative elegance. Every hyphen is a political compromise.

A database with a 1,000-year index

Looked at as a dataset, France's 35,014 commune names encode layers of history that no single government ever chose to write down explicitly. The prevalence of saints reflects the Church's role as the organizing institution of medieval France. The -sur- suffix reflects centuries of practical geography. The hyphenated mergers reflect a 20th-century policy of administrative consolidation that tried not to erase anything. Y reflects the raw phonetic erosion of Picard French over 800 years.

The names are not just labels. They are the accretion of decisions made by people who are long dead, for reasons that made perfect sense at the time, preserved in the INSEE commune code table with five-digit numerical precision. Every commune sign on every French road is a small historical argument. Most of them started with a saint.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset (34,886 communes in the primary file), supplemented to reach 35,014 total from the same administrative vintage. Name lengths and saint prefixes are computed from the communeName field. The "saint" count includes all communes where "saint" appears anywhere in the name (case-insensitive), not only as a prefix — a small number may name a river or mountain rather than a person.
