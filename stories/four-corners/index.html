The Four Corners

France has 49,385 bars. We know where every single one of them is. Here's what we found at the very edge.

2026-05-09 4 min read

When you map every bar in France — all 49,385 of them, each placed at its real coordinates — you end up with a picture of a country. The density clusters are obvious: Paris, Lyon, Marseille, the coast. The voids are equally obvious: the Massif Central, the Alps in winter, the stretches of farmland between them.

But the most interesting question isn't where the bars cluster. It's what's at the edge.

Pick mainland France's four geographic extremes — the northernmost point, the southernmost, the farthest west, the farthest east — and in each case, you find a bar. This is not a coincidence. It is, instead, a kind of proof: wherever there are people in France, however few, however remote, there is a bar.

One corner at a time

A bar at the edge — but not always a doctor

The four corners of France have one more thing in common, beyond the bar: three of the four are officially classified as zones sous-denses — medical deserts. Bray-Dunes in the north has an APL score of 1.45 (the desert threshold is 2.5). Lauterbourg on the Rhine has 1.56. Lamanère in the Catalan Pyrenees has 0.00 — there is no accessible GP at all.

The sole exception is Ouessant. The remote island in Finistère, accessible only by ferry, has an APL score of 7.19 — one of the highest in France. The explanation is simple: an island community of 838 people, cut off from any mainland medical infrastructure, has no choice but to maintain its own resident doctor. Isolation, in this case, forces provision. The less isolated suburbs of Paris have no such incentive, and their APL scores show it.

The most remote inhabited point in metropolitan France — an island reachable only by boat, famous for shipwrecks, population 838 — has better healthcare access than most of the Paris suburbs. The most medically isolated: a village of 55 people in the Catalan mountains, at the country's southernmost tip.

The island next door

Just south of Ouessant, Île-de-Sein (département 29) sits at 4.85°W — the second-westernmost bar position in France. It has a bar called CHEZ BRIGITTE. It has a population of 273. And it has an APL score of 27.68, the highest of any commune we found at the extremes of France, and among the highest in the entire country.

Île-de-Sein is famous for a different reason: in June 1940, when de Gaulle broadcast his appeal from London, the entire male population of the island — 128 men — answered the call and sailed to England to join the Free French. De Gaulle reportedly said: "La France, c'est l'Île de Sein." The island has a bar. It had the will to resist. It has 27 doctors per 10,000 standardised residents. It is not representative of France, and it is, in some sense, what France aspires to be.

The bars at France's four corners are not famous. Most are unnamed in our data — registered as active establishments, geocoded, but without a trading name attached. The named ones are local legends of a quiet sort: THE BELGIAN'S at the border, CHEZ BRIGITTE on the wind-battered island. They are where France ends, or begins, depending on which way you're facing.

A note on the data. Bar locations come from the INSEE SIRENE dataset (NAF 56.30Z, active establishments, March 2026), joined to the SIRENE Géolocalisation file for coordinates. 49,385 points. Geographic extremes are computed by sorting all bars with valid mainland-France coordinates by latitude and longitude. APL scores are from DREES APL 2023, the same source as the medical deserts story. Mainland France is defined as départements 01–95 excluding Corsica (2A, 2B).
