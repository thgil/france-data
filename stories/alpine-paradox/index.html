The alpine paradox

France's best-served département for healthcare isn't Paris. It's a mountain department with 141,000 residents, more ski lifts than RER stations, and an APL score that beats the capital.

2026-05-09 5 min read

Every few years, Le Monde runs a story about how hard it is to find a GP in rural France. The story is true. But the data contains a surprise: the département with the highest average score for GP accessibility isn't Paris. It isn't Lyon. It isn't Strasbourg. It's Hautes-Alpes — population 141,000, capital city Gap, main export: ski holidays.

The APL (accessibilité potentielle localisée) is France's official measure of GP accessibility. It estimates how many standardised consultations a resident can reach within a 20–30 minute drive, adjusted for competing demand from neighbours. A score below 2.5 is an official medical desert. A score above 4.0 is considered well-served. Paris scores 4.96. Hautes-Alpes scores 5.02.

Why a ski resort beats the capital

Hautes-Alpes contains Gap (pop. 40,000), a full regional hospital, and a cluster of medical specialists that serves the entire high-alpine zone. For the hundreds of tiny ski-resort communes within the Briançon catchment area — Montgenèvre (pop. 457), Vars (573), Abriès-Ristolas (382) — the APL formula draws a 30-minute travel-time circle and counts all the GPs accessible within it. The numerator is large (Briançon's doctors), the denominator is tiny (permanent residents of a ski village). The result can reach 40.

Hautes-Alpes has a population-weighted average APL of 5.02, meaning that even weighting by commune size, the department comes out ahead of Paris. This isn't a fluke of tiny outliers distorting an average. Gap and its surrounding towns are genuinely well-served, and the alpine communes benefit structurally from catchment-area geography. The department has only 9.4% of residents in a medical desert — one of the lowest desert rates in France.

Briançon effect. Briançon is France's highest city (pop. 12,000) and has a full hospital, a cluster of GPs, and a legacy of mountain-rescue infrastructure. Its catchment area covers dozens of tiny ski communes whose permanent populations are small but whose GP counts are high. The APL formula treats each resident equally — a resident of Montgenèvre gets the full benefit of Briançon's medical density. The same logic applies across the department.

Four Paris suburbs in the bottom ten

Look at the bottom of the table: four of the ten worst-served départements in metropolitan France are IDF suburbs — Val-d'Oise (APL 2.41, 63% of residents in a desert), Seine-et-Marne (2.38, 55%), Essonne (2.52, 51%), and Yvelines (2.58, 39%). A fifth, Eure-et-Loir (2.41, 56%), lies just outside the IDF border to the south-west.

This is not a rural problem. Val-d'Oise is 30 minutes by RER from the Gare du Nord. Essonne contains Evry-Courcouronnes, a planned city of 75,000 residents, and the Saclay technology campus. Seine-et-Marne borders the eastern edge of Paris. These are dense, populous, connected territories — yet almost two-thirds of Val-d'Oise residents live in official medical deserts, the worst share in metropolitan France.

The ring paradox. Paris (APL 4.96) is the second-best served département in France. But move 20 kilometres east, into Seine-et-Marne, and the average APL drops to 2.38 — a medical desert on the other side of the périphérique. The IDF suburbs were built fast, in the 1970s and 1980s, as dormitory towns. Doctors followed population with a 20-year lag — and in many suburbs, they still haven't arrived.

The gap between Hautes-Alpes and Cher

Last place: Cher (APL 2.25), a département of 300,000 people in the geographic centre of France, whose main city is Bourges. 60.5% of Cher's population lives in a medical desert. The gap between Hautes-Alpes (5.02) and Cher (2.25) is 2.77 APL points — meaning that the average resident of Hautes-Alpes has access to roughly twice as many GP consultations per year as the average resident of Cher. Same country, same national health system, same carte vitale. Different universe.

The national average APL is 3.75. About 16.5% of the metropolitan population lives in a medical desert — some 11.1 million people in total. The story of French healthcare inequality is not primarily a story about remote areas and big cities. It is a story about the ring around Paris, and the invisible centre.

A note on the data. APL scores come from DREES's 2023 APL dataset (Accessibilité Potentielle Localisée aux médecins généralistes). The département averages here are population-weighted means — each commune's APL score is multiplied by its population, summed within the département, then divided by total département population. This gives the score experienced by the average resident, not the arithmetic mean across communes. 97 metropolitan departments (including Corse-du-Sud and Haute-Corse) are included. The five overseas départements (Guadeloupe, Martinique, Guyane, La Réunion, Mayotte) are not shown. APL data for this project comes from the same source file used in Story 2 (Medical deserts).
