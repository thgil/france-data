Twelve Sainte-Colombes

France has 35,014 communes. Twelve of them are called Sainte-Colombe. One is called Y.

2026-05-16 5 min read

There is a commune in the Somme called Y. Just Y — one letter, one syllable, population 89. It is, as far as any official record confirms, the shortest place name in France. Possibly one of the shortest in the world. The inhabitants are called Ypsiloniens.

Y is not a fluke. It is part of a tradition. France has 13 other communes with two-letter names. Some read like words: Eu, Oz, Us. Some like abbreviations waiting for the rest: Gy, Ry, Py. One has an accent: Bû. One has a circumflex and a diaeresis: Oô, in the Haute-Garonne, population 103.

The bell curve of French commune names

Zoom out from those 14 outliers and the shape of French commune names is a lopsided bell curve, peaking at 7–8 characters and trailing off in a long right tail. The median is around 12 characters — something like Saint-Loup or Beauregard. Most communes cluster in that comfortable middle. The tail, though, is long.

The saint network

Now for the dominant pattern. Of France's 35,014 communes, 3,898 — just over 11% — start with Saint or Sainte. More than one commune in nine is named after a Catholic saint. This is not a coincidence or an administrative artefact: it is medieval France, still visible on every map, every road sign, every GPS display.

11.1% of French communes start with Saint or Sainte — 3,898 out of 35,014

The church did not name them all at once. The pattern accumulated over eight centuries of parish formation, feudal land grants, and local veneration. Each saint name was once a living choice — a community's declaration of patron, protector, or founding legend. Today they are road signs in every corner of the country, from Saint-Aubin (10 of them) to Saint-Sauveur (11) to Sainte-Colombe, which holds the record at 12.

Name collision

Out of 35,014 communes, only 32,764 have truly unique names. The other 2,250 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in France. If you say "I'm from Saint-Aubin" to a French person, they have ten possible locations to consider. If you say "Beaulieu" — also ten.

The duplication is not random — it reflects the medieval parish grid. When the same saint was venerated in multiple regions, multiple villages took the name. Sainte-Colombe de Lyon, Sainte-Colombe de la Commanderie, and ten other Sainte-Colombes are all distinct communes, all named for the same 3rd-century martyr, and all in different départements.

The marathon at the other end

At the far extreme from Y sits a commune with 45 characters in its name: Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson, in the Marne. It takes eight hyphen-separated parts to name it fully. It was formed by the merger of three villages, and France, rather than choosing a new name, concatenated the old ones.

    This merger logic explains much of the long-name tail. France has 35,014 communes today, down from over 44,000 in the 1960s. Each fusion either chose one name, invented a new one, or joined them with a hyphen. The hyphenation approach is honest but verbose: 38.3% of all French communes now have at least one hyphen in their name.

    A sediment of history

    French commune names are a sediment layer: Gaulish river-words at the bottom (Oz, Ry, Ur — short vowel-sounds from a pre-Roman landscape), Latin and Frankish on top, then eight centuries of Catholic saint names, then Revolutionary renaming, then post-war municipal fusions. Each era left traces. You can read the stratigraphy just by sorting a spreadsheet.

    35,014 communes. 32,764 names. 3,898 saints. 14 names of two characters or fewer. And a commune in the Somme that answers, correctly and officially, to Y.

    A note on the data. Commune names and populations come from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes across metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name analysis was performed on the communeName field in the dataset. Population figures are from the INSEE 2021 base. All name counts are exact string matches — regional spelling variants (e.g. Saint vs Sant in Occitan areas) are counted separately.
