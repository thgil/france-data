France's bar capital isn't Paris

Per resident, the commune with the most bars in France has 202 per 10,000 permanent residents. Twenty-eight times the national average. It's a ski station in the Alps.

2026-05-23 5 min read

Story 4 mapped all 49,385 French bars as amber dots — every débit de boissons, placed at its real address. The map made the patterns visible: a dense north, a bright coastal rim, the familiar diagonale du vide cutting through the middle. But raw counts favour the places with the most people. The natural follow-up is simpler and stranger: per resident, where does France drink most?

The national average is 7.3 bars per 10,000 residents. Paris sits at 14.6 — high for a major European city, nearly double the average. Finistère, home to over 1,000 bars and under a million people, runs at 11.0. These were the headline numbers from Story 4. Now we have the commune-level count, joined to population. The answer is not any of those places.

Les Deux Alpes, Isère. Population: 1,933. Bars: 39. That's 201.8 bars per 10,000 permanent residents — 28 times the national average, and more than 13 times Paris.

The skiing effect

Below are the 15 communes with the most bars per 10,000 permanent residents, excluding communes under 500 residents (where even a single bar produces extreme ratios). The pattern is immediate.

Eleven of the top fifteen are ski resorts. The other four — Rogliano (a Corsican village), Laveissière (a small commune in Cantal), La Roche-Bernard and Île-aux-Moines (Breton tourist towns) — all share the same underlying logic: bars built to serve visitors, counted against a permanent population that's a fraction of peak occupancy.

The denominator is not wrong. It's just measuring something different from what you might expect. When Les Deux Alpes fills with 30,000 winter skiers, its 39 bars are working hard. The 1,933 permanent residents who anchor the population figure are not alone in using them. The ratio is technically accurate and humanly misleading in exactly the same way — which is, arguably, the point.

Strip out the resorts: what's underneath

At département level the tourist distortion smooths out — populations are large enough that no single resort dominates the denominator. What emerges is a different kind of pattern.

Haute-Corse leads at 21.1 bars per 10,000 — nearly three times the national average. Corse-du-Sud follows at 16.9. Corsica is not a tourist anomaly the way Val-d'Isère is: its population is stable year-round, and the village café is genuinely an institution. In many Corsican villages the bar doubles as the mairie annexe, the post-office waiting room, and the committee room for everything that matters.

Lozère (17.6) and Cantal (16.6) are the emptiest departments in metropolitan France — the heart of the diagonale du vide. Their high bar-per-capita scores reflect not abundance but subtraction: rural depopulation has hollowed out the surrounding population while the bars — often the last commercial premises in a village — stay registered. A bar licence costs almost nothing to maintain and is worth something to inherit.

The bar desert

The most striking end of the ranking is not the top. It's the bottom.

Essonne: 3.1 bars per 10,000. Yvelines and Hauts-de-Seine: 3.3. These are the outer suburbs of Paris — the same ring that Story 2 showed as a medical desert. By this measure, the IDF inner suburbs have fewer bars per resident than any other part of France, including the rural interior.

Paris itself sits at 14.6/10k — double the national average, on a par with Hautes-Alpes. Cross the périphérique into the first ring of suburbs and the rate collapses by a factor of four. The suburban communes that grew fastest in the postwar decades were planned for bedrooms and supermarkets, not for the neighbourhood café. Their commercial streets never developed the density of a Haussmann boulevard or a Breton market town. The bars never came, and then the expectation of them went too.

It's the same geography every story in this series has found: a capital that concentrates resources, a ring around it that has less of everything, and a rural interior running its own quiet arithmetic.

A note on the method. Bar counts come from the same SIRENE dataset used in Story 4 — 49,385 geocoded active establishments with NAF code 56.30Z (débits de boissons), March 2026 snapshot. Commune populations come from DREES APL 2023, which uses INSEE 2021 census population as its base. The join is by five-digit INSEE commune code. Communes under 500 residents are excluded from the commune ranking to avoid extreme small-denominator ratios. Départements under 30,000 residents (none of the main 95) are excluded from département rankings; overseas territories are excluded from the bottom rankings. Population figures are permanent residents, not seasonal visitors — which is precisely why ski resorts appear where they do.
