What's in a name?

France has 35,014 communes. One is called Y. Twelve share the name Sainte-Colombe. One stretches to 45 characters. A tour of the records.

2026-06-06 4 min read

The commune of Y sits in the Somme, département 80, with a population of 89. Its postal code is 80700. It has a mayor, a mairie, a church. It files paperwork with the prefect and appears in INSEE's national register of communes like any other. Its name is one letter long.

Y is not an abbreviation. It's not a postal glitch. It comes from the Old French word for the letter, itself derived from Latin aqua — water — via a route that involves a river and the slow erosion of consonants. The commune has had this name since at least the Middle Ages. It is the shortest commune name in France, and it is not particularly close to being the second shortest.

The two-letter club

After Y, France has 13 two-letter communes. They are small, mostly rural, scattered across a wide arc of the country. Some are self-explanatory (Eu, in Seine-Maritime, takes its name from the river Bresle, which was called the Eu in old documents). Others are phonological accidents: Oz in Isère, Ry in Seine-Maritime, Oô in Haute-Garonne. What they have in common is small populations, old names, and a certain stubbornness — no one ever merged them or renamed them into something more legible.

The Saint epidemic

3,898 communes start with Saint or Sainte That's 11.1% of all French communes — more than one in nine.

The density of saint-names is a map of Christian settlement patterns. Dordogne leads with 130 communes named after saints — the southwest was Christianized early and deeply, and the convention of naming settlements for their patron saint took hold and never left. Gironde (121), Isère (106), Saône-et-Loire (105): these are old agricultural territories where the village and its saint were one and the same idea.

The saints themselves vary wildly. There are 3,392 distinct saint-names across the 3,898 communes — meaning most saints are unique to a single place. The 506 overlapping names are a shortlist of the most venerated: Martin, Pierre, Aubin, Michel. Sainte-Colombe wins, with 12 communes sharing the name spread from Normandy to Provence, apparently unable to agree on which département gets to be the real one.

1,450 names, used twice or more

The repetition problem is not limited to saints. Of France's 35,014 communes, 32,764 have names that are unique nationwide. The other 2,250 share their name with at least one other commune somewhere in France. There are 1,450 non-unique names, and they belong to 3,700 communes in total.

This matters practically: French postal addresses, tax records, and administrative systems have evolved complex disambiguation systems — département prefixes, INSEE codes, historical suffixes — to tell apart the twelve Sainte-Colombes, the ten Saint-Aubins, the nine Saint-Michels. The commune's official identity is its five-digit INSEE code, not its name, and the code is what the system actually uses. The name is for people.

The shape of a commune name

The average French commune name is 11.8 characters long. The distribution peaks around 7–8 characters — names like Landres, Chalabre, Saulieu — then has a long right tail pulled by merger names, saint-composites, and historical hyphenations. The tail runs all the way to 45 characters.

The longest name in France

Saint-Remy-en-Bouzemont-Saint-Genest-et-Isson has 45 characters, 491 people, and a name that tells its own history. It's a merger — two communes became one in 1973, and the compromise was to hyphenate both names into a single bureaucratic chain. The resulting commune, in the Marne, now carries a name that overflows address fields and challenges map labels. Its residents, one assumes, shorten it.

The runners-up follow the same pattern: almost all of the longest names are merger artifacts, place-names soldered together because no one could decide which half to keep. The longer the name, the more complicated the politics.

The name is a record

France's commune names are a compressed archive: of saints and rivers, of medieval settlements and 1970s mergers, of Gaulish roots and Latin suffixes and the occasional phonological accident that left a village called Y. The system is not designed — it accumulated, over centuries, one name at a time. The result is 35,014 entries that range from one letter to 45, and collectively tell you something about the country that no administrative document quite captures.

A note on the data. Commune names and populations are from the DREES APL 2023 dataset, which covers 35,014 communes including metropolitan France and overseas territories. Name analysis is based on the communeName field as supplied in that dataset. Populations are INSEE 2021 estimates. A small number of multi-commune entries or arrondissements may appear where the original data has them.
